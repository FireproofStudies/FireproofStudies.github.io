The Love That Cannot Be Severed

The Spirit’s Work in the Heart through the Epistle to the Romans

Read Romans Again

Christians commonly read Romans 8:35–39 as an assurance that nothing can make God stop loving us. That conclusion is certainly true, but it is not the whole of Paul’s point. In fact, this familiar reading may be too simplistic—and more than a little self-centered.

Paul asks, “Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?” He then names tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, peril, and sword. But none of these things could tempt Christ to stop loving His people. Persecution does not place pressure upon Christ’s allegiance to us. It places pressure upon our allegiance to Him. The persecutor’s purpose is not to persuade Christ to abandon the believer, but to frighten the believer into abandoning Christ.

The usual reading turns Paul’s question almost entirely toward God’s disposition: Will He continue to love me when I suffer? Paul’s argument confronts a more demanding question: Will I continue to love Him?

That question does not make Christian perseverance dependent upon the natural strength of the human will. Paul has already explained why the converted heart loves God. In Romans 5:5, he writes that “the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us.” God first demonstrated His love toward us when Christ died for us while we were still sinners. The Holy Spirit then poured answering love for God into the converted heart. Our love for Him does not originate autonomously within us; it is the Spirit-produced response to His prior love in Christ.

This movement does not appear only in two isolated expressions. It runs through the entire Epistle to the Romans. Paul begins with a humanity that knows God but refuses to glorify Him, thank Him, or remain under His government. He anticipates a circumcision of the heart accomplished by the Spirit. He then describes God’s love demonstrated toward His enemies, the love poured into the converted heart, obedience rendered from that heart, the hostility of the flesh replaced by the government of the Spirit, and believers explicitly identified as “them that love God.” He asks whether suffering can overturn that new orientation, and answers that believers conquer through the Christ who first loved them. Finally, he shows that love governing their conduct toward one another, toward their enemies, toward civil authority, toward weaker brethren, and toward the mission of the gospel.

Romans is not merely an explanation of how God can justify sinners. It is also the account of what His gospel makes of them. It traces the heart from resistance to love, from autonomy to submission, and from hostility toward God to a loyalty that no created power can finally sever.

Reading Romans as an Argument

Before tracing that movement, we must decide how an epistle should be read. Romans is not a collection of independent doctrinal statements arranged under later chapter headings. Paul wrote one sustained argument. Words acquire force from the paragraphs in which they appear, paragraphs from the movements they complete, and those movements from the purpose of the whole letter.

This matters especially when Paul writes of “the love of God” or “the love of Christ.” The Greek genitive can describe love originating from God or love directed toward God. Grammar identifies the possibilities; it does not decide between them. The decision must arise from Paul’s argument: What problem has he established? What change is he describing? What does the immediate paragraph require? How does the same idea develop before and after the expression?

The question, therefore, is not which meaning is more familiar. Nor is it which meaning fits most comfortably within a theological system already brought to Romans. The question is which reading allows Paul’s argument to proceed naturally from beginning to end.

The Heart That Will Not Love God

Paul begins Romans by announcing the gospel as “the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth” (Romans 1:16). In it the righteousness of God is revealed. But the need for that saving revelation becomes evident immediately, because the wrath of God is also revealed “against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who hold the truth in unrighteousness” (1:18).

Humanity’s problem is not the complete absence of knowledge. “That which may be known of God is manifest in them” (1:19). His eternal power and Godhead are displayed in creation. They know enough to be responsible, but their knowledge does not become worship. “When they knew God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful” (1:21).

This is not merely intellectual failure. It is a refusal of rightful relationship. To glorify God as God is to acknowledge His supremacy. To thank Him is to acknowledge dependence. Humanity refuses both because the autonomous heart will receive neither His government nor His gifts on His terms.

Paul’s repeated language of exchange reveals the direction of the will. Men exchange the glory of the incorruptible God for images. They exchange the truth of God for a lie. They worship and serve the creature more than the Creator. Their thoughts become vain because the heart governing those thoughts has chosen autonomy. “Their foolish heart was darkened” (1:21).

God’s present judgment corresponds to that choice. Three times Paul says that God gives them up or gives them over. God does not implant rebellion; He judicially removes restraint and allows autonomy to produce its own disordered desires, corrupted thinking, and destructive conduct. The wrath feels like liberty because those who refuse God are handed over to the government they have chosen.

Romans 2 turns from pagan rebellion to religious self-confidence, but the governing problem remains. The man who judges others while practicing the same things despises “the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering,” not recognizing that God’s goodness leads toward repentance (2:4). The obstacle is a “hardness” and “impenitent heart” (2:5). God’s goodness approaches; the heart refuses to turn.

External religion cannot correct this condition. Circumcision in the flesh, possession of the law, and the ability to teach others do not guarantee inward submission. Paul therefore anticipates the necessary change:

“But he is a Jew, which is one inwardly; and circumcision is that of the heart, in the spirit, and not in the letter” (Romans 2:29).

The contrast is not between a religious and an irreligious person, but between outward conformity and inward government. The letter can address conduct from outside. The Spirit must act upon the heart. Before Paul explicitly describes love poured into that heart, he has already established why such an act is necessary. The natural heart does not lack bare information. It lacks a willing orientation toward God.

Loved Before We Loved

Romans 3 and 4 establish the objective foundation of salvation. All have sinned. No flesh will be justified by deeds of the law. God sets forth Christ as a propitiation, demonstrating His righteousness so that He remains “just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus” (3:26). Abraham contributes no meritorious work; he believes God, and righteousness is counted to him.

This order is essential. Paul does not begin with humanity discovering love for God. He begins with God acting for people who possess no claim upon Him. Faith does not purchase His favor. It abandons every competing claim and receives what grace provides.

Romans 5 therefore begins with accomplished justification:

“Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ” (Romans 5:1).

This peace is not first an emotion. It is the objective end of hostility. The rebel has surrendered and been received, not as a prisoner awaiting execution, but as one granted access into grace. Through Christ “we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand” (5:2). The justified person is no longer approaching God across hostile ground. He stands within grace.

Yet life within grace still includes tribulation. Paul does not promise that justification removes pressure. He says pressure now operates inside a new relationship and toward a new end. Tribulation works patience; patience produces tested character; tested character produces hope. Then Paul declares:

“And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us” (Romans 5:5).

The sentence deserves careful attention. Hope will not leave the believer ashamed or exposed as one who trusted a false promise. Why? Because love for God has been poured into the heart through the Holy Spirit who was given to us.

Paul does not describe a natural religious affection slowly cultivated through discipline. The verb ekkechytai—“has been poured out”—is perfect passive: the pouring has occurred, and its result remains. The believer is the recipient. The Holy Spirit is the agent through whom the pouring occurs. The heart is the place in which the new love has been established.

The heart in Scripture is not merely a container for religious feeling. It is the governing center of the person, the oriented will from which thoughts, evidence, desires, and emotions receive their practical weight. To say that love for God has been poured into the heart is to say that the Spirit has established a new governing orientation toward Him.

Paul then explains the origin of that love:

“For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly” (Romans 5:6).

The repeated “for” matters. Human love normally answers perceived worth. One may possibly die for a righteous or good man. God’s love does not wait for worthiness. “While we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” (5:8). When we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of His Son (5:10).

Paul’s deliberate change of wording in verse 8 is especially important. In verse 5 he writes simply hē agapē tou theou (ἡ ἀγάπη τοῦ θεοῦ)—“the love of God”—and says that this love has been poured out in our hearts through the Holy Spirit. In verse 8, however, he does not merely repeat the same expression. He writes tēn heautou agapēn eis hēmas (τὴν ἑαυτοῦ ἀγάπην εἰς ἡμᾶς): God demonstrates “his own love toward us.” The reflexive heautou makes the possessor explicit, and eis hēmas makes the direction explicit. Verse 8 unmistakably identifies God’s own love moving toward us and demonstrated historically in the death of Christ.

That deliberate clarification allows the two verses to perform different but connected work. Verse 5 describes the inward result: the Holy Spirit has poured love for God into our hearts. Verse 8 identifies its objective ground and originating cause: God first demonstrated His own love toward us at the cross. Paul is not explaining an inward impression by merely repeating that God loves us. He is tracing answering love in the converted heart back to the divine love that preceded and produced it.

The movement is not from our love to His. It is from His love to ours. God demonstrates His love toward those who are without strength, ungodly, sinful, and hostile. The Spirit then pours answering love for God into the hearts of those reconciled through Christ.

This reading gives each part of the paragraph its full force. The cross is the objective demonstration of God’s prior love. The Spirit’s work is the inward production of answering love. The changed heart is evidence that hope is not an empty claim. Tribulation may hurt the believer, but it cannot be interpreted as proof that grace has failed, because the Spirit has already begun within him the life toward which justification leads.

Paul’s conclusion in verse 11 confirms the relational movement: “we also joy in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have now received the atonement.” The justified do not merely rejoice that punishment has been avoided. They rejoice in God Himself. Hostility has become love; distance has become access; autonomy has become glad relation.

Love Takes the Form of Submission

Romans 6 explains what the new orientation does. Grace does not free the believer into neutrality. It transfers him from one government to another.

Paul asks, “Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?” and answers, “God forbid” (6:1–2). Union with Christ means that the believer is dead to sin and alive unto God. Paul therefore commands him not to allow sin to reign in his mortal body and not to yield his members as instruments of unrighteousness. Instead, he must yield himself unto God “as those that are alive from the dead” (6:13).

The language is volitional. Sin still appeals. The mortal body still experiences desire and weakness. But sin no longer possesses a legitimate throne. The believer is commanded to present himself according to the life and allegiance he now possesses.

Paul then identifies the inward change explicitly:

“But God be thanked, that ye were the servants of sin, but ye have obeyed from the heart that form of doctrine which was delivered you” (Romans 6:17).

Paul thanks God because the change is not autonomous human achievement. Yet the converted people themselves obey. Divine grace and human response are not competitors. Grace produces a heart capable of responding to truth in surrendered faith, and that heart obeys.

“From the heart” distinguishes this obedience from compelled conformity. The gospel has not merely imposed a new regulation upon an unchanged will. The governing center has changed allegiance. Those who were servants of sin have become servants of righteousness.

Love for God and submission to God are therefore not separate additions to Christian life. Love takes governing form as submission. The heart loves God by welcoming His authority, valuing His will, and presenting the life to His service. Submission without love would be resentful bondage. Love without submission would be religious sentiment. The converted heart possesses an answering love that obeys.

Romans 7 prevents us from confusing this new government with the disappearance of conflict. The person Paul describes can delight in the law of God after the inward man while experiencing another law in his members. He can recognize the good, desire the good, and yet discover powerful contrary impulses.

Whatever conclusions are reached about every question surrounding Romans 7, one point is unmistakable: a Godward inward orientation does not make struggle imaginary. The converted heart may govern the life without every thought, appetite, emotion, or bodily habit immediately becoming coherent with its new principle.

This is why sanctification requires continual presentation. The heart’s new government must train the life. Contrary impulses still press upon the will, but they no longer possess rightful authority. Love for God is real even when obedience is difficult. Indeed, difficulty is the environment in which love becomes visible as loyalty.

From Hostility to “Abba, Father”

Romans 8 brings the movement into full view. The chapter opens with no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus. It then contrasts two governments:

“For they that are after the flesh do mind the things of the flesh; but they that are after the Spirit the things of the Spirit” (Romans 8:5).

The word translated “mind” refers not merely to isolated thoughts but to a governing disposition. Paul states the problem directly: “the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be” (8:7).

This is the condition Romans has described from the beginning. The unconverted person’s central problem is not that he has never encountered evidence of God. He is governed by an orientation hostile to God and therefore will not submit to Him.

The Spirit establishes a different government. “Ye are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit, if so be that the Spirit of God dwell in you” (8:9). The change is not merely improved conduct. It is a new sphere of life and allegiance. The believer is no longer a debtor to the flesh. Through the Spirit he mortifies the deeds of the body. He is led by the Spirit of God.

Paul then describes the relational character of this new submission:

“Ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father” (Romans 8:15).

The Spirit does not replace one terror with another. He does not produce frightened servitude but filial welcome. The heart now approaches God as Father. Submission is not merely concession to superior power; it is the trust of a son who has been received into the household.

Yet sonship includes suffering. Believers are heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ, “if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together” (8:17). Paul does not conceal the cost of allegiance. He places suffering inside the relationship produced by the Spirit.

Creation groans. Believers groan. Even prayer may collapse into inarticulate weakness. But the Spirit who established the heart’s filial orientation does not leave the believer when strength fails. He helps our infirmities and intercedes according to the will of God. The Father searches the hearts and knows the mind of the Spirit (8:26–27).

Then Paul identifies the people under discussion:

“And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose” (Romans 8:28).

This description is not incidental. Paul does not merely say “believers,” though they certainly believe. He calls them “those loving God”—tois agapōsin ton theon (τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν τὸν θεόν). The object is explicit. The people in whom the Spirit dwells, whom He leads, whose weakness He helps, and whose lives God is conforming to Christ are people whose governing relationship to God is now love.

Their love does not cause God’s saving purpose. The order of Romans forbids that conclusion. God loved while they were enemies. Christ died while they were sinners. The Spirit produced the answering love. But the love is genuinely theirs. They love God because He first acted toward them in Christ and because His Spirit has changed their hearts.

God works all things for their good, and Paul immediately defines that good: conformity to the image of His Son. Suffering is not good because pain is inherently virtuous. It is gathered into a divine purpose that forms Christ’s likeness in those who love God. The heart’s allegiance is tested, trained, and displayed under pressure.

The Love That Persecution Cannot Break

Romans 8:31–39 is often treated as though Paul changes subjects. He does not. He draws the conclusion of everything he has said about suffering, the Spirit, the heart, love, and divine purpose.

“If God be for us, who can be against us?” (8:31). Paul does not deny the existence of enemies. He denies their ability to overturn what God has purposed. The God who did not spare His own Son will supply everything necessary to complete His work. No charge can reverse His verdict. Christ died, rose, and intercedes.

Then Paul asks:

“Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?” (Romans 8:35).

The list that follows determines the practical direction of the question: tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, peril, and sword. These are pressures brought against believers because they belong to Christ. They cannot tempt Christ to become unfaithful. They are intended to make Christians renounce their allegiance.

The question is therefore not merely whether Christ will continue to love the sufferer. Paul has already demonstrated His prior love and will state it again. The question is whether any pressure can finally sever the Spirit-produced love by which the converted heart clings to Christ.

Paul quotes Psalm 44:

“For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter” (Romans 8:36).

The words “for thy sake” are decisive. These people suffer because their loyalty remains. The sword is not evidence that the relationship has disappeared. It is the instrument being used to test whether the relationship can be broken.

Paul answers:

“Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us” (Romans 8:37).

Victory does not consist in avoiding tribulation. It consists in remaining Christ’s within it. Believers conquer not by escaping every threat but because no threat succeeds in turning their hearts finally away from Him.

Yet the victory is not credited to superior human resolve. It comes “through him that loved us.” Paul returns to the originating cause. Christ’s prior love produced the answering love; His continuing life and intercession sustain those who suffer for His sake. The believer’s loyalty rests upon Christ’s initiative, not upon an autonomous will attempting to preserve itself.

Paul then expands the challenge beyond visible persecution:

“For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38–39).

Nothing within the created order can break the love-relation established in Christ. Death cannot. Life cannot. Spiritual powers cannot. Present suffering and future uncertainty cannot. The believer’s love is not independent of God’s love; it exists within it, answers it, and is sustained by it.

This is more than the reassurance that God does not become fickle under pressure. It is the declaration that His love has accomplished something in the believer that persecution cannot finally undo. The Spirit has established love for God as the governing orientation of the converted heart. That love may be frightened, grieved, confused, and severely tested. Peter may deny before he is restored. A faithful believer may cry under the apparent contradiction between God’s goodness and present suffering. But the converted heart cannot finally be restored to autonomy as its governing principle. Nothing created can sever the love God has established in Christ.

Love Enlarged beyond the Self

Romans 9–11 might appear to interrupt this movement, but Paul’s own heart demonstrates its result. He begins Romans 9 with “great heaviness and continual sorrow” for Israel. He could wish himself accursed from Christ for his brethren, his kinsmen according to the flesh (9:2–3).

Paul is not claiming that he could actually become Israel’s substitute in that statement. He is exposing the direction of his heart. The love produced by Christ does not remain self-protective. It moves outward toward people who have opposed Paul, rejected his gospel, and often persecuted him.

Romans 10 begins similarly: “Brethren, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved” (10:1). His theological disagreement has not become contempt. The gospel has trained his heart to desire the salvation of those whose zeal remains ignorant of God’s righteousness.

At the same time, Romans 9–11 preserves the priority of divine mercy. God’s purpose does not originate in human willing or running but in God who shows mercy. Gentiles have no ground for boasting over broken Jewish branches. They stand by faith. Israel remains beloved for the fathers’ sakes, and the gifts and calling of God are without repentance.

The section ends not in self-congratulation but worship:

“For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen” (Romans 11:36).

Love for God finds its proper voice in doxology. The heart that once refused to glorify and thank God now stands amazed before His wisdom, judgment, mercy, and faithfulness. Romans 1 described humanity withholding glory. Romans 11 shows the converted heart giving it.

Love Becomes a Living Sacrifice

Romans 12 begins with one of the most important transitions in the epistle:

“I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice” (Romans 12:1).

The “therefore” gathers the argument. God has justified the ungodly, reconciled enemies, given the Spirit, adopted sons, sustained sufferers, and displayed mercy toward Jew and Gentile. Paul does not respond to those mercies by asking for admiration alone. He asks for presentation.

The same person who once yielded his members to sin now presents his whole embodied life to God. This is love taking sacrificial form. The body is not despised or escaped; it is placed at God’s disposal. Such worship is reasonable because it corresponds to what God has done.

The mind must also be renewed. The world continues pressing its patterns upon the believer, but the life is now to be transformed through a renewed judgment of what is good, acceptable, and perfect. The heart’s new orientation must train the thoughts, habits, relationships, and uses of strength that constitute ordinary life.

Paul then writes:

“Let love be without dissimulation. Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good” (Romans 12:9).

Genuine love is not morally empty approval. It hates what destroys and fastens itself to what is good. It honors others, serves the Lord, rejoices in hope, endures tribulation, continues in prayer, gives to need, welcomes strangers, blesses persecutors, shares joy and sorrow, refuses retaliation, and overcomes evil with good.

Here the love poured into the heart in Romans 5 becomes visible. It is not a private feeling used to reassure the believer of his spiritual condition. It is a governing principle producing coherent conduct. It directs the believer toward God, then orders his treatment of everyone who bears God’s image—including enemies.

The connection to Romans 8 is especially important. Tribulation appeared in Romans 5 as the pressure through which hope is tested. It appeared in Romans 8 as a possible instrument of separation. Romans 12 commands the believer to be “patient in tribulation” and to bless those who persecute him. The love that cannot be severed by persecution becomes love extended toward the persecutor.

Love Fulfills the Law

Romans 13 presses the renewed life into public obligations. The Christian submits to lawful authority, renders what is due, and refuses the spirit of rebellion—not because every ruler is righteous, but because the believer is governed by God.

Paul then states the continuing obligation:

“Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law” (Romans 13:8).

Love does not replace the moral law by making conduct irrelevant. It fulfills the law by producing the orientation toward the neighbor at which the commandments aim. Adultery, murder, theft, false witness, and coveting are incompatible with love because each treats the neighbor as an object to exploit, remove, deceive, or dispossess.

“Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law” (13:10). The law can identify and forbid harm. Spirit-produced love establishes a governing disposition that refuses harm because it values the neighbor’s good.

This is not sinless perfection. Believers still require commands, correction, repentance, and renewed presentation. But the direction of the life has changed. Contradictions are no longer authorized expressions of the governing principle; they are failures to be exposed and corrected. Integrity is the coherence of a life ordered by one controlling principle. In Romans, that controlling principle is love produced by the Spirit, directed first toward God, and consequently toward those whom God commands us to love.

Paul then returns to the urgency of the coming day. The believer casts off works of darkness, puts on the armor of light, and puts on the Lord Jesus Christ. Love for God does not remain an inward preference. It refuses to make provision for the flesh because the heart’s welcome of Christ must govern the arrangements of life.

Love Limits Liberty

Romans 14–15 provides one of the most searching tests of this governing love. Believers in Rome disagreed over food, days, and matters of conscience. The strong were tempted to despise the weak; the weak were tempted to judge the strong.

Paul does not resolve the conflict by declaring every conscience equally informed. He states that nothing is unclean of itself, while recognizing that it is unclean to the person who regards it as such. The stronger believer may be correct about the object and still wrong in the use of his liberty.

“But if thy brother be grieved with thy meat, now walkest thou not charitably” (Romans 14:15).

The question is no longer merely, “Am I permitted?” Love asks, “Will my use of this liberty injure the brother for whom Christ died?” Christ’s prior love toward that brother establishes the value by which my freedom must be governed.

The kingdom of God is not meat and drink, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. Love therefore follows the things that make for peace and edification. Strength carries the weak rather than using them as an audience before which to display freedom.

Romans 15 makes Christ the pattern: “For even Christ pleased not himself” (15:3). Believers receive one another as Christ received them. Once again, His action becomes the cause and measure of theirs:

Christ loved us; therefore we love. Christ received us; therefore we receive. Christ did not please Himself; therefore strength is used for another’s good.

Near the end of the section Paul appeals “for the Lord Jesus Christ’s sake, and for the love of the Spirit” (15:30). This love is not ornamental language attached to a practical request. Paul asks the Roman believers to strive together with him in prayer. The Spirit’s love produces shared burden, costly intercession, and participation in gospel ministry.

The People Love Makes

Romans 16 is not an appendix. It is evidence.

Paul closes with names: Phoebe, Priscilla, Aquila, Epaenetus, Mary, Andronicus, Junia, Amplias, Urbane, Stachys, Apelles, and many others. He identifies servants, helpers, laborers, hosts, relatives, prisoners, men and women, Jews and Gentiles. The gospel has not produced isolated individuals privately certain of God’s favor. It has produced a people bound together in Christ.

Priscilla and Aquila laid down their own necks for Paul’s life. Here Romans 8 becomes flesh. People whose love cannot be severed from Christ are free to risk themselves for one another. Others labor in the Lord, open homes, carry messages, support ministry, and receive commendation not for prominence but faithfulness.

Paul also warns against those who cause divisions contrary to the doctrine they have learned. Love is not sentimental tolerance of what destroys the people Christ formed. The same governing love that receives the weak must resist deception. Integrity requires both welcome and discernment.

The epistle ends where it began: with the gospel proclaimed among all nations “for the obedience of faith” and with glory given to God through Jesus Christ. The heart once disobedient has been brought to faith. The faith obeys. The obedience loves. The love forms a people. And the people glorify God.

Read Romans Again

The argument presented here will sound unfamiliar to many readers. That is not because it depends upon an obscure manuscript, an unusual definition of a Greek word, or an elaborate theological construction. It arises from following Paul’s thought through the epistle.

Humanity begins Romans refusing to glorify God, refusing to thank Him, and exchanging His truth for autonomous worship. The resistant heart must be circumcised by the Spirit. God then demonstrates His prior love through Christ’s death, and the Holy Spirit pours answering love for God into the converted heart. That heart obeys from its new governing center. The Spirit replaces hostility with submission and bondage with the cry of a son: “Abba, Father.” Paul consequently identifies believers as “them that love God.” He then asks whether suffering, persecution, deprivation, death, or any other created power can sever that Spirit-produced allegiance. They cannot, because the believer conquers through the Christ who first loved him.

The remainder of Romans shows what this love does. It presents the body to God as a living sacrifice. It abhors evil and cleaves to good. It blesses persecutors, refuses vengeance, fulfills the law, restrains liberty for the sake of a weaker brother, and receives others as Christ received us. The love poured into the heart in Romans 5 becomes the governing principle of Christian conduct in Romans 12–15 and the organizing bond of the people named in Romans 16.

Do not accept this reading merely because it has been argued here. But do not reject it merely because it differs from the interpretation you have always heard.

Read Romans again.

Read it without allowing familiar sermon outlines, chapter headings, systematic categories, or inherited explanations to decide Paul’s meaning before Paul does. Read the epistle from beginning to end. Follow the heart from resistance to obedience. Follow the work of the Spirit from inward circumcision to filial submission. Follow love from the cross, into the converted heart, through suffering, and outward into the believer’s conduct toward others.

Then return to Romans 5:5 and ask what love the Holy Spirit pours into the heart. Return to Romans 8:28 and notice how Paul identifies the converted. Return to Romans 8:35 and ask what persecution is actually attempting to accomplish. Return to verse 37 and ask why our victory comes “through him that loved us.” Return to Romans 12–15 and ask where the love governing this new life came from.

Most importantly, ask whether Paul’s argument flows more naturally when Romans 8 merely assures us that Christ will never stop loving us, or when it declares something larger: because Christ first loved us and the Spirit has poured answering love into our hearts, no created power can finally make the converted heart stop loving Him.

Read Romans with fresh eyes. Do not begin with what you have been taught that Paul must mean. Begin with what Paul wrote, follow where his argument leads, and allow the Word itself to correct the interpretation you brought with you.
