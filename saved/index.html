What Does It Mean to Be Saved?

Rescue, Healing, and Salvation in New Testament Context

The New Testament does not use the word saved to mean one thing. It means a woman's bleeding stopped. It means a ship's company reached shore. It means a people came out of Egypt. It means a sinner passed from death into life in Christ. The meaning has to be settled passage by passage — and that is what this project does.

What This Project Has Found

One Word, Many Rescues

The English word saved has been asked to carry one meaning — conversion — and every passage where it means something else has quietly been mistranslated in the reader's head or passed over. Matthew uses the word three times in two verses for a woman whose bleeding stopped. Luke uses it of 276 people reaching a beach. Jude uses it of a nation leaving Egypt, and then says God destroyed the unbelievers among them.

None of that makes salvation less than it is. The range is not a collection of unrelated meanings. Bodily restoration, historical rescue, conversion, continuing preservation, and final salvation are all forms of deliverance from ruin toward life — and their common centre is not the English word but the God who saves through His Son.

So each study asks the same questions of its own passage. What ruin is in view? Who is delivered, and by whom? What means or response does the text name? What condition results, and when? And does this passage refer to the comprehensive salvation God gives in Christ — yes, no, or indirectly?
