“As Many As Touched Were Made Whole”

A summary of many healings in a few verses, where the saving verb describes bodies restored by the touch of a garment’s hem

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Mark 6:53–56
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 6:53–56

Not every occurrence of the saving verb is a doctrine. Some are a summary of a busy afternoon, and this is one.

And whithersoever he entered, into villages, or cities, or country, they laid the sick in the streets, and besought him that they might touch if it were but the border of his garment: and as many as touched him were made whole.

Mark 6:56

Esōzonto — were made whole, were healed. The imperfect tense, describing something that kept happening: person after person, town after town, restored.

A Summary, Not a Scene

Mark has just told the storm on the lake and the walking on water. He now pulls back from individual episodes to give a wide-angle report of Jesus’ effect on a whole region. There is no named sufferer here, no dialogue, no single miracle to describe. There is a landscape: sick people laid in marketplaces, and a rumour moving ahead of Jesus so that wherever He arrived, the ill were already waiting.

The saving verb, in this setting, means one thing. Bodies that were sick became well. It is the plainest register the word carries, and the passage does not reach past it.

The Border of the Garment

The detail Mark keeps is the hem. They begged only to touch the border of his garment — the tassel at the edge of the outer robe, the furthest and least of Him they could reach.

This is the same border a woman had touched in the crowd, and been made whole, and been told thy faith hath made thee whole. The story had evidently spread, and now whole villages act on it. There is something both true and precarious in it: true, because the healing did flow even through the hem; precarious, because a garment’s edge can shade into a charm, as though the cloth held the power rather than the One wearing it.

Mark does not stop to correct the crowds, and neither should a study of this verse press them too hard. What the passage records is that the power was real and the touch was enough. The theology of where the power resided is worked out elsewhere, in the encounters Mark tells at length.

Why the Plain Sense Matters

It would be possible to feel that a verse like this is beneath the subject — that a gateway about salvation should hurry past the afternoons of ordinary healing to reach the weighty uses of the word.

That would be a mistake, and the corpus is built to resist it. The same verb that means he shall save his people from their sins means, here, the fever left and she got up. Keeping both in view is the point. A Saviour who heals bodies in a marketplace and a Saviour who raises the dead at the last day are not two figures; the New Testament uses one word because it means to hold them together.

What the Word Means Here

Bodily healing, plainly, for many people at once. No layered second meaning is signalled; Mark is not hinting at deeper salvation under the surface of these cures. He is reporting that Jesus healed a great many sick people across a wide area, and that the least contact was sufficient.

Its place in this gateway is exactly that width. Before the word is asked to carry the weight of eternal life, it is worth seeing it do the ordinary work of making sick people well — because the same hand does both, and the same word names both.

At a Glance
