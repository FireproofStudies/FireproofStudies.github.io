What Must I Do to Be Saved?

What the Philippian jailer was really asking, and why Paul’s four-word answer needed a sermon after it

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Acts 16:25–34
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Acts 16:25–34

There is a detail in this story that is easy to miss, and everything turns on it.

By the time the jailer asks his famous question, his emergency is already over. He had drawn his sword because he assumed the prisoners had fled and his life was forfeit. Paul stopped him: “Do thyself no harm: for we are all here.” The prisoners are accounted for. Nobody has escaped. Whatever penalty Rome might have exacted is no longer coming.

And then he calls for a light, springs in, falls down trembling, brings them out, and asks: “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?”

He is not asking how to survive the night. That has been settled. Something larger has opened up, and he has no words for it except the word saved.

Before the Earthquake

The witness in this passage begins long before anything supernatural happens.

Paul and Silas have been beaten without trial—Roman citizens, publicly stripped and flogged—thrown into the inner prison and fastened in stocks. At midnight, wounds still open, doors still shut, they are praying and singing praises to God. And Luke adds, almost in passing, that the prisoners heard them.

They were not singing because they knew an earthquake was on the way. No one had promised them one. They carried their pain toward God in a dark cell with no prospect of release, and other men in chains listened to suffering that had not collapsed into despair.

Then the earthquake. Doors open, bonds loosen, and nobody runs.

The jailer wakes, sees the open doors, and reaches for his sword. What he encounters instead is a voice from inside the prison telling him not to hurt himself. The man he had locked in the stocks uses his first moment of freedom to save the life of his jailer.

So by the time the question is asked, this man has seen three things: prisoners who sing after abuse, a deliverance they refused to exploit, and mercy from someone his own prison had injured. The earthquake displayed God’s power. Paul’s refusal to walk out displayed his Lord’s character.

Sirs, and the Lord

There is a quiet turn in the wording worth noticing.

The jailer addresses them as kurioi—the plural, properly rendered “Sirs.” They answer by directing him to the singular kurios: “the Lord Jesus.”

They do not accept the role he is offering them. They do not suggest that the earthquake proves something about their personal spiritual power, or invite him to attach himself to them. They take his fear, his reverence, and his need, and hand all three to Jesus.

Human messengers are necessary, and this passage insists on their necessity. But they are not saviors, and they know it.

Believe On

“Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved.”

The preposition is doing real work. Not believe about, not believe that—believe upon. Jesus is presented as the One on whom the man’s whole weight is to be placed.

So faith here is more than accepting a proposition. The mind receives the gospel’s testimony about Jesus; the will abandons self-rule and hands the life over; the person rests his hope on Christ instead of on himself. That is trusting surrender, and it is what the word believe is carrying.

It is also a genuine human act. Paul commands the jailer to believe. Paul does not believe on his behalf, and the Spirit does not perform the jailer’s trust for him. Yet faith earns nothing and achieves nothing. It receives the Lord in whom salvation is found. A man who grabs a rope thrown to him has not rescued himself.

Both verbs in the exchange are passive, incidentally—“what must I do so that I may be saved,” and “thou shalt be saved.” The jailer already senses that this is something done to him, not something he can perform. What he does not know is what the required response is, or who the Savior is.

Where Is Repentance?

Paul never says the word. Peter, at Pentecost, never says believe. The two accounts are not offering rival plans of salvation; they are describing the same turn from opposite sides. Repentance is the turning away; faith is the turning toward.

And Luke shows us the repentance rather than naming it. Watch what the jailer does in the next few minutes.

He washes their wounds—the stripes his own institution put there. He is baptized, he and all his, straightway. He brings them into his house. He sets food before them. And he rejoices, believing in God, with his whole household.

None of that purchases anything. It is what a changed allegiance looks like within the hour. The fruit does not create the root, but living trust starts bearing fruit immediately, and here it does so with unusual speed.

Why Preach, If the Answer Was Four Words?

Because “Believe on the Lord Jesus” names the response and its object without telling the man who this Lord is.

So Paul and Silas “spake unto him the word of the Lord, and to all that were in his house.” The short answer was a doorway, not the whole explanation. The command is not a formula whose repetition saves; faith requires a revealed object, and no one can entrust himself to an undefined name.

This is also what separates the earthquake from the gospel. The earthquake woke him, interrupted him, and terrified him. The mercy of the prisoners made him willing to listen. Neither one told him who Jesus was. Providence can bring a person to the edge of the gospel; only the gospel reveals the Christ to whom he must surrender.

Nobody in this story is told to trust the earthquake.

“And Thy House”

The promise is genuinely household-shaped: “thou shalt be saved, and thy house.” Salvation is not presented as a private transaction with no bearing on the people bound to this man.

But notice how Paul carries the promise out. He does not tell the jailer that his decision has settled the matter for everyone under his roof. He and Silas speak the word of the Lord to all that were in his house. The promise to the house is fulfilled by bringing the gospel to the house.

Verse 34 rewards precision. The participle “having believed” is singular and describes the jailer; the rejoicing is household-wide. Luke states the jailer’s faith explicitly and the family’s shared joy explicitly. The universal preaching and the household baptism portray a receptive house, but Luke does not narrate each person’s inward response one by one.

The passage also gives no ages and should not be conscripted to settle later arguments about who may be baptized. What it plainly shows is the gospel entering a household as a social unit without anyone being excused from hearing and receiving Christ.

What Baptism Contributes

It happens “the same hour of the night.” Luke does not present it as a ceremony to be scheduled once things calm down.

And there is a striking sequence of water in this story. The jailer washes the stripes on Paul and Silas’s backs; then he and his household are washed in baptism. He moves from guarding wounded prisoners to caring for brothers, from the cell to his own table, from terror to joy.

Water does not manufacture faith or life. The Word was proclaimed, the man believed, and his new allegiance was already visible in his hands before he ever went down into the water. Baptism answers his faith; it does not replace it. But neither should it be thinned out into a symbol of no consequence. It is the convert’s immediate public act of obedience, marking him out among those who belong to the Lord Jesus.

What Actually Changed

Luke does not describe this conversion by listing benefits. He describes a life turning over in a few hours.

A man who had been ready to fall on his sword is rejoicing in God. A man who held Christ’s servants under guard is serving them. Wounds inflicted under his authority are washed by his hands. Prisoners under his lock are guests at his table. Freedom that he would have used for self-preservation is now spent on other people. A household that had never heard the Word has heard it and answered.

None of this earns anything, and reading it as a checklist misses the point. It shows what kind of faith Paul was commanding. The jailer did not add a religious opinion to an unchanged life. He gave himself to a Lord, and his relationships, his actions, his home, and his joy reorganised themselves around that surrender within the night.

Saved from More Than the Night

Layer the perils and you can see why the word saved was the right word for his question.

There was immediate self-destruction, which Paul prevented before anything else. There was fear before a power his world could not explain. There was sin and a former allegiance that trusting Jesus as Lord would require him to leave. There was death—the crisis had shown him how thin his hold on his own life was. And underneath all of it, there was existing outside of Christ, which is the deepest peril in the passage and the one the earthquake had merely uncovered.

Paul rescued him instrumentally from the sword. Jesus saved him comprehensively when he entrusted himself to Him.

Before the doors opened, suffering had become witness. After they opened, freedom became an opportunity to love. Somewhere between those two, a man asked what he must do to be saved, and was told to stop carrying his own life and give it to the Lord Jesus.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sin, judgment, death, self-destruction, and a life lived outside Christ’s lordship
Saved into what?Life under the Lord Jesus, baptismal identification, transformed relationships, fellowship, and joy
Who saves?God, through the Lord Jesus
Through what means?The word of the Lord proclaimed; believing on Jesus; immediate baptism
Does one man’s faith save his relatives?No. The Word is spoken to everyone in the house
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. The emergency raises the question, but Paul’s answer is conversion
