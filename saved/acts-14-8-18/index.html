“Perceiving That He Had Faith to Be Healed”

A man crippled from birth is made whole at Lystra — and the crowd’s response shows exactly how the saving word can be misread

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Acts 14:8–18
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Acts 14:8–18

This healing closely mirrors the one at the Beautiful Gate, and Luke tells it in nearly parallel terms — but the sequel at Lystra turns it into a lesson about mistaking the healer.

The Man and the Command

And there sat a certain man at Lystra, impotent in his feet, being a cripple from his mother’s womb, who never had walked: the same heard Paul speak: who stedfastly beholding him, and perceiving that he had faith to be healed, said with a loud voice, Stand upright on thy feet. And he leaped and walked.

Acts 14:8–10

Sōthēnai — to be healed, to be saved-made-whole. The peril is congenital: a man who had never walked. The result is immediate and total — he leaped and walked.

Luke gives one detail worth pausing on: Paul, perceiving that he had faith to be healed. The man had been listening to Paul preach, and something in his hearing became faith — not a general belief, but a specific confidence that he could be made whole. Paul saw it before he spoke. The command followed the faith; it did not manufacture it.

The Same Pattern as the Beautiful Gate

Luke clearly means this to echo Peter’s healing of the lame man in Acts 3. Both men are lame from birth. Both are healed by an apostolic word in Jesus’ name. Both leap up. The parallel is deliberate: what Peter did in Jerusalem among Jews, Paul now does in Lystra among Gentiles, and the same power is at work in both.

That earlier healing produced the sermon containing one of the sharpest saving sentences in Acts — neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved. The Lystra healing does not carry that speech, but it stands in the same frame: a bodily saving that opens onto the question of the greater one, and of the name by which it comes.

The Crowd Draws the Wrong Conclusion

Then the scene turns, and this is why Luke tells it.

The crowd, seeing the miracle, concludes that the gods are come down to us in the likeness of men. They name Barnabas Jupiter and Paul Mercury, and the priest of Jupiter brings oxen and garlands to sacrifice to them.

The healing was real; the interpretation was catastrophic. The people took a genuine act of God’s power and attached it to the wrong source entirely — to the apostles themselves, as though the men held the power. It is the exact error the garment-hem crowds flirted with, now made explicit and idolatrous.

Paul and Barnabas react with horror — they rent their clothes, and ran in among the people, crying out — and redirect everything: “We also are men of like passions with you, and preach unto you that ye should turn from these vanities unto the living God.” The healers refuse the worship and point past themselves to the God who made heaven and earth.

Why This Belongs in the Gateway

The occurrence itself is a bodily healing, one register of the saving word. But Luke has framed it to teach something about all the healings: that the power is God’s, and that a miracle can be witnessed accurately and still be credited to the wrong source.

The Lystra crowd saw clearly and concluded wrongly. That is a standing danger wherever the saving power is displayed — to marvel at the instrument and miss the God who wields it. Paul’s frantic correction is the passage’s own guard against it, and it belongs in a study of the word because it protects every other occurrence from the same mistake.

What the Word Means Here

Bodily healing of a lifelong disability, through an apostolic command met by faith to be healed. That is the direct sense, and it stands beside the Beautiful Gate as its Gentile counterpart.

But Luke will not let the healing be admired in the wrong direction. The men through whom the power flowed tore their clothes to stop being worshipped, and pointed the crowd to the living God. The saving act was real; the Saviour was not standing in sandals accepting oxen. That correction is the passage’s gift to the whole gateway.
