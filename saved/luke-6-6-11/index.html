“To Save Life, or to Destroy It?”

Luke’s account of the withered hand sharpens Mark’s question by one word — and the physician notices which hand it was

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Luke 6:6–11
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Luke 6:6–11

Luke tells the same Sabbath confrontation Mark tells, and the differences are small and deliberate. He is writing as a careful investigator, and he adds three things Mark leaves out.

“It Was the Right Hand”

Luke alone specifies which hand. For most people, the right hand is the working hand — the one a carpenter, farmer, or scribe depends on. Whatever this man had been, his ability to earn had gone with it.

That is the kind of detail a physician notices, and Luke has been identified with the medical profession since the earliest tradition. It is not decoration. It tells you what the man had lost.

They Were Watching for It

“And the scribes and Pharisees watched him, whether he would heal on the sabbath day; that they might find an accusation against him.”

Luke’s verb suggests sustained observation — they were watching closely, on the lookout. They had come with a purpose, and the man with the withered hand was the instrument.

Then Luke adds something else Mark does not: “But he knew their thoughts.”

So the whole scene is transparent to Jesus before it begins. What follows is not a man being caught out by a trap. It is a man walking into one deliberately, with the mechanism fully visible to Him.

“Rise Up, and Stand Forth in the Midst”

He will not do it quietly. The man is called out into the open, and the whole room is made to watch.

That is worth pausing on. Jesus could have healed him after the service, privately, and avoided the confrontation entirely. He chooses instead to make the question unavoidable — for the man, who must stand up in front of everyone; for the watchers, who must decide what they are seeing; and for the congregation, who will have to think about it afterwards.

The Question, Slightly Sharper

I will ask you one thing; Is it lawful on the sabbath days to do good, or to do evil? to save life, or to destroy it?

Luke 6:9

Mark’s version ends to save life, or to kill. Luke has to save life, or to destroy it — a broader word, covering ruin as well as killing.

The saving verb is the ordinary one, sōsai, and it means here what it means in every sickroom: to preserve and restore a life.

The structure of the question is the point. Jesus offers two options and no third. He does not permit the reasonable-sounding middle ground — heal him tomorrow — because He is making a claim about what withholding good actually is. In front of a person you could restore, there is no neutral position.

Their Answer

Mark says they held their peace. Luke says something slightly different and, if anything, worse: “And he looked round about upon them all, and said unto the man, Stretch forth thy hand.”

Luke does not record any answer at all. Jesus looks at every one of them, gets nothing, and proceeds.

Then: “And he did so: and his hand was restored whole as the other.”

The command is impossible to obey until it is obeyed. Luke’s phrasing — whole as the other — quietly measures the result against the working hand. Nothing partial.

“Filled with Madness”

Luke’s closing line is his own:

And they were filled with madness; and communed one with another what they might do to Jesus.

Luke 6:11

The word is anoia — literally without mind, senselessness, a fury that has displaced reason. Mark says they went out and took counsel to destroy Him; Luke says first that they lost their reason, and then that they began to confer.

That is a precise piece of observation. They had asked a question about lawfulness on the Sabbath. They received an answer they could not argue with — a man’s hand working in front of them — and the result was not reconsideration but rage.

Jesus had asked whether the day was for saving life or destroying it. Within a few verses, one party in that synagogue has restored a hand and the other is discussing how to destroy Him. The question answered itself.

What the Word Means Here

Bodily preservation and restoration. The peril is a withered hand in a body that has to work; the result is a hand that functions.

Nothing here says the man was converted, and Luke does not follow him out of the building. This is D1 in the project’s classification — healing and bodily restoration — and it should not be inflated.

Why It Matters Anyway

Because of what Jesus attaches to it.

He does not defend the healing on the grounds that the case was urgent. A withered hand is not an emergency; it could have waited a day, and the watchers knew it. He defends it on the grounds that doing good is what the day is for, and that the alternative to doing good is not doing nothing.

That is a claim with a long reach. A great deal of religious harm gets done in the space Jesus refuses to allow — the space where a person with the power to help declines on principle and calls it faithfulness.

Luke, who will later record a Samaritan crossing a road and two religious professionals passing by on the other side, seems to have been interested in exactly this question. The priest and the Levite were not cruel men. They simply had reasons.

Jesus called that choice by its name, and then healed the hand.

At a Glance

Saved from what?A withered hand — and, behind the question, a religion that would rather watch than heal
Saved into what?A hand restored whole as the other
Who saves?Jesus, who knew their thoughts before He acted
Through what means?A command to stretch out the hand that could not be stretched
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?No. Bodily restoration, inside a question about what the law was for
