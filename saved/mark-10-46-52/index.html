“Thy Faith Hath Made Thee Whole” — Blind Bartimaeus

A blind beggar’s healing, named and dated by Mark — and the man who received his sight followed Jesus in the way

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Mark 10:46–52
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 10:46–52

Mark gives this blind man a name — Bartimaeus — which is rare in his healing accounts, and it signals that Mark wants the man remembered. The saving word means his sight restored, and the scene closes on something more than sight.

The Cry

And when he heard that it was Jesus of Nazareth, he began to cry out, and say, Jesus, thou son of David, have mercy on me.

Mark 10:47

Bartimaeus is a blind beggar by the Jericho road. Hearing that Jesus is passing, he cries out — and the crowd tries to silence him. He only cries louder: Thou son of David, have mercy on me. The title matters. Son of David is a messianic name; the blind beggar sees, in a sense the sighted crowd does not, who is passing by. His persistence against the crowd’s rebuke is faith refusing to be quieted.

Jesus stops and calls him. And Mark records a small, vivid detail: Bartimaeus casting away his garment, rose, and came to Jesus. The cloak was a beggar’s one possession, his tool of trade spread out for coins. He throws it off to come. There is something already whole-hearted in the man before he is healed.

The Healing

And Jesus said unto him, Go thy way; thy faith hath made thee whole. And immediately he received his sight.

Mark 10:52

Hē pistis sou sesōken se — thy faith hath made thee whole, thy faith hath saved thee. The immediate meaning is unmistakable and it is bodily: immediately he received his sight. The peril was blindness; the result is sight. The saving word here means the healing of a blind man, and the study should not obscure that plain sense under a weight of theology it will not bear. This is, first and most clearly, a miracle of restored vision.

The faith Jesus names is the faith that cried out, refused to be silenced, and cast off the cloak to come. It was not a theological achievement; it was a desperate, persistent reaching for mercy from the Son of David. And Jesus credits it: thy faith hath made thee whole.

The Ending That Opens Outward

But Mark does not end the sentence where the healing ends. He adds a clause:

And immediately he received his sight, and followed Jesus in the way.

Mark 10:52

Followed Jesus in the way. Jesus had told him go thy way — and the man’s way turned out to be Jesus’ way. He does not return to the roadside and his cloak; he falls in behind Jesus on the road, which in Mark’s Gospel is the road up to Jerusalem and the cross. The healed beggar becomes a follower.

This is where a careful study lets the passage resonate without over-reading it. The saving verb, on its own, means the healing of blindness — that is its high-confidence sense. But Mark’s closing clause allows a wider echo: the man who was made whole followed Jesus in the way, and in the way is discipleship language in this Gospel. Mark places the healing at the very end of the journey section, just before Jerusalem, as if to say: here is what seeing rightly looks like — following Jesus toward the cross.

The resonance is real, but it must not be forced. Mark does not turn sōzō into a technical term for conversion; he lets the healing be a healing, and then, by the last clause, lets it point past itself. Bartimaeus received his sight, and used the first thing he saw to follow Christ.

What the Word Means Here

The plain sense: bodily healing of blindness, immediate and complete, credited to a persistent faith that cried out to the Son of David for mercy. That is the high-confidence meaning, and it stands on its own.

Its contribution to the gateway is the way the scene opens outward at its close. The verb means the healing of a blind man; the narrative, by adding followed Jesus in the way, lets that healing resonate as a picture of something larger — sight given, and spent on discipleship. Mark does not press the point into doctrine, and neither should the reader. But the man who was made whole did not go back to his cloak. He followed, on the road to the cross.
