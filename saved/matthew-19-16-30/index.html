“Who Then Can Be Saved?” — Matthew’s Account

Matthew alone records the young man’s age and the one commandment Jesus added, and both sharpen the same impossibility

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • Matthew 19:16–30
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Matthew 19:16–30

Three evangelists tell this encounter; this study takes Matthew’s telling, and Matthew keeps two things the others do not.

Matthew’s Two Additions

First, the man is young. Matthew alone calls him the young man — Mark and Luke give his wealth and Luke his rank, but Matthew notes his age, which removes one easy escape. This is not a man at the end of life reckoning with mortality. He is young, capable, and already asking the largest possible question.

Second, when the man says he has kept the commandments and asks what he still lacks, Matthew records Jesus adding one: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. The command to sell all and give to the poor is then set as the test of exactly that commandment. Matthew makes explicit what the others leave implicit — the young man’s wealth was the measure of a love of neighbour he had not, in fact, kept.

“What Good Thing Shall I Do?”

The question is framed as a transaction from the start: “Good Master, what good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?”

Jesus queries the framing before answering it. “Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is, God.” The reply is not a denial of His own goodness; it is a probe. The young man is shopping for a good deed, and Jesus draws attention to the word good to ask whether he knows what he is using it to mean.

Then the commandments, and the confident answer: “All these things have I kept from my youth up: what lack I yet?”

Jesus does not contradict him. He locates the lack: “If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come and follow me.”

The Grief

“But when the young man heard that saying, he went away sorrowful: for he had great possessions.”

Matthew and Mark agree that he left; Luke leaves him standing. In all three the reason is the same, and it is stated flatly: he had a great deal, and could not let it go. The command exposed the one thing he was unwilling to place under Christ, which is precisely what made it the right command for him and no one else.

The Camel and the Needle

“It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God.”

The largest animal in common experience, the smallest opening in common experience. The familiar softening — that the needle’s eye was a low gate — has no support before the medieval period and ruins the sentence. Jesus is not describing something difficult but achievable with effort. He is describing something that does not happen by human capacity at all.

The Question and the Answer

“When his disciples heard it, they were exceedingly amazed, saying, Who then can be saved?”

Their astonishment is the interpretive key. In their world wealth read as blessing; if the blessed cannot enter, no one can. And the equivalence across the passage is complete: the young man asked about eternal life, Jesus spoke of entering the kingdom of heaven, the disciples ask who can be saved. Three phrases, one reality.

The answer settles the whole matter: “With men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible.”

Not difficult with men, easier with God. Impossible with men. The possibility is relocated entirely to God’s side. Jesus does not tell them how to shrink the camel. He tells them the thing cannot be done from where they are standing, and can be done from where God is.

Peter’s Question and the Hundredfold

Peter, having watched a rich man decline, presses the contrast: “Behold, we have forsaken all, and followed thee; what shall we have therefore?”

Jesus does not rebuke the question. He answers it generously — thrones in the regeneration, and for everyone who has left houses or family or lands for His name’s sake, an hundredfold, and shall inherit everlasting life. What the young man could not surrender, the disciples had surrendered, and the promise attached to their loss is out of all proportion to it.

Then the sentence that turns the reckoning on its head: “But many that are first shall be last; and the last shall be first.”

The young man was first by every visible measure — young, rich, moral, earnest. The disciples were last. The kingdom does not run on that ledger.

What This Passage Establishes

Saved here carries its full weight: entrance into the kingdom, inheritance of eternal life. No narrower reading is available; the three terms interpret one another.

And the answer — with God all things are possible — is the sentence this gateway keeps arriving at from every direction. The healings show a Saviour who restores bodies; the rescues, one who intervenes in danger; this encounter shows why a Saviour is needed at all. The young man was not turned away for wickedness. He was undone by the ordinary assumption that something this valuable must be purchasable by someone this able.
