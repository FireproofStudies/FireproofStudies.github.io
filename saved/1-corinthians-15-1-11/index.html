Saved in Context

“By Which Also Ye Are Saved”

Paul attaches a condition to salvation — if ye keep in memory — and the condition tests whether the faith had content, not whether real faith can be lost

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 10 min read • 1 Corinthians 15:1–11
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › 1 Corinthians 15:1–11

Paul is about to spend a whole chapter defending the resurrection of the dead, because some at Corinth were denying it. He opens by reminding them what the gospel is, and the saving verb comes with a condition attached that has troubled readers ever since.

The Gospel Restated

Moreover, brethren, I declare unto you the gospel which I preached unto you, which also ye have received, and wherein ye stand; by which also ye are saved, if ye keep in memory what I preached unto you, unless ye have believed in vain.

1 Corinthians 15:1–2

Sōzesthe — ye are saved, present tense. Paul stacks up four verbs describing the Corinthians’ relation to the gospel: they received it, they stand in it, they are being saved by it, and — the condition — if they keep in memory what he preached. And then the sharp final clause: unless ye have believed in vain.

Everything a study of this passage must decide is packed into that last line and a half. But the decision is easier once the content of the gospel is in view, so Paul supplies it first.

What the Gospel Is

For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; and that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures.

1 Corinthians 15:3–4

This is the gospel reduced to its irreducible core: Christ died for our sins, was buried, rose the third day — all according to the scriptures. Paul then lists the witnesses to the resurrection: Cephas, the twelve, above five hundred at once (most still living, and therefore askable), James, all the apostles, and last of all Paul himself, as of one born out of due time.

The point of the witness list is the point of the whole chapter. The resurrection is not an optional appendix to the gospel; it is of first importance, delivered first of all, attested by named and living people. A gospel without it is not a reduced gospel — it is a different thing altogether, and Paul is about to say so.

The Condition — and What It Tests

Now back to the condition, with the content in hand.

The Corinthians are saved by this gospel if they keep in memory what Paul preached — unless they believed in vain. Read in isolation, this can sound as though salvation hangs by a thread of memory, one lapse away from forfeit. Read in context, it says something quite different.

The context is verse 12: how say some among you that there is no resurrection of the dead? Some Corinthians were holding the name of Christ while denying the resurrection — keeping the label and discarding the article the label stands on. Paul’s condition addresses exactly them. To hold fast the gospel is to keep holding the resurrection that is its heart. To let the resurrection go is not to lose a salvation once possessed; it is to show that what was held was never the gospel at all, but an emptied version of it.

That is what believed in vain means. The Greek — eikē — is to no purpose, emptily, without content. Paul is not describing a real faith that later failed. He is describing a belief that was hollow from the start, because it was assent to a gospel with its centre removed. A faith in a Christ who did not rise is faith in nothing, and Paul will say it flatly a few verses on: if Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain … ye are yet in your sins.

So the condition does not teach that regenerate life can be reversed. It asks whether the professed faith has real content — whether it holds the risen Christ, without whom the gospel is empty. This is the same distinction the parable of the sower draws: a response can look like belief, be called belief, and still not be the rooted thing, because it does not hold. Here the test is doctrinal rather than temporal, but it is the same test. Saving faith holds fast the gospel it received. A faith that jettisons the resurrection reveals that it never grasped the gospel to begin with.

Why It Has to Be This Way

Paul’s logic in the rest of the chapter makes the reading unavoidable. He argues that if there is no resurrection, then Christ is not risen; if Christ is not risen, the preaching and the faith are empty, the apostles are false witnesses, the Corinthians are still in their sins, and the dead in Christ have perished. He is not warning believers that they might slip out of salvation. He is showing that a Christianity without the resurrection saves no one, because it has nothing at its centre to save by.

That is why holding fast matters. It is not that perseverance earns or maintains the salvation; it is that real faith, by its nature, keeps holding the risen Christ, and a faith that lets Him go proves to have been empty. The holding is the evidence, not the payment.

Grace, Not the Grip

Paul guards the point from becoming a new anxiety by where he locates the credit. Describing his own labour, he immediately corrects himself:

But by the grace of God I am what I am: and his grace which was bestowed upon me was not in vain; but I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me.

1 Corinthians 15:10

Even Paul’s holding fast — his abundant labour — he refuses to credit to himself. Not I, but the grace of God. The same grace that saved him is the grace that kept him laboring. So the condition of verse 2 does not throw the Corinthians back on their own strength to grip the gospel tightly enough. It calls them to keep holding what grace gave them to hold, and the holding itself is grace at work.

That is the note the whole gateway keeps returning to, and it belongs here too. The condition is real; the grip is real; and the grip is not the believer’s unaided achievement but the effect of the grace that saved him in the first place.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense — salvation through the risen Christ — held present-tense and inseparable from the resurrection. The peril is an empty gospel and a faith with no content; the means is the apostolic gospel of Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection, received, stood in, and held fast.

Its contribution to this gateway is the reading of the condition. If ye keep in memory is not salvation hanging by a thread of effort, and believed in vain is not real faith undone. Both describe the difference between a faith that holds the risen Christ and a hollow assent that lets Him go. Saving faith keeps its grip on the resurrection because that is what saving faith is — and even the grip, Paul insists, is grace.
