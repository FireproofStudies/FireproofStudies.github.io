“Such As Should Be Saved” — What Luke Actually Wrote

The KJV’s most disputed rendering in Acts, and a Greek participle that says something simpler and better

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • Acts 2:42–47
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Acts 2:42–47

Acts 2:47 contains one of the King James Version’s most argued-over phrases:

And the Lord added to the church daily such as should be saved.

Such as should be saved. Generations of readers have taken that to mean those who were destined to be saved, and the verse has been pressed into service in debates about election on both sides.

The Greek says something simpler, and — for the purposes of this project — more useful.

The Participle

The word is sōzomenous: a present passive participle, accusative plural. Literally, those being saved.

Present tense: ongoing, in progress. Passive: it is being done to them, not by them. Plural and accusative: they are the object of the adding.

So the sentence reads: the Lord was adding to the church daily those who were being saved.

That is not a statement about a future decree. It is a description of what was happening in Jerusalem week by week — people were being saved, and as they were, the Lord was adding them.

The KJV’s should be reflects an older English usage where should could carry a simple future or descriptive sense rather than obligation or predestination. It is not a mistranslation in seventeenth-century English. It has simply drifted, and modern readers hear a claim in it that the Greek does not make.

What Luke Is Actually Reporting

Read the whole paragraph and the emphasis becomes obvious. This is not a doctrinal aside about who gets saved. It is Luke’s summary of what the first church was like, and the saving language appears at the end as the engine underneath it.

They continued stedfastly in four things: the apostles’ doctrine, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers. Fear came upon every soul. Wonders and signs were done. They had all things common, sold their possessions as any had need, continued daily in the temple and from house to house, ate with gladness and singleness of heart, praised God, and had favour with all the people.

And the Lord added.

Two Things Held Together

The passage does something the modern imagination tends to pull apart. It puts God’s saving work and the church’s ordinary life in the same paragraph without embarrassment.

The Lord is the one adding. Luke is careful about that — it is not the apostles growing an organisation, and no strategy is described. The verb is passive on the human side.

And yet what the added are added to is entirely concrete: a community with teaching, meals, shared money, daily meeting, and prayer. Salvation in Acts does not deposit people into a private relationship with God. It puts them in a room with other people, most of whom they did not choose.

That connection is not decoration. Peter had just told the crowd to be saved from a crooked generation, and being saved from a people necessarily meant being added to one. Luke says so in the previous paragraph — three thousand souls were added on the day of Pentecost — and repeats the verb here.

Daily

The word does a lot of work. The Lord added to the church daily.

Pentecost was not a one-off harvest followed by consolidation. Luke is describing a rate. Whatever was happening in Jerusalem in those weeks, it was happening continuously, and the community was absorbing new people constantly.

Which is worth pausing on, because everything else in the paragraph — the shared possessions, the daily meeting, the singleness of heart — was being maintained while the membership changed every day. That is not a settled congregation. It is a community under constant expansion, holding together.

What “Being Saved” Means Here

The peril is the one Peter named in the sermon: a generation under judgment, a false verdict about Jesus, sins requiring remission. The result is what these verses describe: forgiven people, given the Spirit, incorporated into a body, learning, eating, praying, and praising.

So this is the comprehensive sense of the word, viewed from an unusual angle. Most passages describe salvation from the standpoint of the person being saved. This one describes it from the standpoint of the community watching it happen — new people, every day, being handed to them by the Lord.

What the Verse Does Not Settle

It does not resolve the debate it is usually recruited into. A present participle describing people currently being saved neither establishes nor refutes a doctrine of election; it simply is not addressing the question. Anyone building a case on should be in this verse is building on an English idiom rather than on Luke.

What it does establish is worth more for this project. Salvation in Acts is something happening to people, continuously, by the Lord’s action — and it lands them in a church.

The four things they continued in are worth naming again, because they are the plainest description in the New Testament of what a saved people does with itself: doctrine from the apostles, fellowship with each other, bread broken together, and prayer.

Nothing there is remarkable. That is rather the point. The Lord was doing something extraordinary daily, and what it produced was people eating together with glad and simple hearts.

At a Glance

Saved from what?The condemned generation Peter had just told them to escape
Saved into what?Addition to Christ’s gathered people, and a shared life of teaching, fellowship, bread, and prayer
Who saves?The Lord, who added to the church daily
What does the Greek say?A present passive participle — “those being saved”
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes, described from the side of the community that receives them
