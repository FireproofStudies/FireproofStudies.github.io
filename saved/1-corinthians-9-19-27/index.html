“That I Might By All Means Save Some”

Paul makes himself servant to everyone, adapting endlessly, for one fixed purpose — and then warns that the preacher himself must not be disqualified

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • 1 Corinthians 9:19–27
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › 1 Corinthians 9:19–27

Paul is defending his refusal of certain rights as an apostle, and he explains the principle behind it: he has made his whole ministry serve one end, and that end is stated with the saving verb.

Free, and Servant to All

For though I be free from all men, yet have I made myself servant unto all, that I might gain the more.

1 Corinthians 9:19

Then the famous sequence: to the Jews he became as a Jew; to those under the law, as under the law; to those without the law, as without law; to the weak, weak. And the refrain that closes it:

I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.

1 Corinthians 9:22

Hina pantōs tinas sōsō — that I might by all means save some. This is instrumental salvation stated as a life’s organizing purpose. Paul is not the saviour; God saves through the gospel. But Paul bends everything he can bend — his rights, his liberty, his comfort, his manner — to be a means by which some are saved.

Adaptation, Not Compromise

The passage is easily misread as saying Paul had no fixed convictions and simply blended in wherever he went. It says the opposite. What Paul surrenders is his rights and preferences, not the gospel. He guards that carefully: even when he is without law to the lawless, he is not without law to God, but under the law to Christ. The content never moves. What moves is Paul.

The self-denial is real and costly. He gives up eating what he likes, insisting on support he is owed, standing on customs he is entitled to — all so that no removable obstacle stands between a hearer and the gospel. He removes himself as a barrier. The adaptation is a form of love, not a lack of backbone.

And the aim keeps the whole thing from drifting: by all means save some. Every accommodation is measured against whether it serves someone’s salvation. That is what makes it discipline rather than people-pleasing.

“For the Gospel’s Sake”

Paul states the motive plainly: “And this I do for the gospel’s sake, that I might be partaker thereof with you.” He does not stand above the gospel dispensing it; he wants a share in it alongside those he reaches. The servant of all is also a fellow-recipient, hoping to partake of the very salvation he carries to others.

That keeps the instrumental note humble. Paul’s ministry is a real means of others’ salvation, and Paul himself is a man who needs the gospel he preaches.

The Race and the Warning

Then the tone shifts, and Paul turns the instrument back on himself.

Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain.

1 Corinthians 9:24

He speaks of temperance, of a fixed goal — “I therefore so run, not as uncertainly; so fight I, not as one that beateth the air” — and of disciplining his own body. And the reason he gives is startling:

Lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.

1 Corinthians 9:27

Adokimos — disqualified, rejected as failing the test. Paul, who has just described making himself all things to all men to save some, now says he keeps his own body in check lest the preacher himself be found disqualified.

A study must handle this soberly and without overreaching. Paul is not teaching that salvation is earned by athletic effort, nor undermining everything the corpus has said about salvation resting on God. He is saying that the man who preaches salvation to others is not thereby exempt from the call to persevere — that a herald can proclaim the terms and himself fail to run by them. The warning is real, and it is aimed first at Paul, which is why he can hand it on without cruelty. The one who is most an instrument of others’ salvation refuses to presume upon his own.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense — salvation from unbelief into a share in the gospel — with the accent, as in Romans 11, on ministry as a genuine instrument. Paul organizes his liberty, his rights, and his whole manner of life around being a means by which some are saved.

Its contribution to the gateway is the pairing of two things people often separate. Verse 22 makes ministry an instrument of others’ salvation; verse 27 makes the minister himself a runner who could be disqualified. Paul holds both. He spends himself utterly to save some, and he disciplines himself lest the one who preached to others should himself be a castaway — instrument and pilgrim in the same man.

At a Glance
