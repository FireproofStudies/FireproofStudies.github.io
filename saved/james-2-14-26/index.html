What Does “Can Faith Save Him?” Mean?

The kind of faith James says cannot save anyone—and why that is not a contradiction of Paul

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 11 min read • James 2:14–26
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › James 2:14–26

“Can faith save him?”

Coming from an apostle, that question is startling enough to have caused real trouble in the history of the church. Luther famously struggled with this letter. Generations of readers have felt the pull between James here and Paul in Romans, and have quietly decided one of them must be handled carefully.

The Greek settles more than it may appear to. James does not ask whether faith can save. He writes hē pistis—the faith—and the article points back to the thing he has just described: the faith of a man who says he has it and has no works. And he frames the question with a negative particle that expects the answer no.

So the question is not “is faith able to save?” It is “that faith—the sort just described—that cannot save, can it?”

Everything else in the passage follows from getting that right.

The Argument Started in Chapter One

James has already laid the groundwork: “Receive with meekness the engrafted word, which is able to save your souls. But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.”

The Word is able to save. That is not in question. What James doubts is whether hearing that never touches how a person lives counts as receiving it. His word for such a person is self-deceived.

Chapter two applies that to mercy. People in these congregations were professing faith in “our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory” while seating the rich well and humiliating the poor. James reminds them that judgment will be merciless to the one who has shown no mercy. And then he asks his question.

So the contrast running through this passage is not faith against moral effort. It is claimed, inactive faith against living faith.

A Hungry Person Cannot Eat Kind Words

James’s first illustration is deliberately mundane, and it is devastating.

A brother or sister is without clothing and without daily food. One of you says, “Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled”—and gives them nothing.

Notice that the words are not false. Warmth and food are exactly what that person needs; the speaker has correctly identified the situation and expressed a genuinely good wish. The words are empty because the speaker withholds the only thing that would make them mean anything.

That, James says, is the structure of the faith he is describing. True religious words, sincerely meant perhaps, held apart from the self that would embody them.

The illustration also keeps “works” from floating off into abstraction. James is not building a theology of merit. He is defending actual hungry people from believers who honour doctrine with their mouths and withhold mercy with their cupboards.

One clarification matters here, because this passage has been used to wound people. James is not saying that the poor, the ill, the disabled, or the constrained have defective faith because they cannot produce impressive works. His target is someone able to meet a plainly encountered need who substitutes speech for available mercy. The measure is not productivity. It is whether the life answers God’s claim within the capacity actually given.

What the Demons Believe

Then James turns to doctrine, and the sentence lands hard: “Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble.”

He concedes the point first—thou doest well. The confession is correct. Monotheism is not a small thing; it is the foundational confession of Israel’s faith.

And demons hold it, along with something most professing believers do not have: an emotional response proportionate to the truth. They tremble. Whatever else can be said of demonic belief, it is not complacent.

What it lacks is surrender. They recognise God’s identity while remaining in rebellion against His rule.

Which means neither correct doctrine nor intense feeling is what makes faith saving. A person can understand the gospel, agree with it, feel fear, and undergo profound conviction while still declining to hand himself over to Christ. Saving faith includes understanding and assent, and then reaches the will. Demons believe that something is true. Abraham and Rahab ordered their lives around the God whose word they believed.

Dead Faith

“As the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.”

The analogy is precise, and it is easy to misread in a way that reverses it. James is not saying that enough activity will animate a corpse. Works are not playing the role of life-giver. He is saying that the total absence of living expression is how you identify a corpse.

So works do not generate faith. Their complete absence exposes that the profession has no living operation in it.

Nor is James narrating a living faith that later died. From its first appearance in verse 14, this is something a man says he has while having no works. James calls that isolated claim dead in itself. Scripture elsewhere allows for real temporary reception, genuine enlightenment, belief that never becomes rooted—experiences that are real at their own level without being regeneration. The faith James dissects has never become embodied allegiance at all.

Show Me

“Shew me thy faith without thy works, and I will shew thee my faith by my works.”

The first half is an impossible demand, and James knows it. An invisible inner claim cannot be produced for inspection. The second half is the only available alternative: faith is displayed in what it does.

But he refuses the opposite separation just as firmly. The works are not free-standing good behaviour. They are intelligible only as the expression of trust in what God has said.

When he gets to Abraham he makes the relationship explicit: faith “wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect”—brought to its intended completion. Not repaired. Not supplemented. Carried into the action the situation called for.

Justified by Works?

James’s Abraham argument depends entirely on chronology, and it is easy to miss.

Genesis 15: Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness. Genesis 22, years and years later: the offering of Isaac. And James says the later act fulfilled the earlier Scripture.

That order rules out the obvious misreading. Abraham’s obedience on the mountain cannot be the meritorious cause of a standing God had already granted decades before. The command about Isaac placed that long-held trust under its most severe test, and Abraham’s action brought his faith to completed expression.

James’s vocabulary throughout is demonstrative—show me, I will show thee, seest thou, ye see. Justification in this passage carries the sense of vindication and manifestation. It should not be reduced to a performance for human spectators, though: God Himself is the one testing Abraham, and Abraham’s action genuinely aligns his life with the God he trusts.

Held together, it coheres. God brings the believing sinner into right standing. Faith receives that standing rather than earning it. The declaration is true because the relationship is real. Works embody that alignment under God’s Word. And obedient faith is vindicated as living rather than merely claimed.

Abraham’s work did not create a second righteousness. It fulfilled in life the faith God had already counted.

Why Rahab?

The second example is chosen with obvious care, because she is Abraham’s opposite in every social respect: a Gentile, a prostitute, a resident of a condemned city, with no covenant history and no accumulated record of piety.

Before she acts, she confesses what she has heard—that the LORD dried up the Red Sea, that He gave Israel victory, that “he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath.” Then she acts on it, at considerable personal risk, by hiding the messengers and sending them out another way.

Her works are plainly not moral credentials banked before grace; her history could not supply any. They are the point at which her allegiance visibly changed sides.

Abraham and Rahab together make James’s case unmistakable. Living faith is not restricted to one kind of person, one social history, or one kind of work. What matters is that God’s revealed claim becomes the ruling reality of a life.

Do James and Paul Contradict Each Other?

No—but the resolution is not to soften either of them. They use overlapping vocabulary against opposite errors, and each says what he means.

Paul confronts the person who treats works, law-keeping, ancestry, or merit as the ground of righteous standing. His question is: how is a sinner brought into right standing with God? His answer is grace, received through faith, apart from works, with boasting excluded.

James confronts the person who treats verbal or intellectual profession as saving faith while his life remains untouched. His question is: is the faith you are claiming alive, and can it save you? His answer is that faith apart from embodied action is dead.

Even their handling of Abraham divides along those lines. Paul goes to Genesis 15 and excludes works as the ground of standing. James joins Genesis 15 to Genesis 22 and includes works as faith’s completed expression.

Paul’s saving faith was never dead assent—he tells the Ephesians that God’s workmanship is created for good works. James’s works are never independent merit—he quotes Genesis 15 himself. Both men refuse to make human achievement the source of salvation, and both refuse to separate God’s saving work from the life it produces.

Does He Deny Justification by Faith Alone?

He denies justification “by faith only” when faith means the isolated claim he has been examining since verse 14. We should not protect a slogan by blunting his sentence. A faith consisting of words, correct ideas, and inward assertion does not justify and cannot save.

And in the same breath he quotes the foundational declaration that Abraham believed God and it was counted for righteousness. He does not add works as a second object of trust or an additional instalment. Faith receives; works express the receiving.

So the real distinction is not between faith and faith-plus-works, as though two instruments were competing. It is between dead faith alone—a profession separated from life—and living faith, which is trusting surrender and therefore necessarily becomes visible.

Faith alone receives God’s gift, because neither works nor faith earns it. But the faith that receives Christ is never an abstraction sealed off from the person doing the believing. When the self is entrusted to Christ, the life comes under His rule. That is not an addition to faith. It is what the word means.

What This Should Do to Your Assurance

James intends examination, not perpetual self-surveillance, and the difference matters enormously for how this passage is used.

He is confronting someone comfortable with a profession while refusing plainly available mercy. He is not telling a frightened believer to count works until he feels entitled to trust a promise.

Living faith can be weak, immature, inconsistent, and badly in need of correction. Abraham’s record includes some notable failures. Rahab’s occupation is named in the text without softening. Their justification does not rest on flawless performance. What their works revealed was the direction of allegiance when God’s claim became concrete.

So assurance looks first to the Christ the gospel reveals, not to confidence in a moral record. It can then recognise the marks of His life in real, imperfect mercy, obedience, repentance, and return. The question James presses is not have I produced enough? It is has my professed trust become the allegiance by which I actually entrust myself to Him?

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sin’s completed death, self-deception, judgment without mercy, and exclusion from life with God
Saved into what?Living fellowship with God, righteous standing, mercy at judgment, and final life
Who saves?God. Neither faith nor works functions as an independent saving power
Through what response?Trusting surrender that receives God’s Word and takes embodied form in mercy and obedience
What faith cannot save?A claimed faith—true words held apart from the life they describe
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. James is testing whether the professed response is living faith at all
