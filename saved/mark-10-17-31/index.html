Saved in Context

“Who Then Can Be Saved?”

The disciples ask the question after watching a good man walk away — and the answer moves salvation out of human reach entirely

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Mark 10:17–31
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 10:17–31

Three phrases in this passage mean the same thing, and Mark uses them as equivalents without pausing to explain.

The man asks how to inherit eternal life. Jesus talks about how hard it is to enter into the kingdom of God. The disciples, astonished, ask who then can be saved?

Eternal life, the kingdom, being saved. Same reality, three angles. That equivalence is one of the most useful things this passage establishes, and it is why saved here carries its full weight rather than the narrower senses it has in a storm or a sickroom.

He Did Everything Right

The man comes running and kneels — an unusual amount of eagerness and deference for someone of his standing. His question is serious: “Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?”

Jesus first queries the adjective. “Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is, God.” That is not a denial of His own goodness; it is a question put back to a man who is handing out the word casually. Do you know what you just said?

Then the commandments, and the man’s answer: “Master, all these have I observed from my youth.”

And here is the detail that makes the passage hard: Jesus does not contradict him. He does not say no you haven’t. Mark says instead: “Then Jesus beholding him loved him.”

That look is the hinge of the story. Whatever comes next is not said with contempt.

The One Thing

“One thing thou lackest: go thy way, sell whatsoever thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come, take up the cross, and follow me.”

This is not a general rule for everyone who wants eternal life. Jesus does not say this to Peter, or to Zacchaeus, or to Joseph of Arimathea. It is aimed at what this particular man is holding.

And it lands: “he was sad at that saying, and went away grieved: for he had great possessions.”

Note the direction of ownership in that last clause. Mark could have written that he owned a great deal. He wrote something closer to: the possessions had him.

Why the Disciples Are Astonished

Jesus looks round and says how hardly they that have riches shall enter into the kingdom of God — and Mark records that the disciples were astonished at his words.

We miss why. In their world, wealth was widely read as evidence of God’s favour. If anyone was well positioned for the kingdom, surely it was the prosperous, devout, morally serious man who had just knelt in the road.

So when Jesus repeats it — “Children, how hard is it for them that trust in riches to enter into the kingdom of God!” — and adds the camel and the needle’s eye, the disciples are not merely surprised. Their whole framework has been knocked over.

Hence the question, which they ask “astonished out of measure”: “Who then can be saved?”

It is not a request for statistics. It means: if not him, then nobody.

The Answer

“With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible.”

That is the theological centre of the passage, and it is worth refusing to soften it.

Jesus does not answer their question by lowering the requirement. He does not say the camel is a rope, or the needle’s eye a small gate in the Jerusalem wall that a kneeling camel could squeeze through — a charming explanation with no evidence behind it before the middle ages, and one which ruins the sentence. He says impossible, and then locates the possibility somewhere other than in human capacity.

So the answer to who can be saved? is: nobody, by himself. And: anyone, by God.

That is the shape of the gospel in one exchange. Salvation is not the reward of the well-positioned. It is a divine possibility given to people for whom it is otherwise out of reach — the rich man, the poor man, the moral man, and the disciples who had already left everything.

What About the Leaving?

Peter, characteristically, says the thing everyone is thinking: “Lo, we have left all, and have followed thee.”

Jesus does not rebuke him for it. He promises a hundredfold — houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, lands — in this present time, with persecutions, and in the world to come eternal life.

That promise is worth reading carefully. It is not a guarantee of prosperity; the persecutions are inside the list, not after it. What it describes is the new family that following Christ produces: a person who loses a household gains a hundred, because the people of God become his household.

And then the line that keeps the whole thing from becoming a new ranking system: “But many that are first shall be last; and the last first.”

Peter had just implied a scoreboard. Jesus dismantles it in a sentence.

What This Passage Does Not Say

It does not teach that poverty saves anyone, or that wealth disqualifies. The problem named is trusting in riches, and the man’s grief is the evidence of where his trust was. A poor man can be owned by what little he has.

It does not say the man was lost. Mark tells us he went away grieved and tells us nothing further. We are not given the end of his story, and it is not our business to supply one.

And it does not make selling everything the price of eternal life. If it did, the answer to who then can be saved would be those who liquidate, and Jesus says the opposite: the impossibility is not solved on the human side at all.

Why It Belongs Here

Because saved in verse 26 is the full sense. Not a body preserved, not a rescue from water — the disciples are asking about entering the kingdom and inheriting eternal life, and they use the saving verb to say it.

And because the answer they receive is the one the whole gateway keeps arriving at from different directions. The healings show a Savior who restores. The rescues show a Savior who intervenes. This passage shows why a Savior is needed at all: because the thing being asked for cannot be reached from where any of us are standing.

With men it is impossible. That sentence is not a discouragement. It is the reason the gospel is good news rather than good advice.
