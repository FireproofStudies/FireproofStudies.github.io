How Did the Woman’s Faith Save Her?

Why her tears and ointment could not purchase forgiveness—and what Jesus meant by sending her into peace

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 9 min read • Luke 7:36–50
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Luke 7:36–50

A woman walks into a dinner party where everyone knows what she is, kneels behind Jesus, and weeps until His feet are wet. She has no towel, so she uses her hair. She kisses His feet and pours out ointment she must have paid dearly for. Nobody invited her, and nobody wanted her there.

And at the end of the scene Jesus tells her, “Thy faith hath saved thee; go in peace.”

The question the passage raises is unavoidable: saved from what, and by what? She has just performed an extraordinary act of devotion in front of hostile witnesses. Did that buy something? Preachers have sometimes implied it, and one verse in the middle of the story seems to say so outright. But the parable Jesus tells before it points firmly the other way, and His last sentence settles the matter.

Two Ways of Seeing

Luke has just recorded the accusation that the Son of man is “a friend of publicans and sinners.” He then seats Jesus at a Pharisee’s table and lets a woman known as a sinner walk in. The story shows what that friendship actually costs and means.

Simon sees a category. “This man, if he were a prophet, would have known who and what manner of woman this is that toucheth him: for she is a sinner.” He assumes two things—that Jesus does not know, and that holiness protects itself by keeping its distance.

He is wrong on both counts. Jesus knows exactly who she is. He also knows what Simon is thinking, which Simon has not said aloud.

It is worth noting what Luke does not tell us. He never names her, never specifies her sins, and never calls her a prostitute. She is not Mary Magdalene, whom Luke introduces by name in the next chapter, nor Mary of Bethany in the later and separate anointing. The anonymity is deliberate, and it keeps our eyes where Jesus puts them: on many real sins, an unpayable debt, and a woman at His feet.

Neither Had Anything to Pay

Jesus tells Simon a story about two debtors. One owes five hundred denarii, the other fifty—a tenfold difference, and Jesus is clearly inviting Simon to place himself and the woman on that scale.

But the decisive clause is the one about both of them: “when they had nothing to pay.” The smaller debtor is not nearly solvent. He is just as bankrupt as the larger one. Simon’s respectability has not made him able to settle his account; it has only made the account smaller in his own estimation.

The creditor, the KJV says, “frankly forgave them both.” The Greek verb is built on the word for grace: he graciously released them. He takes no part payment, requires no schedule of future works, and waits for no display of affection.

Then comes the question: which of them will love him most? Simon answers correctly, and in doing so has already interpreted the woman’s behaviour without realising it.

“For She Loved Much”

Now the hard verse. “Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much.”

In English that sounds causal—forgiven because she loved. But that reading turns the parable Jesus has just told upside down. The debtors did not love their way into release; they were released and therefore loved. If the parable means anything, love follows forgiveness.

The Greek conjunction can introduce evidence rather than cause: her many sins stand forgiven, as her great love makes plain. And Jesus immediately gives the governing principle in the other direction: “to whom little is forgiven, the same loveth little.” Forgiveness explains love, not the reverse. Simon’s small love is diagnostic of how little release he thinks he needs.

Then the sentence that closes the question. Jesus does not say your ointment has saved you, or your tears, or your humiliation, or even your love. He says: Thy faith hath saved thee.

Love and faith are not rivals here, but they are not the same thing either. Faith entrusts the self to Jesus. Love is what the freed life does next.

What Kind of Faith Was It?

She never speaks. Not one word of hers is recorded. Her faith is entirely visible rather than audible, and it is worth asking what it looked like.

She entered a room where she knew she was despised. She went to Jesus rather than away from Him. She stayed at His feet while a Pharisee silently catalogued her. She gave Him something expensive and something humiliating—her tears, her hair, her reputation, her ointment—without defending herself or explaining.

She accepted Jesus’ verdict about her instead of Simon’s. That is the heart of it. She did not come to argue that she was better than the room believed. She came believing that His holiness could receive her rather than recoil from her.

Simon, meanwhile, has Jesus in his house without entrusting himself to Him at all. Proximity is not faith. One of them is nearer to Christ, and it is not the host.

Repentance is never named in this passage, but it is unmistakably present. She is not looking for relief from shame while keeping the life that produced it. She comes into the open, accepts His authority over her sins, and turns her whole devotion toward Him.

Seest Thou This Woman?

For most of the scene Jesus is turned toward her and speaking to Simon. Then He asks the question that breaks the room open: “Seest thou this woman?”

Simon has been looking at her the entire time and has not seen her once. So Jesus itemises—the water Simon did not provide, the kiss he did not give, the oil he did not offer—each set against what she has done unasked. The host has been outdone in hospitality by an intruder.

Then Jesus speaks to her directly, out loud, in front of everyone: “Thy sins are forgiven.”

He could have said it quietly. He does not. The public declaration contradicts the identity the room has assigned her. Her sins are not minimised—He calls them many—but they no longer determine her standing before God, and everyone at the table now knows it.

They also grasp what He has claimed. “Who is this that forgiveth sins also?” That question is the theological centre of the story. Her salvation does not rest on the intensity of her weeping. It rests on the authority of the One receiving her.

How Can Faith Save Anyone?

Faith is the grammatical subject of “hath saved thee,” and that has bothered readers for centuries. Does faith save?

Scripture is quite willing to name the receiving instrument without making it the Savior. Look at what Jesus does in this passage: He receives the sinner, exposes the true condition of both debtors, freely releases the debt, declares the forgiveness publicly, identifies her faith, and sends her into peace. Every saving action in the story is His.

Her faith saves her because it puts her in His hands. It contributes no payment, no righteousness, no atonement. It is not a spiritual quality being credited for its own excellence—it is reliance on a Person who is standing right there.

And the tense matters. “Hath saved” describes a completed action with a standing result. She does not walk out of that house hoping that enough future love will eventually persuade Him. She walks out already forgiven, by His word.

Go into Peace

The phrase is literally go into peace—she is being sent forward into something, not merely dismissed from something.

Peace here is not the calm that follows an emotional storm. It is the relational wholeness that exists when guilt and estrangement no longer stand between a person and God. Forgiveness removed the debt, but the removal was in service of the relationship. Jesus does not simply send her away from condemnation; He sends her into fellowship.

There is a practical mercy in this too. Simon’s contempt does not follow her out of the house as God’s verdict. She will likely live in a town that remembers her, and the memory will not be gentle. But the word she now lives under was spoken by the only person in that room who knew her completely.

She came in as everyone’s example of a sinner. She left under Christ’s declaration—forgiven, saved, and at peace—and the difference between her and her host was never that she had sins and he did not. It was that she knew where to bring them.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Many sins, an unpayable debt, estrangement from God, condemnation, and an identity ruled by her past
Saved into what?Forgiven fellowship with God, a public verdict of release, and peace
Who saves?Jesus Christ, who has authority on earth to forgive sins
Through what means?Faith in Jesus—surrendered reliance, shown in repentance and love
Did her love earn it?No. Forgiven debt produces love; her love displays the release she has already received
Is this bodily healing?No. Luke uses the same verb for healing nearby, but here the domain is sin, forgiveness, and peace
