What Does “No Other Name” Mean in Acts 4:12?

How a healed man at the temple gate leads to the claim that there is salvation in nobody else

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Acts 3:1–4:12
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Acts 3:1–4:12

A man over forty years old, lame from birth, is carried every day to a temple gate to beg. He has never walked, and he has learned to expect nothing from passers-by except coins.

Peter has no coins. What he says instead—“In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk”—sets off a chain of events that ends with Peter standing before the Sanhedrin making one of the most sweeping claims in the New Testament: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.”

The distance between those two moments is the whole subject of this passage. One man’s legs, and then the salvation of the human race. The same Greek word does duty for both, and understanding how it stretches from one to the other is the key to reading saving language anywhere in the New Testament.

What the Man Received

The healing is immediate and thoroughly public. His feet and ankle bones receive strength. He stands, walks, leaps, enters the temple, and praises God—and the crowd recognises him as the beggar they have stepped around for years.

Luke’s phrase for it is “perfect soundness,” complete wholeness. This is not the temporary easing of a symptom. A man who had been placed at the gate his whole life walks through it on his own feet.

And when the crowd swings toward Peter and John, Peter shuts that down immediately: “why look ye so earnestly on us, as though by our own power or holiness we had made this man to walk?” Not our power. Not our holiness. The God of Abraham has glorified His Servant Jesus, whom you delivered up and denied, whom God raised from the dead.

By Whom Has This Man Been Saved?

When the council convenes the next day, they ask by what power or name this was done. Peter’s answer contains the first of our two saving statements, and the KJV renders it “by what means he is made whole.”

The Greek verb is sōzō—the ordinary word for save—in the perfect passive. A flat-footed rendering would be: by whom has this man been saved?

The peril here is entirely specific. He has been saved from congenital lameness. The perfect tense fits the scene exactly: the result is standing in front of them.

The KJV’s “made whole” is not an evasion of the Greek; it is an excellent contextual translation. Sōzō covers rescue, preservation, healing, and comprehensive salvation, and the passage decides which. Here the passage could not be plainer: the man’s legs work.

This matters for how we read the rest of the New Testament. Calling this healing salvation does not quietly prove that every healing is a conversion. Luke never narrates this man’s forgiveness or new birth. His praise and his presence in the temple point toward something larger, but the saving verb in 4:9 names the wholeness everyone in the room can see.

The Name Is Not a Formula

Peter returns to the name relentlessly—in the command, in the explanation to the crowd, in the council’s question, in his answer, and finally in the great claim of verse 12.

The name is not an incantation whose correct pronunciation produces power. A name in Scripture stands for the person: his identity, his authority, his active presence. Peter is naming a specific man who was publicly known in Jerusalem, publicly executed, and—he insists—publicly raised.

This is why he refuses to let the miracle build him a following. The power is not in his voice, his touch, his office, or his holiness. The apostles are witnesses and instruments; the living Jesus is the agent. When the council asks which name, they have unknowingly asked the only question that matters, and Peter answers it with a sermon.

Whose Faith Was It?

Acts 3:16 is famously tangled: “his name through faith in his name hath made this man strong … yea, the faith which is by him hath given him this perfect soundness.”

Luke does not clearly identify whose faith. Before the healing the man was expecting money, not legs. Peter commands, takes his hand, and lifts him. Nothing suggests that Peter first measured the man’s confidence or that the man generated the miracle by believing hard enough.

The phrase may point to the apostles’ reliance on Jesus, or to the man’s responsive trust as he is pulled upright, or to faith at work throughout the encounter. What Peter does make unmistakable is the source: it is “the faith which is by him”—faith that comes through Jesus, not an autonomous human power that operates on Him.

That distinction is not academic, and it needs saying plainly for pastoral reasons. This passage has been used to tell suffering people that they remain ill because their faith is deficient. The man in this story had lived more than forty years without healing, and Luke offers not one word suggesting that his condition was a verdict on his spiritual state.

The Prince of Life

Peter’s title for Jesus is arresting: the Prince—the Author, the Founder—of life. And he sets it in the sharpest possible antithesis:

And killed the Prince of life, whom God hath raised from the dead; whereof we are witnesses.

Acts 3:15

Human authorities pronounced death on the Author of life. God overturned the verdict. And Jesus now acts from beyond death with a life that death could not contain—which is precisely what the crowd has just watched happen to a pair of useless legs.

So the healing is not a crowd-gathering device attached to the front of a sermon. It is a sign of the resurrection Peter is preaching. The risen Christ’s life reached into a damaged body and made it work.

It is a sign, though, and not the completion. The man is still mortal. Acts nowhere promises immediate bodily healing to every believer. But his soundness is a genuine anticipation of what the Author of life will finally do, and it points in the right direction.

Why the Crowd Must Repent

The miracle exposes a crisis that has nothing to do with anyone’s legs. The people and their leaders rejected the Holy and Just One, asked for a murderer instead, and killed the Author of life.

Peter grants their ignorance. He does not treat ignorance as safety, now that God has raised His Son and made Him known. So he commands: “Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out.” And later: God sent His risen Son “to bless you, in turning away every one of you from his iniquities.”

That is the language that prevents Acts 4:12 from being about physiotherapy. The need Peter is addressing now includes guilt over a rejected Christ, sins requiring erasure, iniquities to be turned from, a promised Prophet who must be heard, and destruction outside the people God is restoring.

Once again repentance and faith are one movement: turn from the false verdict, and entrust yourself to the Jesus God has vindicated.

Does Acts 4:12 Refer Only to Healing?

No—and the language itself rules it out.

If Peter meant only that no other healer could have fixed this man’s legs, the sentence is wildly over-built. Instead he says there is salvation in no other person; that no other name exists under heaven; that this name has been given among men; and that we must be saved by it.

That “we” is the tell. Peter is standing in front of the high priest’s family, and he places himself and them inside the same necessity. The word must names a need that no office, no ancestry, no health, and no religious pedigree exempts anyone from.

And the sermon that precedes it supplies the content: repentance, sins blotted out, refreshing from the presence of the Lord, the restitution of all things, blessing through Abraham’s seed, and turning from iniquity. Verse 12 gathers all of it into one Person.

Why No Other Name

Christian exclusivity is often heard as the claim that one religious password works and the others fail. That is not what Peter is arguing.

The name identifies a Person who occupies a place nobody else occupies. In this passage alone Jesus is the Holy and Just One; the Servant glorified by the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; the Prince of life; the One killed by men and raised by God; the promised Prophet who must be heard; the seed in whom the families of the earth are blessed; and the stone the builders rejected, now made the head of the corner.

No other name saves because no one else stands in that place. Salvation cannot be detached from Christ and redistributed through other channels, because its end is life with God in the risen Son—and nobody else is the risen Son.

Note too that the name is “given among men.” Salvation is God’s provision before it is anyone’s response. He supplies the Savior, raises Him, establishes Him, and makes Him known. The command to repent does not create a second saving source; it summons people to receive the one God has given.

Distinguished, and Not Severed

Everything in this passage depends on holding two things together without confusing them.

They are not identical. Acts 4:9 is one body rescued from one disability. Acts 4:12 is what every human being needs. A healed man may still reject Christ; a believer may remain disabled for life and possess everything Peter is talking about.

They are not unrelated. The same Jesus is the agent, the same name is proclaimed, and Luke has deliberately used the same verb for both. The healing shows what kind of Savior this is—one who restores, makes whole, reverses ruin, and gives access.

And the sign points forward. The man’s new feet are not the whole of salvation, but they are not stage scenery either. The Author of life will not finally leave His people as forgiven souls in ruined bodies. What happened at the Beautiful gate is a small, early, physical instalment of something He intends to finish.

He needed strength in his feet. Everyone in the room, Peter included, needed the life found in God’s risen Son.

At a Glance

Saved from what? (4:9)Lameness from birth—an inability to stand, walk, or enter the temple
Saved from what? (4:12)Sin and iniquity, a false verdict about Jesus, alienation from the Author of life, and destruction
Who saves?The risen Jesus, in both verses. Peter explicitly refuses credit
Through what means?The name of Jesus, and faith in or through that name; then repentance and turning
Are the two the same?No. But they are not unrelated—the healing is a sign of what the greater salvation completes
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?4:9, no. 4:12, unmistakably yes
