“Is It Lawful to Save Life, or to Kill?”

Jesus puts saving and killing in the same sentence on a Sabbath morning — and the men watching Him answer with their silence

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • Mark 3:1–6
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 3:1–6

The question Jesus asks in the synagogue that morning is not the one anybody expected, and it is not really about the Sabbath.

Is it lawful to do good on the sabbath days, or to do evil? to save life, or to kill?

Mark 3:4

To save life, or to kill. Those are the two options He offers, and the men He is speaking to had not thought they were choosing between them. They thought they were choosing between healing today and healing tomorrow.

The Setup

Mark tells us the shape of the room in one sentence: “And they watched him, whether he would heal him on the sabbath day; that they might accuse him.”

There is a man there with a withered hand. He has not asked for anything. He may not know he is about to become the centre of an argument. And there is a group present whose interest in him is entirely instrumental — he is useful as a test.

That is the situation Jesus walks into, and the first thing He does is refuse to let it stay private. “Stand forth,” He says to the man. Literally, rise into the middle. Whatever is about to happen will happen in full view.

What the Word Means Here

The verb in Jesus’ question is sōsai — the aorist infinitive of the ordinary word for saving. The KJV renders it save, and pairs it, as the Greek does, with to kill.

The peril in view is not sin or judgment. It is a ruined hand in a body that has to work for a living, and behind that, the loss of life itself. Jesus is asking what the day is for: is it a day on which a life may be preserved and restored, or a day on which one may be left to waste?

That is the whole contextual range of the word here. It means to make a life whole rather than let it be destroyed.

Why the Alternatives Are So Extreme

Notice that Jesus does not offer a moderate option. He does not say is it lawful to heal today or to wait until tomorrow? — which is how the men in the room would have framed it, and which would have been a reasonable rabbinic question with reasonable answers on both sides.

He says: to save life, or to kill.

That is deliberately uncomfortable, and it depends on a principle running underneath the scene: there is no neutral ground in front of a person you could help. The withheld good is not nothing. To have the power to restore and to decline it is to side with the ruin.

The men are silent. Mark says so — “they held their peace” — and it is not the silence of men thinking. It is the silence of men who can see where the answer leads and will not go there.

And then the terrible irony that Mark places six verses later: the people who would not permit a healing on the Sabbath leave that same Sabbath to plot a killing. “And the Pharisees went forth, and straightway took counsel with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him.”

Jesus asked whether the day was for saving or killing. By the end of the morning both parties in the synagogue had answered.

Anger and Grief

Mark alone records what was on Jesus’ face. “And when he had looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts.”

This is one of the few places in the Gospels where Jesus’ anger is named directly, and Mark pairs it immediately with grief. The word for hardness is a medical term for the callus that forms over a healed bone — tissue that has become insensible.

That combination is worth pausing on. The anger is not irritation at being tested; it is a response to people who have become unable to feel the case in front of them. And it is grief, not contempt. He is not disgusted with them. He is grieved for them, which is a different thing, and it means He still regards their condition as a loss.

The Healing

“Stretch forth thine hand.”

It is a command the man cannot obey. That is the point. He is asked to do the one thing his condition prevents, and in the attempt the power arrives. Mark records no touch, no word over the hand, no ceremony — only an instruction and a hand that works.

There is a small and honest observation here that the passage supports without straining: God’s commands often arrive at exactly the place of incapacity, and obedience turns out to be the form the gift takes. That is not a general theory of healing, and it should not be turned into one. But it is what happened in this room.

Why This Is in a Study of Salvation

Because the word is here, and because the question it sits inside is larger than the hand.

Jesus is not merely defending a healing. He is asking what God’s law was ever for. The Sabbath was given as rest — the day when a people who had been worked to death in Egypt were told to stop. To turn that into a reason for leaving a man crippled is to invert it completely.

So the passage does two things at once. It records a real bodily restoration, which is what saved means in verse 4. And it exposes a religion that had become capable of watching suffering with theological satisfaction.

That second thing is not a small point in a project about salvation. A great deal of harm has been done by people holding correct doctrine in front of someone they could have helped. Jesus called it a choice between saving and killing, and He was not exaggerating for effect.

The Narrower Sense and the Wider One

Nothing here says the man with the withered hand was converted. Mark does not tell us what became of him. The saving in this verse is the preservation and restoration of a life, and it should not be inflated.

But it belongs to a pattern. The One who asks whether the day is for saving or killing will shortly be killed on a day the same authorities considered holy — and by that death will save the people they were so careful to protect from Him.

The hand was restored in front of everyone. The larger restoration was, for the moment, invisible in the room. Both were the work of the same person, and the question He asked that morning still stands: given the power to make something whole, what does a religion that declines think it is for?

At a Glance

Saved from what?A withered hand, and the deeper ruin of a religion that would rather watch than heal
Saved into what?A restored hand, and a restored place in the working life of the community
Who saves?Jesus, by a word — “Stretch forth thine hand”
Through what means?A command obeyed by a man who could not have obeyed it before
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?No. This is bodily restoration, set inside a question about what religion is for
