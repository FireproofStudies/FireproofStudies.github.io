Saved in Context

“To the Saving of the Soul”

The letter’s sharpest warning ends not in despair but in confidence — we are not of them who draw back, but of them that believe

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Hebrews 10:26–39
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Hebrews 10:26–39

This is the passage most likely to frighten a believer, and it ends on one of the most reassuring notes in the letter. A study has to hold both, because Hebrews does.

The Warning

For if we sin wilfully after that we have received the knowledge of the truth, there remaineth no more sacrifice for sins, but a certain fearful looking for of judgment.

Hebrews 10:26–27

The language is severe, and there is no softening it honestly. The writer describes a wilful sinning after receiving the knowledge of the truth — not a stumble, not a season of weakness, but a deliberate, settled repudiation. He goes on: someone who has trodden under foot the Son of God, counted the blood of the covenant an unholy thing, and done despite to the Spirit of grace. For such rejection, he says, there remains no more sacrifice — because the one sacrifice, Christ’s, is exactly what is being trampled.

This is the same warning territory as Hebrews 6, and a study should state plainly what it is and is not. It is not a threat hung over every believer who sins; the whole letter has been urging sinners to draw near to the throne of grace for mercy. It is a warning against the specific, decisive act of turning from Christ altogether — walking away from the only sacrifice there is. To the one doing that, there is nothing left, because he is spurning the very thing that could save him.

Whose Warning, and to Whom

The writer keeps the warning inside the believing community by his pronouns: if we sin wilfully. He includes himself in the address. This is a family warning, spoken to keep people from a cliff, not a verdict pronounced over them.

And its function is exactly that — preservation. The warnings of Hebrews are among the means God uses to keep His people from drawing back. To hear the warning and be sobered by it is itself a mark of not being the person it describes. The one truly treading the Son of God underfoot is not troubled by verse 26; the believer who trembles at it is showing the very tenderness of conscience that apostasy has lost.

The Turn to Encouragement

The writer does not leave his readers on the edge. He turns immediately to their past and their future, and the tone changes entirely.

He reminds them of the former days, when after they were illuminated they endured a great fight of afflictions — publicly reproached, made a spectacle, taking joyfully the spoiling of their goods, standing with prisoners. These are not people on the verge of apostasy; they are people who have already suffered for Christ and held. The writer recalls it to steady them: you have endured before, do not throw it away now.

Cast not away therefore your confidence, which hath great recompence of reward. For ye have need of patience, that, after ye have done the will of God, ye might receive the promise.

Hebrews 10:35–36

The remedy for the fear the warning raises is not frantic effort but patience — a continuing, not a starting over. Do not cast away the confidence you already have. What is needed is endurance, the same thing the good ground in the parable brought forth fruit with.

“To the Saving of the Soul”

Then the close, and the saving word:

Now the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back, my soul shall have no pleasure in him. But we are not of them who draw back unto perdition; but of them that believe to the saving of the soul.

Hebrews 10:38–39

Eis peripoiēsin psychēs — to the saving, the gaining, the securing of the soul. The writer sets two paths against each other — drawing back unto perdition, or believing to the saving of the soul — and then, crucially, he places himself and his readers on the second: we are not of them who draw back … but of them that believe.

That is where the warning has been driving all along. It ends not in suspense but in confidence. Having sounded the alarm as loudly as anywhere in the New Testament, the writer expresses settled assurance about the people he has warned. The warning was real, and it did its work — and the conclusion is not perhaps you will make it but we are of them that believe.

This is the pattern the whole corpus keeps meeting, stated here at its sharpest. Warning and assurance are not rivals; they are two instruments of the same keeping. The warning guards the door; the assurance names those inside it. And the person who believes to the saving of the soul is precisely the one the warning has kept from drawing back.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense — final preservation of the soul through persevering faith — set against perdition. To believe to the saving of the soul is to be of the company that does not draw back, that lives by faith, that holds its confidence to the end.

Its contribution to the gateway is the deliberate joining of the severe and the sure. Hebrews will not let the warning be softened, and it will not let the warning have the last word. It ends by placing the believer on the saved side of the line — of them that believe to the saving of the soul — which is exactly the reassurance the warning was written to secure.

Through what means?Patient confidence that does not cast away, and does not draw back
