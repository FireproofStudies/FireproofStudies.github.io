Saved in Context

Three Uses of “Saved” in One Story

A dying girl, a bleeding woman, and a father who asks that his daughter may be “made whole” — Mark uses the saving verb three times and means her body every time

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Mark 5:21–43
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 5:21–43

Mark uses the ordinary Greek word for saving three times in this passage, and the King James translators rendered it three different ways: she shall be healed, I shall be whole, hath made thee whole.

An English reader would never guess the word underneath is sōzō — the same verb behind “he shall save his people from their sins.”

Three occurrences, one story, and every one of them means a body that stopped working started working again.

Two Stories, One Interruption

Mark tells this as a sandwich, and the structure carries meaning. A synagogue ruler named Jairus falls at Jesus’ feet because his daughter is dying. Jesus goes with him. On the way a woman in the crowd reaches out, and everything stops. Only when her business is finished does the story return to the house — by which time the girl is dead.

The delay is not incidental. Mark makes the father stand there and listen while his daughter’s time runs out.

Jairus: “That She May Be Healed”

“My little daughter lieth at the point of death: I pray thee, come and lay thy hands on her, that she may be healed; and she shall live.”

The Greek is sōthē — a passive subjunctive of the saving verb. That she may be saved. The peril is stated plainly in the same sentence: she is at the point of death.

Notice who is asking. Jairus is a ruler of the synagogue — an official of the institution that in the previous chapter had begun taking counsel to destroy Jesus. He falls at His feet anyway. Mark does not comment on the cost of that, but there was one.

The Woman: “I Shall Be Whole”

Mark gives her more space than Matthew does, and the details are pitiless: twelve years of haemorrhage, many physicians, everything she had spent, and she was nothing bettered but rather grew worse.

Then, from inside the crowd: “If I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole.”

Sōthēsomai — future passive. I shall be saved. She is not asking for forgiveness. She is stating what she believes will happen to her body.

Under the purity law her condition made her ceremonially unclean, and everything she touched with her. Twelve years of that is not merely illness; it is a life arranged around not making contact. Which explains why she comes from behind, in a crush of people, hoping to take something without being seen.

Why He Would Not Let It Stay Anonymous

Jesus stops. He knows power has gone out of Him, and He asks who touched His clothes.

The disciples think the question is absurd — the crowd is pressing on Him from every side. But Mark distinguishes what the crowd is doing from what she did. Many were touching Him. One reached.

She comes “fearing and trembling,” falls down, and tells Him all the truth. In public, to a synagogue official’s face, in front of everyone she had been avoiding for over a decade.

And He says: “Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague.”

Sesōken se — a perfect: has saved you, with a standing result. She is not going to be healed. She is healed, and will stay healed.

Three things in that sentence are worth having. He calls her daughter, which restores her to a family in front of the crowd. He sends her into peace, not merely away from illness. And He tells her to be whole of her plague — the affliction is named and dismissed rather than politely ignored.

Did Her Faith Do It?

“Thy faith hath made thee whole” is the sentence that causes trouble, and it needs the same answer it needs everywhere in the Gospels.

Faith has no power in itself; it has an object. Hers was reliance on Jesus, expressed by getting close enough to touch Him. The cloth was the point of contact, not the source — and Jesus deliberately moves the conversation off the garment and onto her, precisely so that no one leaves thinking the fringe was doing something.

The alternative reading — that sufficient belief compels a healing — is exactly what this passage prevents. He asked who touched Him because He intended her to be known, addressed, and sent away with more than a cure.

The Interruption Was the Point

While He is still speaking, men arrive from the house: “Thy daughter is dead: why troublest thou the Master any further?”

And Jesus says to Jairus, in the middle of that: “Be not afraid, only believe.”

The father has just watched a woman with a twelve-year illness receive what he was hoping for, and lost his window while it happened. Mark has arranged the delay so that Jairus hears thy faith hath made thee whole just before he is told his daughter has died.

The girl is twelve. The woman had been bleeding twelve years. Mark expects the reader to notice.

“The Damsel Is Not Dead, but Sleepeth”

At the house He puts out the mourners, who laugh at Him, and takes the parents and three disciples into the room.

Talitha cumi. Mark keeps the Aramaic — the actual words, in the actual language, remembered. Damsel, I say unto thee, arise.

She gets up and walks. And then the detail that always startles: “he commanded that something should be given her to eat.” The Lord of life, having raised a dead child, notices that she is hungry.

What This Passage Claims and What It Does Not

The saving verb here means bodily restoration in all three instances. It does not mean conversion, and none of the three occurrences should be read as though it did. Mark does not tell us what the woman believed about Jesus’ identity, or what became of Jairus’s household.

But this is the passage where the narrower sense presses hardest against the larger one. Jesus raises a dead girl, and calls her death sleep. That word does not stay small in the New Testament — the apostles will use it for Christians who have died, precisely because of what the resurrection makes of death.

So Mark is doing two things honestly at once. He is recording that a woman stopped bleeding and a child got out of bed, in the plain sense of the word he uses. And he is showing the reader who it is that can speak to a corpse in Aramaic and be obeyed.

The word means her body. The story means rather more than her body — and the second thing does not require the first to be exaggerated.

At a Glance

Saved from what?A twelve-year haemorrhage, and a twelve-year-old girl’s death
Saved into what?Bodily healing, peace, freedom from the affliction, and a girl restored to her parents
Who saves?Jesus. The woman’s faith is her reliance on Him, not a power of its own
Through what means?Faith that reached Him — and, for the girl, a hand and a word
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Not directly. But the raising of the dead is a signpost the Gospel intends
