“He That Shall Endure Unto the End, the Same Shall Be Saved” — Mark’s Account

Mark’s Olivet Discourse gives the endurance promise its own frame — and adds that the gospel must first be published among all nations

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Mark 13:1–13
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 13:1–13

Mark’s version of the endurance promise sits in his Olivet Discourse, parallel to the published Matthew 24 study. The words are the same, and Mark surrounds them with one emphasis of his own.

The Setting

The disciples marvel at the temple stones; Jesus foretells that not one will be left upon another. Pressed for signs, He warns of deceivers, wars, earthquakes, famines — the beginnings of sorrows — and then turns from the world’s convulsions to the disciples’ own trials:

But take heed to yourselves: for they shall deliver you up to councils; and in the synagogues ye shall be beaten: and ye shall be brought before rulers and kings for my sake, for a testimony against them.

Mark 13:9

The persecution is for my sake, as in the mission charge, and it lands on the disciples personally — councils, beatings, trials, betrayal by brother, father, child. And into this Jesus sets the promise:

And ye shall be hated of all men for my name’s sake: but he that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved.

Mark 13:13

Ho hypomeinas eis telos sōthēsetai — he that endureth to the end shall be saved. Identical to Matthew, and identical in force. The verb is passive; the disciple endures, God saves. Endurance marks the person who is saved; it does not purchase the salvation.

The Gospel First Published

Mark’s distinctive emphasis comes one verse before the promise:

And the gospel must first be published among all nations.

Mark 13:10

Mark plants this in the middle of the persecution forecast, and the placement is telling. The very trials that threaten to overwhelm the disciples are the occasions of the gospel’s spread. Hauled before rulers and kings for a testimony, the persecuted become witnesses; the courtroom becomes a pulpit. The endurance Jesus asks is not merely private survival — it is bound up with the mission, the publishing of the gospel to all nations, which must happen before the end.

So the endurance promise in Mark is set inside a purpose larger than the disciple’s own preservation. He endures not only to be saved himself, but as part of the gospel’s advance through a hostile world. The hatred does not halt the mission; it carries it forward.

Not by Their Own Words

As in Matthew, Jesus removes the anxiety of the courtroom: “But when they shall lead you, and deliver you up, take no thought beforehand what ye shall speak … for it is not ye that speak, but the Holy Ghost.” The endurance is Spirit-supplied. The disciple standing before a governor is not thrown on his own eloquence; the Holy Ghost speaks in him. The salvation of those who endure runs on divine enabling, not human strength — the same principle the whole gateway keeps meeting.

Endurance, Not Achievement

The reading holds exactly as in the parallels, and it is worth stating once more against the fear the verse provokes. He that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved is not a threat that a single failure forfeits salvation. It describes the character of saving faith — that it holds to Christ through hatred, betrayal, and trial, to the end. The endurance is the fruit and evidence of a real allegiance, not a wage that buys deliverance.

Mark’s own narrative proves the point. The disciples he is writing for, and the twelve who first heard it, would falter — one would deny Christ three times that very week. Restored, they endured to the end and most died for the name. The promise is not that the saved never stumble; it is that the God who saves keeps His own holding to Christ across a lifetime of pressure, and vindicates them at the last.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense — final salvation and vindication — for those who endure persecution for Christ’s name. The peril is being overcome by hatred; the response is enduring allegiance; the agent, by the passive verb, is God.

Its contribution to the gateway, beside its Matthean parallels, is Mark’s binding of endurance to mission: the gospel must first be published among all nations. The disciple’s perseverance is not only self-preservation but participation in the gospel’s spread, its trials turned into testimony. The one who endures to the end is saved — kept by the Spirit who speaks in him, in the service of a gospel that must reach every nation before the end comes.
