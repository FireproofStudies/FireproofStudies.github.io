What Does “By Grace Are Ye Saved through Faith” Mean?

Why Paul describes salvation as being made alive, raised, and seated with Christ—and what that leaves for works to do

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Ephesians 2:1–10
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Ephesians 2:1–10

Ephesians 2:8 may be the most quoted sentence in the New Testament about salvation, and it is often quoted alone. Pulled out of its paragraph it becomes a slogan about the mechanism of getting saved—grace in, works out—and the argument Paul is actually making disappears.

Read the whole passage and something larger emerges. Paul is not primarily explaining a transaction. He is describing a movement: from death to life, from the course of this world to a seat in the heavenly places, from walking in trespasses to walking in works God prepared. And every step of that movement happens with Christ and in Christ. The prepositions are not decorative. They are the point.

Chapter One Is Still Running

Paul has just finished describing what God did to Jesus. He raised Him from the dead, seated Him at His own right hand, put all things under His feet, and gave Him as head over all things to the church.

Chapter two applies that same sequence to us. God made Christ alive; God makes believers alive with Christ. God raised Christ; God raises believers with Him. God seated Christ in the heavenly places; God seats believers in Christ Jesus. Paul even coins compound verbs to say it—made-alive-together, raised-together, seated-together.

So salvation here is not a parcel dispatched from a distant Christ to people who remain where they were. It is participation in the life, the standing, and the destiny of the risen Son. Everything else in the passage hangs on that.

The two saving statements both use the same construction: charitī este sesōsmenoi—“by grace you are, having been saved.” A completed action with a standing result. Not “God once helped you,” and not “your salvation is perpetually undecided.” You have been saved, and you now live inside what that action produced.

Dead People Who Walk

Paul’s dead are busy. They walk, they follow, they desire, they fulfil, they obey a pattern. Whatever “dead in trespasses and sins” means, it plainly does not mean unconscious, inert, or incapable of choosing.

Death here is human life cut off from communion with God and running under a hostile management. Paul names the management with some care: the trespasses themselves, the course of this world, the prince of the power of the air, the spirit working in the children of disobedience, and the desires of flesh and mind. These people are active—but no amount of that activity can produce the life they were made for. A man may swim vigorously in the wrong ocean.

This is worth stating carefully, because the metaphor gets pressed too hard in both directions. Paul is not describing corpses who cannot hear, resist, or answer when God addresses them through the gospel. Nor is he describing people who could climb out if they tried harder. He is describing morally active human beings whose entire walk belongs to a dying humanity, and who therefore need life to come from outside—from God, in Christ.

Children of Wrath by Nature

Paul is careful to include himself: “among whom also we all had our conversation in times past.” Jew and Gentile alike. Nobody starts from a neutral humanity, and no ancestry exempts anyone.

“By nature” keeps its ordinary force—a condition associated with birth. By natural birth human beings belong to the humanity east of Eden: mortal, cut off from the Tree of Life, born into a world already structured by rebellion, and moving toward judgment. That is inherited. What is not inherited is anyone’s personal guilt for Adam’s act.

And Paul does not let the inherited condition swallow personal responsibility. The people under wrath walked in trespasses, followed the world’s course, and fulfilled the desires of flesh and mind. Later in the letter Paul says God’s wrath comes upon the children of disobedience “because of these things”—naming practiced sins. The condition is real and constitutive; the willing sins make the rebellion personally their own.

Wrath, then, is not divine bad temper toward creatures God made. It is His settled judgment on a rebellion that people join.

But God

Two words turn the paragraph, and they are the only turn available. Not but they tried harder, not but something in them survived. But God.

He is rich in mercy. He acts out of great love. He makes alive together with Christ, raises together, seats together. He intends to display the exceeding riches of His grace in the ages to come. He makes a new workmanship and prepares a new walk.

Grace in this paragraph is not mere leniency or a generally favourable attitude. It is God’s free, generous, effective kindness toward people who could not establish life with Him on any terms. Mercy answers their misery; love moves toward them; grace supplies what they could neither earn nor manufacture.

The verb in verse 5 is worth pausing on. God did not give life and then introduce us to Christ. He made us alive together with Christ. The life is not a spiritual substance issued to independent people. Its source and its location are the risen Son.

Raised and Seated—Already?

Paul is not claiming that believers’ bodies have risen or that they have left the earth. He is describing where they now stand and who they now are.

Because Christ is their life and their representative head, His resurrection and His heavenly position determine their standing. They belong to the new humanity and the coming age while still living in this one. Their bodily resurrection is so secure in Him that Paul can speak of the governing reality as already theirs.

And the seating has a purpose beyond safety: “that in the ages to come he might shew the exceeding riches of his grace.” The saved are not merely pulled out of danger. They become the permanent exhibit of what God’s kindness in Christ actually cost and accomplished. Humanity was made to display God. Salvation restores the vocation—now as new creatures displaying grace.

Is Faith Itself the Gift?

“And that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God.” Generations have argued about what that refers to.

The grammar does not point most naturally at faith alone. Faith is feminine in Greek; that is neuter. The demonstrative most naturally gathers up the whole preceding reality—by grace you are saved through faith, and this whole thing is not from yourselves; it is God’s gift.

Grammatical gender cannot settle every theological question by itself, and Paul is certainly not describing faith as an autonomous human virtue. God gives the Son, announces Him in the gospel, and works by the Spirit to convict, draw, and enlighten. The entire encounter is surrounded and enabled by grace.

But faith remains a real response by a real person. It is not a substance implanted in a passive recipient and then operated remotely. To believe is to entrust yourself to Christ—to stop ruling yourself and rely on Him. Faith earns nothing, contributes nothing, and creates nothing. It receives; God gives life.

Paul’s point is bigger than the disputed pronoun, and it survives whichever way you resolve it: nothing in this saving reality gives anyone grounds to boast. Not one part of it.

What Works Are Actually For

Paul excludes works and then, two clauses later, restores them: “Not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them.”

Works cannot be the source. The dead do not generate their own life, and the thing made does not make itself. Works also cannot be the ground of boasting, as if the saved man had supplied the deciding factor.

But watch the word walk. In verse 2 they walked in trespasses and sins according to the course of this world. In verse 10 they are created in Christ Jesus to walk in good works God prepared beforehand. Two walks—one belonging to the old humanity, one to the new. The change of walk is not the cause of the new creation. It is what the new creation looks like from outside.

So good works are neither the root of salvation nor a decorative extra fastened onto it. They are the appointed fruit and vocation of God’s workmanship. A person who has been made alive with Christ and left entirely unchanged is not a category Paul recognises.

Rescue and Vocation

Gather up what Paul says we are saved from: alienation from God’s life; personal trespasses and sins; the age and current of the world; the influence of the evil one; the self-directed desires of flesh and mind; the old humanity under wrath; and the boastful project of building a life before God out of one’s own works.

And what we are saved into: made alive with Christ; raised with Him; seated in Him; kept as continuing recipients of divine kindness; made an eternal display of grace; constituted His workmanship; created in Christ Jesus; and given a walk that was prepared before we arrived.

This is why salvation cannot be reduced here to forgiveness, indispensable as forgiveness is. Paul moves from death to life, from the world’s course to heaven’s position, from an Adamic identity to creation in Christ, and from one walk to another.

Union with Christ is the organising centre of the whole thing. Grace brings the sinner there. Faith receives Him. Being made alive is the life-giving dimension; being seated is the standing and identity dimension; new creation is the resulting existence; good works express it; and the ages to come display where it all came from.

“By grace are ye saved through faith” is true, and it is not a slogan about a mechanism. It is Paul’s compressed way of saying that dead people have been given the life of the risen Christ, for nothing, and are now expected to walk like it.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Death in sins, the course of this world, the rule of the evil one, the desires of flesh and mind, wrath, and boastful self-salvation
Saved into what?Life with Christ, resurrection standing, a heavenly identity, new creation, and a walk of prepared good works
Who saves?God, rich in mercy, moved by great love, through Christ Jesus
Through what means?By grace, through faith—not of yourselves, not of works
When?Accomplished in conversion, presently possessed in Christ, displayed through the ages to come
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. This may be the clearest compact description of salvation as union with Christ
