What Does “We Shall Be Saved by His Life” Mean?

Why Paul tells people who are already justified and reconciled that they “shall be saved”—and why that is reassurance, not doubt

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Romans 5:1–11
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Romans 5:1–11

Paul says something in this paragraph that has troubled careful readers: people who are already justified, already at peace with God, already reconciled, and already indwelt by the Spirit are told that they shall be saved.

Future tense. Twice.

If salvation is settled, why is it still coming? And if it is still coming, how settled is it?

The answer is that Paul is not expressing doubt. He is building an argument for confidence, and the future tense is load-bearing in that argument rather than a crack in it.

Everything That Is Already True

Count what Paul stacks up before he reaches the future verbs.

They have been justified by faith. They have peace with God. They have access by faith into this grace. They stand in it. God’s love has been poured into their hearts by the Holy Ghost, who has been given to them. They were reconciled to God by the death of His Son. And in verse 11 they have received the reconciliation—present possession, not pending application.

These are not almost-Christians waiting on a final qualifying act. Everything that establishes the relationship has already happened.

Notice too what justification does in Paul’s hands here. It is not merely a document filed somewhere outside the believer. Hostility gives way to peace. Distance gives way to access. Instability gives way to standing. Fear of shame gives way to hope of glory. A verdict, yes—but a verdict that puts a person somewhere.

What Is Still Coming

And yet Paul also names things not yet possessed.

The glory of God is still the object of hope. Tribulation is still doing its work of producing patience. Wrath is still a future judgment. The body is still mortal, in a world where death reigns. Resurrection has not happened to anybody but Christ.

So “we shall be saved” describes consummation. The justified will pass through judgment without falling under wrath. The reconciled will enter the completed life that Christ’s resurrection already displays.

Future salvation is not uncertain salvation. Making that certain is the entire purpose of the paragraph.

“Much More”

Paul’s logic runs from the harder case to the easier one, and it is worth laying out because the force of it is easy to skim past.

While we were without strength, Christ died for the ungodly. While we were yet sinners, God demonstrated His love. While we were enemies, we were reconciled by the death of His Son.

Then: “much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him.”

The argument is not about quantities of salvation. It is about confidence. If God acted savingly toward people who were powerless, guilty, and hostile, He is hardly going to abandon them now that they are justified, at peace, and reconciled. The hard part is behind Him.

Paul even pauses to observe how rare this kind of love is among people: scarcely would anyone die for a righteous man, though perhaps for a good man someone might dare it. That is the ceiling of human self-giving—and God went well past it, in the opposite direction, toward His enemies.

Saved from Wrath

Verse 9 names the peril outright. Wrath, in Romans, is God’s settled judicial opposition to sin; Paul has already spoken of “the day of wrath and revelation of the righteous judgment of God.”

The future verb looks toward that day. And the justified do not approach it as autonomous rebels trying to assemble a defence. They stand in Christ’s own right relationship to God, and they are saved through Him.

Verse 10 does not repeat an object. Instead it sets the Son’s death against the Son’s life, and the chapters that follow tell us what is at stake: death as a reigning power in Romans 5:12–21, dominion broken in Romans 6, mortal bodies made alive in Romans 8.

So the reconciled are saved from wrath—that is explicit—and from death’s final claim, which the surrounding argument makes plain.

“By His Life,” or “In His Life”?

The KJV has “we shall be saved by his life.” The Greek is en tē zōē autou, and the preposition can carry either sense.

“By” is a legitimate rendering, and it says something true. The living Christ acts for His people. His present intercession is a real part of how they are kept.

But the phrase should not be shrunk to Jesus is alive somewhere and continues to help from a distance. Paul’s argument over the next three chapters gives “His life” far more depth than that.

Romans 5:17 speaks of reigning in life through Jesus Christ. Romans 6 says believers died and rose with Him and now walk in newness of life. Romans 6:9 says death has no more dominion over the risen Christ. Romans 8:11 promises that the Spirit who raised Jesus will give life to mortal bodies.

Mediation and participation are not competing readings. The risen Son preserves His people precisely because their life and their future have been bound to His.

What Kind of Life Is His Risen Life?

This is worth stating carefully, because it is the theological centre of the passage.

The eternal Son possesses divine life in Himself; He receives existence from no one. In the incarnation He genuinely assumes mortal human life—He can suffer, and He can die, according to the humanity He has taken. His divine life does not cease, but the Person truly enters human death.

And in resurrection He re-enters embodied creation in a new order of human life. “Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over him.”

That is new. Before the resurrection, human life as such remained susceptible to death. The risen Christ possesses embodied human life beyond death’s reach, as the firstfruits of a new humanity.

To be saved in His life, then, is not merely to go on existing. It is to share, by union with Him, human resurrection life that death cannot sever from God.

Does This Make the Cross Insufficient?

No, and Paul is not setting an incomplete death against a life that finishes the job.

The death demonstrates God’s love, occurs for sinners, justifies by His blood, and reconciles enemies. The risen life vindicates the crucified Son, continues His living ministry, embodies death’s defeat, and carries the reconciled to salvation’s end.

These are two movements of one saving work. The death removes the enmity; the risen life is what the reconciled are brought into.

Reconciled to God

Verse 10 restates the same people in relational terms: “when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son.”

Sin here is not only a broken rule or an adverse verdict. It is hostility and alienation. And the direction is worth noticing: we were reconciled to God. Salvation does not merely improve how the sinner feels about God. God acts, through the death of His Son, to restore a relationship that was actually broken.

Which explains where the paragraph ends. Not with relief at wrath escaped, but with this: “we also joy in God through our Lord Jesus Christ.”

That is the destination. Not safety, but God Himself.

A note on the KJV’s next phrase, “by whom we have now received the atonement.” The Greek word is katallagē—reconciliation. The translators used atonement in its older sense of at-one-ment. Modern readers hear it as a technical term for the work done at the cross, which is essential but is not quite Paul’s point here. What believers have received is the reconciliation itself.

Can the Justified Still Fail?

Paul’s paragraph is engineered to produce assurance, not suspense, and the ground of the assurance is worth naming precisely.

It rests on God’s demonstrated love toward enemies, on Christ’s completed death, on justification already granted, on reconciliation already accomplished, on the Spirit already given, and on Christ’s present risen life.

Paul has a great deal to say elsewhere about warning, endurance, suffering, and obedience, and nothing here makes any of it unreal. But Romans 5 locates final confidence in God’s action and in the life of the risen Son—not in the believer’s success at maintaining an independent standing.

Once joined to Christ, a person’s future is not separable from Him. The old autonomous creature is not waiting in the wings to see whether it will be readmitted at the judgment.

If God reconciled His enemies through the death of His Son, He will not lose His friends in the life of the risen One.

At a Glance

Saved from what?The coming wrath, and death’s remaining claim on a mortal body
Saved into what?The completed life and glory secured in the risen Christ
Who saves?God, through Jesus Christ—both verbs are passive
On what grounds?Christ’s blood and death, already accomplished; His risen life, presently active
When?Future. Justification and reconciliation are already possessed; the consummation is not
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes, with the weight falling on its final horizon
