What Does “He Was Healed” Mean in Luke 8:36?

A man who lived in the tombs is found clothed and in his right mind — and the town asks Jesus to leave

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • Luke 8:26–39
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Luke 8:26–39

Luke describes the outcome twice, and the second time he uses the ordinary Greek word for saving:

They also which saw it told them by what means he that was possessed of the devils was healed.

Luke 8:36

Esōthē — he was saved. The KJV renders it healed, which is exactly right for what happened, and which once again conceals from an English reader that the verb is the same one behind he shall save his people from their sins.

What was he saved from? Luke tells us in unsparing detail before he tells us anything else.

The Condition

The man meets Jesus coming off the boat. Luke’s summary is a catalogue of losses.

He had devils a long time. He wore no clothes. He lived not in any house, but in the tombs. He was kept bound with chains and fetters, and broke the bands. He was driven into the wilderness. Mark adds that he cut himself with stones and cried out night and day.

Read as a list, it is the systematic dismantling of a human life: no clothing, no home, no community, no self-control, no safety, no rest. He is living among the dead because there is nowhere else left.

That is the peril. When Luke says he was saved, this is what he was saved from.

The Demons Know Exactly Who Has Arrived

The first words spoken are not the man’s. “What have I to do with thee, Jesus, thou Son of God most high? I beseech thee, torment me not.”

This is the strange feature of the scene. The correct Christological confession in this chapter comes from the demons. They know His name, His title, and that He has authority over their fate — Matthew’s account adds art thou come hither to torment us before the time?, which shows they know there is a schedule.

The disciples in the boat, one paragraph earlier, had just asked what manner of man is this? The demons could have told them.

That is not incidental. It is one of the clearest demonstrations in the Gospels that knowing the truth about Jesus and being saved are not the same thing. James will make the point flatly later: the devils also believe, and tremble.

Legion

“What is thy name?” The answer — Legion, because many devils were entered into him — is the only piece of information Jesus asks for, and it is about the scale of the problem.

A legion was around six thousand men. Whether the number is literal or the word is chosen for its sense of overwhelming force, the effect is the same: this man had not been slightly troubled.

They ask not to be sent into the deep, and then to go into the swine. Jesus permits it, the herd runs down the steep place and drowns, and the keepers run to tell the town.

Luke offers no explanation of why the request was granted, and the honest thing is not to supply one. What the passage does establish is that the destruction was real, visible, and immediately economically costly — which sets up what happens next.

Clothed, and in His Right Mind

“Then they went out to see what was done; and came to Jesus, and found the man, out of whom the devils were departed, sitting at the feet of Jesus, clothed, and in his right mind.”

Every phrase reverses an item from the earlier list. He was driven into the wilderness; now he is sitting. He wore no clothes; now he is clothed. He was out of his mind; now he is in his right mind. He lived among the dead; now he is at the feet of Jesus, which is where Luke seats disciples.

Luke is a careful writer, and this is a deliberate inventory of restoration. It is bodily and more than bodily: sanity, dignity, presence, and a posture of learning.

That is why the classification of this passage as bodily healing is right but slightly narrow. Nothing in the text says the man was converted in the theological sense — Luke does not use that language. But the result described is the restoration of a whole person, and it ends with him wanting to follow.

“And They Were Afraid”

Here is the sentence that should stop any reader who assumes a miracle produces faith:

Then the whole multitude of the country of the Gadarenes round about besought him to depart from them; for they were taken with great fear.

Luke 8:37

They had lived with this man’s screaming for years. They had tried chains. Now he is sane and dressed, sitting quietly, and they ask the man who did it to leave.

The pigs are part of it — a herd that size was a substantial loss, and the region evidently did not want to find out what else this authority might cost. But Luke names fear, not anger. They had encountered something they could not control, and they preferred the problem they were used to.

It is one of the bleakest verses in the Gospel, and it belongs in a study of salvation. Deliverance is not always welcome. It disturbs arrangements people have made.

The One Request Jesus Refuses

The delivered man asks to go with Him. It is the only thing he asks, and Jesus says no.

Return to thine own house, and shew how great things God hath done unto thee.

Then Luke adds the line that quietly makes the point: “And he went his way, and published throughout the whole city how great things Jesus had done unto him.”

Jesus said God. The man said Jesus. Luke lets the substitution stand without comment.

So the town that asked Him to leave was not left without a witness. They got one they could not dismiss — a man they had all known, walking around sane, telling them who had done it.

What This Passage Establishes

The saving verb here means deliverance from possession and the restoration of a wrecked life. It should not be inflated into a conversion narrative the text does not give, and it should not be shrunk into a merely physical healing either. Luke’s own list of results — clothed, seated, sane, sent — is broader than a body.

Three things worth carrying away.

Right belief about Jesus is not salvation; the demons had the doctrine exactly. Deliverance can be resisted by the people it happens near, for reasons that are about cost and control rather than evidence. And the man who most wanted to follow was sent home instead — because the first mission field in Gentile territory was a town that had just asked Jesus to go.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Demonic possession, and the ruin of a life — naked, homeless, chained, and living among graves
Saved into what?Sanity, clothing, a seat at Jesus’ feet, and a commission to go home and tell
Who saves?Jesus, by a word to what held the man
Through what means?No stated response from the man. He is delivered before he is asked anything
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Not stated. But Luke’s description of the result is more than physical
