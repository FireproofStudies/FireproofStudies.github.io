Saved in Context

“Who Hath Saved Us, and Called Us”

Not according to our works, but according to His own purpose and grace given before the world began

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • 2 Timothy 1:8–12
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › 2 Timothy 1:8–12

Paul is urging Timothy not to be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul’s chains, and he steadies him with a sentence about who God is and what God has done. The saving verb anchors it.

The Ground of the Appeal

Be not thou therefore ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me his prisoner: but be thou partaker of the afflictions of the gospel according to the power of God; who hath saved us, and called us with an holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began.

2 Timothy 1:8–9

Tou sōsantos hēmas — who has saved us. Paul grounds his call to courage in the character of the God who saved. Timothy can face affliction without shame because the salvation behind him rests on something unshakeable — not his performance, but God’s own purpose.

The sentence is one of the clearest in the New Testament on the source of salvation, and it moves in two beats: what it is not based on, and what it is.

Not According to Our Works

Paul rules out human works with the same flat clarity found in Titus and Ephesians: not according to our works. Salvation did not answer to anything Timothy or Paul or anyone else produced. This is stated not as a fine theological point but as the reason a man can be brave — if salvation rested on works, it could be lost by failure, and shame under pressure would be a live danger. Because it does not, it cannot.

According to His Own Purpose and Grace

The positive ground is stated in two words joined together: his own purpose and grace. And Paul dates the grace: it was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began.

That phrase reaches back before creation. The grace that saved Timothy was not improvised in response to his life; it was given in Christ before the world began, and only manifested in time. Paul draws the contrast in the next verse: this purpose, hidden through ages past, is now made manifest by the appearing of our Saviour Jesus Christ. The grace is ancient; its appearing is recent. What was purposed before the world was shown at the coming of Christ.

This is where a study can rest without straining. Paul is not laying out a system of eternal decrees for their own sake; he is telling a frightened young minister that his salvation is older than the world and does not depend on him. The pastoral use governs the doctrine.

Life and Immortality Brought to Light

Paul names what the Saviour’s appearing accomplished: Christ hath abolished death, and hath brought life and immortality to light through the gospel.

Death is not merely postponed but abolished — stripped of its finality — and life and immortality, previously in shadow, are brought to light. The gospel is the lamp that reveals them. The salvation given in eternity and manifested in Christ has, as its content, the undoing of death and the illumination of a life that does not end. For a man tempted to shame under threat of death, this is the exact comfort needed: the gospel he might be ashamed of is the one that abolished the thing his opponents could threaten him with.

“I Know Whom I Have Believed”

Paul then makes it personal, and models the confidence he is asking of Timothy: “For the which cause I also suffer these things: nevertheless I am not ashamed: for I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day.”

Paul is not ashamed — there is the very thing he asked of Timothy, lived out in his own chains. And his confidence is not in himself but in the One he has trusted: I know whom I have believed. He has committed something to Christ and is persuaded Christ is able to keep it against that day. The salvation that began in eternal grace is guarded, to the end, by the same faithful hand.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense: God has saved us and called us, and the whole weight of the sentence falls on the ground of that salvation — not works, but God’s own purpose and grace, given in Christ before the world began and manifested at His appearing, with death abolished and life brought to light.

Its contribution to the gateway is the reach of the grace and the courage it produces. Salvation is older than creation and independent of human works, which is precisely why it can steady a minister facing affliction and death. Paul asks Timothy to be unashamed, and gives him the reason: the God who saved him did so by an eternal grace, abolished death, and is able to keep what is committed to Him against that day.

Through what means?Grace given in Christ before the world, manifested at His appearing
