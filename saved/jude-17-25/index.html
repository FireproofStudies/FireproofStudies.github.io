How Can Believers Save Others in Jude 23?

Jude commands ordinary Christians to save people by snatching them out of fire—without any of them becoming a savior

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 9 min read • Jude 17–25
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Jude 17–25

“And others save with fear, pulling them out of the fire.”

Save is an imperative here, and it is plural. Jude is telling an ordinary congregation to go and save people.

That should sit uncomfortably for a moment before it is explained away. Jude knows perfectly well that God is the Savior—he ends this letter praising “the only wise God our Saviour” as the One able to keep them from falling. He gives the command anyway, in the same paragraph.

Which means the New Testament is willing to say two things that seem to compete, and to say them a few lines apart.

What Jude Is Responding To

Most of this short letter exposes teachers who had slipped into the Christian community, distorted grace into permission, followed corrupt desire, and denied Christ’s authority.

The danger they created was not only to themselves. People were listening to them.

So verses 17–25 answer the practical question: what should the faithful actually do? And Jude gives three movements that belong together.

Keep yourselves—building on the faith, praying in the Holy Ghost, remaining in God’s love, looking for Christ’s mercy unto eternal life.

Rescue others—with discerning mercy, urgent intervention, and moral vigilance.

Trust God to keep—entrusting the whole church to the One able to present them faultless before His glory.

Notice that the second is placed between the other two. Believers do not keep themselves by withdrawing from everyone in danger, and they do not attempt rescues while neglecting their own dependence on God.

Snatching

The verb behind “pulling” is harpazō—to seize, to snatch, to take by force and quickly. It is the word used for the Spirit catching Philip away, and for believers being caught up to meet the Lord.

It is not a word for gentle concern expressed at a distance. Jude pictures someone reaching into a dangerous place and pulling a person out of it.

And the command is plural. This is not assigned to apostles or to church officers. Rescue belongs to the shared life of the beloved.

What the Fire Is

Fire has already appeared in this letter. Sodom and the cities around it are “set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire.” Jude has spoken of judgment on the ungodly and of blackness of darkness reserved forever.

So the fire in verse 23 carries a final edge. But the rescue happens now. Jude does not imagine Christians retrieving people from the judgment after it falls. They pull people out of the error and corruption that are carrying them toward it.

That distinction matters pastorally. Anyone who can still be warned, corrected, and restored is not to be written off. Confusion, doubt, entanglement in sin, and even serious doctrinal error must not be treated lightly—and they must not be diagnosed hastily as final, irreversible rejection of Christ.

Can One Christian Really Save Another?

Instrumentally, and the New Testament says so more often than most readers notice.

Paul adapts his ministry that he might “save some.” A believing spouse may save an unbelieving one. Timothy’s perseverance in life and doctrine will save both himself and his hearers. James says whoever turns a sinner from the error of his way “shall save a soul from death.” And Jude tells the whole congregation to save people from the fire.

These do not confuse levels of agency; they distinguish them.

God reveals, draws, convicts, gives life, joins the believer to Christ, and remains the source of every saving blessing. Human beings speak, embody, correct, plead, warn, pray, remove obstacles, and intervene where someone is being destroyed. The Spirit may apply the gospel through those actions to another person’s will.

The instrument is not the source. But the instrument is real—and “only God can save” must never become a reason for declining the actions through which God has chosen to rescue people.

Does Snatching Permit Coercion?

No, and the distinction needs stating carefully, because urgent language has been misused to justify a great deal.

No Christian can seize another person’s will, manufacture repentance, or produce saving faith. Manipulation, humiliation, threats meant to control, and abusive spiritual authority all contradict the mercy that governs this very passage.

But refusing coercion is not the same as passivity. Snatching may well include naming a destructive lie plainly; telling someone where his present path ends; bringing hidden corruption into the light; removing a vulnerable person from a predatory influence; setting protective boundaries within the church; continuing to plead when a comfortable answer would be easier; and restoring a repentant wanderer instead of leaving him under his shame.

The aim is rescue, not control. The endangered person must finally turn from self-rule and entrust himself to Christ. Human intervention serves that surrender and cannot substitute for it.

Why “with Fear”?

Fear here recognises two things at once: how serious the judgment is, and how vulnerable the rescuer is.

The KJV attaches fear directly to the saving. Some readings of the disputed Greek attach it to mercy shown to a further group. Either way it guards against casual engagement with corruption.

Part of it is reverence. The danger is not a pastoral problem to be managed; it is divine judgment. Warnings should therefore tell the truth about where rebellion leads rather than softening it into an inconvenience.

And part of it is humility. Paul gives the same balance: restore such a one in the spirit of meekness, “considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted.” Rescuers are not fireproof. They go in as people who still need grace themselves.

Fear must not crowd out mercy, and mercy must not deny the danger. Jude asks for both in the same sentence.

The Spotted Garment

“Hating even the garment spotted by the flesh.”

The word refers to the tunic worn next to the skin. Jude is picturing corruption so defiling that whatever touches it is stained—the closest layer, the one hardest to keep clean.

The image may echo Zechariah 3, where Joshua the high priest stands in filthy garments and is called “a brand plucked out of the fire.” The same chapter, the same two images.

And note precisely what is to be hated: the stain, not the person. Biblical mercy does not require pretending that corruption is harmless. A believer can move toward someone in compassion while refusing any fellowship with the thing destroying him.

That is the balance the passage is built on. Holiness without mercy abandons people in the fire. Mercy without holiness climbs in beside them and calls the burning warmth.

Different People Need Different Things

Jude 22–23 has one of the most complicated textual traditions in the letter, and the differences are worth disclosing.

The KJV and Textus Receptus give two responses: have compassion, making a difference; and save others with fear. The modern critical editions generally yield three: have mercy on those who doubt; save others, snatching them from fire; and have mercy on still others with fear. The Tyndale House text reads reprove or correct where most editions read have mercy in verse 22.

The variations affect whether Jude names two groups or three, and where mercy, correction, and fear each land. What no reading removes is the command to save, the human agents, the urgent snatching, the fire, or the demand for vigilance. Verification against Hodges–Farstad is still pending for this project.

And on any reading, the pastoral point survives: treating everyone identically is not necessarily mercy. The doubting may need patient clarification. The deceived may need direct correction. The deeply endangered may need urgent warning. The defiant may need boundaries that protect others while still telling the truth about repentance. Discernment asks what response actually serves this person’s rescue.

Keeping, in Four Directions

Jude puts several kinds of keeping into one paragraph, and the arrangement is deliberate.

False teachers are reserved for judgment. Believers keep themselves in the love of God. Believers help rescue others from destruction. And God is able to keep them from falling and present them faultless.

None of these compete. Believers keep themselves by continuing in the faith, praying in the Spirit, and looking to Christ’s mercy. They help keep one another by mercy, truth, warning, and restoration. And underneath all of that human faithfulness, God is the one doing the keeping.

Divine preservation does not make human ministry unnecessary. Mutual rescue is one of the ways the keeping God keeps His people—which is why a letter that ends in a doxology about God’s ability to hold onto us contains, four verses earlier, an order to go and pull somebody out of a fire.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Destructive error, fleshly corruption, and the judgment pictured as fire
Saved into what?Truth, restoration, and the mercy of Christ that leads to eternal life
Who saves?God, through Christ, by the Spirit. Believers are genuine instruments
Through what means?Discerning mercy, correction, warning, urgent intervention—and prayer
Does “snatching” permit coercion?No. Urgency is not domination, and no one can produce another’s repentance
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Indirectly. The two destinations Jude names are final ones
