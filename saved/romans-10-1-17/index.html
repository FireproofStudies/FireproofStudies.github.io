What Must I Believe and Confess to Be Saved?

Why believing in the heart, confessing with the mouth, and calling on the Lord are one surrender and not three requirements

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Romans 10:1–17
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Romans 10:1–17

Few passages have been used more often at the end of an evangelistic appeal than Romans 10:9, and few have been made to carry more anxiety. If salvation depends on confessing with the mouth, what about the believer too frightened to speak? If it depends on believing in the heart, how sure does the believing have to be? People have counted their words and examined their sincerity and come away from this verse less certain than when they arrived.

Paul is doing something else entirely. Read from verse 1, and the chapter is not a checklist for closing a decision. It is a description of what happens when God brings a finished work near to people who had been trying to build their own.

Zeal Is Not the Problem

Paul opens with his own heart: “my heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved.” He is not standing at a distance classifying other people’s unbelief. He wants them saved and he prays for it.

And he is clear that their problem is not irreligion. They have zeal—he says so plainly, and he grants it as real. They have Scripture, covenant history, moral seriousness, and genuine effort. What the zeal lacks is knowledge, and what the knowledge lacks is submission. “They being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and going about to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted themselves unto the righteousness of God.”

That is the axis of the whole chapter. Not religion against irreligion, but a righteousness you establish against a righteousness you receive.

Self-righteousness, in Paul’s account, is not merely a miscalculation about how salvation works. It is a refusal. It insists that the self will determine, achieve, and hold its own standing before God. Which is why the remedy is described as submitting.

Nobody Has to Climb

Paul then does something remarkable with Deuteronomy 30. Moses had told Israel that God’s commandment was not far off in heaven or across the sea, requiring somebody to fetch it. Paul reads that nearness in the light of what Christ has done.

Do not say in your heart, “Who shall ascend into heaven?”—that is, to bring Christ down. He has already come. Do not say, “Who shall descend into the deep?”—that is, to bring Christ up from the dead. God has already raised Him.

The point is that the saving work is not waiting behind a human achievement nobody could perform. There is no ladder to climb and no abyss to enter. “The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach.”

The gospel is not instructions for accomplishing Christ’s work. It is the announcement that God has done it, in the crucified and risen Lord, together with the summons to trust Him.

And this is why Paul can say that “Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth.” The word carries the sense of goal as much as termination. Christ is not the announcement that God’s moral will never mattered. He is what the law was pointing toward, and the place where the standing the law could never manufacture is actually found.

Believing and Confessing Are Not Two Conditions

Now the famous verses—and notice the order.

Verse 9: confess with the mouth, believe in the heart, and thou shalt be saved. Verse 10: with the heart man believeth, and with the mouth confession is made.

Paul reverses them within two sentences. If he were laying out a required sequence, that reversal would wreck it. He is not describing steps. He is describing one thing from the inside and the outside.

The heart, in Scripture, is not the seat of the emotions as we use the word. It is the inner person entire—understanding, desire, allegiance, will. To believe in the heart that God raised Jesus is not to concede that a historical event probably occurred. It is to entrust your standing, your allegiance, and your future to the One God raised.

The mouth says out loud what the heart has done: Jesus is Lord. Confession is not an incantation whose correct pronunciation produces new life. It is the public voice of an inward allegiance. Real confession hands over the throne to the One it names.

Which means two pastoral things need saying plainly. A person who cannot speak is not thereby shut out of salvation. And a new believer whose public courage has faltered has not thereby invalidated his faith. Paul is describing the nature and direction of faith, not installing a speech requirement.

What Calling on the Name Actually Means

Paul quotes Joel: “whosoever shall call upon the name of the LORD shall be saved”—and applies it, without apology, to Jesus. The Lord Jesus is not a messenger directing us toward some other saving name. He is the Lord on whom Jew and Greek alike call.

To call on the name is not to request assistance while keeping control of the outcome. A name in Scripture stands for the person—his identity, authority, character, and rightful claim. Calling says something like: Lord, I cannot establish myself. I depend on You. I belong to You. Save me.

So calling is faith turned Godward as appeal and dependence. Paul confirms the relationship in the next verse: how shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? Calling is not a further step bolted onto faith. It is faith addressing its object.

This is also the answer to the question people ask about the sinner’s prayer. A prayer may honestly voice the call of faith, and often does. But Paul supplies no script, and he never says that reciting words produces life. The same mouth can say “Lord” without surrender—Jesus warned about exactly that. Romans 10 binds the mouth to the heart and the calling to the believing, which protects the invitation from both directions: salvation is not earned by performing a flawless conversion ritual, and it is not promised to empty recitation.

Faith Comes by Hearing

Paul then runs the chain backwards, and it is worth following slowly: calling depends on believing, believing on hearing, hearing on preaching, preaching on sending.

The logic is not bureaucratic. Faith requires an object. Nobody can trust a Christ who has never been set before him, and nobody calls on a Lord he has not heard of. This is why sending and preaching are not optional extras to the doctrine of salvation—they are how the risen Lord addresses people.

But “faith cometh by hearing” does not mean that audible sound mechanically produces belief. Paul says so himself in the same breath: “they have not all obeyed the gospel,” and then Isaiah’s ache, “Lord, who hath believed our report?” Hearing establishes a real encounter and a real summons. The Spirit works through that proclaimed truth to convict and draw. The hearer may resist it or may yield.

Faith comes by hearing because hearing supplies the Christ that faith trusts. Faith does not invent its object or rest on its own intensity.

A Note on the Text

The KJV ends verse 17 with “the word of God,” following the Textus Receptus, and Robinson–Pierpont agrees. The Tyndale House edition and the principal critical texts read “the word of Christ.” Verification against Hodges–Farstad is still pending for this project.

The difference touches emphasis rather than argument. On one reading, hearing comes by God’s utterance; on the other, by the proclamation concerning Christ, or by Christ’s own address through the gospel. The preached gospel is in fact both—God’s Word, and the Word that carries Christ’s summons.

Does “Whosoever” Really Mean Anyone?

Yes, and Paul makes the scope explicit: “there is no difference between the Jew and the Greek: for the same Lord over all is rich unto all that call upon him.”

The promise does not dissolve Israel and the nations into an abstraction. It removes ancestry, law-possession, religious pedigree, and moral achievement as grounds of privilege. The same Lord answers everyone who calls.

Nor does “whosoever” mean that everyone is saved regardless of faith. It means that no one who truly calls on Christ is turned away for who his parents were, what his history has been, or how far outside the covenant he started.

Lord, I Cannot Save Myself

Gather what Paul says this salvation delivers people from: the exhausting project of establishing their own righteousness; ignorance of God’s; the pride that refuses to submit; unbelief toward the gospel; sin and the condemnation Romans has already established; shame when God’s verdict is finally revealed.

And what it delivers them into: God’s righteousness in Christ; a standing that will not be ashamed; the riches of a Lord who answers all who call; an allegiance openly owned; a people where Jew and Gentile come the same way; and a salvation secured in the risen Christ.

The heart trusts. The mouth yields allegiance. The whole person appeals to Jesus as Lord. That is one surrender described from three angles—and its content is roughly this: Lord, I cannot save myself. I am Yours. Save me.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Self-established righteousness, unbelief, self-rule, sin, shame, and final judgment
Saved into what?God’s righteousness in Christ, an unashamed standing, the riches of the one Lord, and final deliverance
Who saves?God, through the risen Lord Jesus
Through what means?The sent and preached gospel is heard; the sinner believes, confesses, and calls upon Christ
When?The saving relationship begins in conversion and carries an assured final horizon
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. Paul joins righteousness, faith, allegiance, proclamation, and final salvation
