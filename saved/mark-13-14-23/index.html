“No Flesh Should Be Saved” — Mark’s Account

Mark’s telling of the shortened days, where the Lord Himself is named as the one who cuts the tribulation short

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Mark 13:14–23
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Mark 13:14–23

Mark tells the shortened days as Matthew does, and the saving verb carries the same sense — survival, not final salvation. But Mark words the sentence to put the emphasis somewhere slightly different.

And except that the Lord had shortened those days, no flesh should be saved: but for the elect’s sake, whom he hath chosen, he hath shortened the days.

Mark 13:20

Esōthē — should be saved, in the sense of coming through alive. The tribulation is severe enough that, unchecked, it would leave no living thing. To be saved is to survive it.

Mark Names the Agent

Matthew writes that the days shall be shortened, a passive that leaves the actor unstated though obvious. Mark makes the actor explicit twice: except that the Lord had shortened those days, and for the elect’s sake … he hath shortened the days.

The effect is to place the whole limiting of the catastrophe squarely in the Lord’s hand. The disaster does not exhaust itself, and it is not curbed by human ingenuity or resistance. It is shortened because the Lord shortens it. Mark leaves no room to read the survival of any flesh as an accident of circumstance.

And Mark adds a clause to the elect: whom he hath chosen. The choosing is stated as the ground of the shortening. The days are cut short for the sake of a people the Lord has already chosen — their existence, and His prior commitment to them, is why the disaster is bounded.

The Same Two-Register Discourse

As in Matthew, this occurrence sits in a discourse that also uses the saving word in its final sense — the enduring that ends in salvation. The two meanings live a few sentences apart, and the difference is set by the peril each addresses. Where the danger is death within history, saved means survival. Where the danger is falling away before the end, saved means final deliverance.

Mark, like Matthew, felt no need to distinguish them with different vocabulary. The context does the distinguishing. A reader who insists the word must mean the same thing throughout will flatten one sense into the other and lose what Mark is saying.

The Deception That Frames It

The shortened days are again set against false rescuers. “If any man shall say to you, Lo, here is Christ; or, lo, he is there; believe him not: for false Christs and false prophets shall rise, and shall shew signs and wonders, to seduce, if it were possible, even the elect.”

Then the summary warning: “But take ye heed: behold, I have foretold you all things.”

The survival God grants is not a reward for finding the right refuge or the right claimant to follow. It is the Lord’s own shortening of the days. In a period desperate enough to threaten all flesh, the pull toward any announced deliverer is strong, and Jesus arms His people against it in advance: the deliverance that matters comes from the Lord’s hand, not from the loudest voice promising rescue.

What the Word Means Here

Preservation of physical life through a historical tribulation, granted by the Lord for the sake of His chosen people. Not the new birth, not final salvation — Mark reserves no hidden second meaning here. The peril is bodily death in the catastrophe, and to be saved is to be brought through it alive.

Its place in the gateway is the same as its Matthean parallel: a clear instance of the saving word meaning survival, held deliberately beside the same word’s fuller senses so that neither is collapsed into the other.
