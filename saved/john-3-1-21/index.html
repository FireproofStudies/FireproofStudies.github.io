What Does “That the World through Him Might Be Saved” Mean?

How new birth by the Spirit, belief in the lifted-up Son, and rescue from condemnation belong together in one saving work

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • John 3:1–21
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › John 3:1–21

Nicodemus comes at night with a compliment, and it is a true one. “Rabbi, we know that thou art a teacher come from God: for no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him.” He has looked at the evidence and drawn the right conclusion. He is a ruler of the Jews, a teacher of Israel, a serious man taking a serious risk to have this conversation at all.

Jesus does not thank him for it. He tells him he must be born again.

That is the shock of the passage, and everything else in it follows from that moment. A man who already believes the right things about Jesus is told that he cannot so much as see the kingdom of God without a birth he cannot arrange for himself. Whatever being saved means in John 3, it is not the acquiring of correct opinions about Christ. Nicodemus already has those.

The Birth No One Can Arrange

The word Jesus uses—anōthen—can mean again or from above, and John almost certainly intends both. Nicodemus hears only the first and pictures the absurdity: a grown man climbing back into the womb. Jesus’ reply supplies the second sense and settles it. This birth is of the Spirit.

Then comes the principle that governs the rest: “That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit.” A source produces life after its own kind. Natural birth produces natural human life, and no amount of refining it will turn it into something else. New birth is not religious flesh improved, educated, disciplined, or made sincere. It is life from a different origin altogether.

Birth is a striking image precisely because no one contributes to his own. You did not assist at your first birth, and you cannot assist at this one. A person does not generate his own life, supply his own beginning, or inherit the kingdom through ancestry—which is exactly the assumption a teacher of Israel would have carried into the room.

Water and Spirit

“Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God.” Jesus then rebukes Nicodemus for not recognizing it: “Art thou a master of Israel, and knowest not these things?” The rebuke tells us where to look. This is not a new idea Jesus is introducing; it is a promise Nicodemus should have known.

Ezekiel had joined the very same elements:

Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean … A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you … And I will put my spirit within you.

Ezekiel 36:25–27

Water and Spirit there are not two separate events. They are cleansing and renewal described together—one promised work of God upon a people who cannot cleanse or renew themselves. That is almost certainly what Jesus means, and it fits the argument he is making.

Two other readings are worth naming and setting aside. The water is unlikely to mean natural birth, since “flesh” in the very next verse already says that without needing water to say it. And the water is not an independent sacramental cause. Christian baptism will embody and announce the believer’s answer to God, and it matters greatly—but water does not manufacture Christ’s life. The Spirit gives the life. The prophetic water declares the cleansing that comes with it.

Does the Wind Mean the Spirit Regenerates People before They Believe?

The wind image invites the question, and it deserves a careful answer rather than a slogan, because a great deal of pastoral confusion lives here.

Jesus says the wind blows where it pleases; you hear it, but you cannot say where it comes from or where it goes. The point of the comparison is the Spirit’s heavenly origin, His freedom, and His perceptible effects. No one commands the wind, manufactures it, or traces it by sight—and yet no one doubts it when the trees move. New birth is God’s work, not a human technique, and it cannot be produced by method.

What the image does not do is establish an interval. The same discourse that says a man must be born of the Spirit says, four times over, that whoever believes in the lifted-up Son has everlasting life and is not condemned. Jesus never describes a person who has already been given new life while remaining an unbeliever, waiting to catch up.

The Spirit’s work certainly precedes the encounter—He draws, enlightens, convicts, and makes the truth pierce. But preparation is not birth. In conversion the sinner believes, and the Spirit gives life. Faith does not regenerate anyone, and the Spirit does not believe on anyone’s behalf. The two actions meet in one moment without collapsing into the same action.

The Serpent in the Wilderness

Jesus reaches back to a strange episode that Nicodemus would have known well. Israel was dying of snakebite under judgment. God told Moses to raise a bronze serpent on a pole, and the dying who looked at it lived.

Notice what looking did and did not do. It did not cure anyone; the healing was God’s. It did not earn the cure; a dying man has nothing to trade. It did not even require understanding how the thing worked. Looking simply relied on the provision God had appointed, in the direction God had appointed it.

“Even so must the Son of man be lifted up.” In John, the lifting up is the cross, and beyond it, the exaltation. The saving provision God has appointed is not an idea, a moral program, or a religious achievement. It is a Person, given and raised up in full view of the dying.

That is why belief here means more than agreement. Nicodemus agrees already. John describes believing through a whole family of personal actions—receiving Christ, coming to Him, hearing His voice, entering through Him, following, abiding. It reaches the will. And John 3 ends exactly there: men are not condemned because the evidence was unavailable, but because “men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.” They will not come, because coming means being seen.

Does God Really Love and Intend to Save the Whole World?

“For God so loved the world.” The world in this passage is not an attractive remainder of humanity that merited the affection. It is the world of verse 19—the one that loves darkness and hides from the light. That is what God loved, and that is what He gave His only begotten Son for.

Two instincts pull against the sentence, and both should be resisted. The first quietly shrinks world until it means only those who will finally believe, which the verse does not say and the following verses do not support. The second expands it into a promise that all will finally be saved, which verse 18 flatly denies: “he that believeth not is condemned already.”

John holds both truths without apology. God’s love and saving mission genuinely face the world. Eternal life belongs to whoever believes in the Son. Unbelief does not expose some deficiency in what Christ provided; it is a refusal of light that has actually arrived.

What the Passage Says We Are Saved From

The ruin here is stated with unusual fullness, and it is worth naming each part.

There is perishing, the destiny of those who do not have life. There is condemnation already—not a risk hovering in the future but a verdict presently in force, which is why Jesus says the unbeliever “is condemned already.” There is exclusion from the kingdom, because flesh cannot produce the life the kingdom requires. There is darkness, chosen and loved because it conceals. And underneath all of it is unbelief toward the Son, the refusal of the one gift that answers the rest.

Against that, the result: born from above; able to see and enter the kingdom; no longer condemned; brought out into the light; given everlasting life in the Son.

That last phrase deserves care. Eternal life in John is not primarily a quantity of time beginning after death. It is life from God, in relationship with His Son, possessed now and destined for resurrection. “He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life”—present tense, already.

New Birth and Faith Are Not a Conveyor Belt

It is tempting to arrange all of this into a sequence and then argue about the order. John gives relationships rather than a timetable.

The Father loves and gives the Son. The Son must be lifted up. The Spirit gives birth from above. The sinner believes in Him. The believer has eternal life and stands uncondemned. Divine action precedes, surrounds, and makes possible the whole encounter; human faith neither causes Christ’s mission nor produces the Spirit’s life.

But the discourse never presents new birth as a hidden condition an unbeliever might already possess. Birth by the Spirit and belief in the Son are the divine and the human sides of one entrance into one life. Trying to prise them apart and stack them in order produces a great deal of anxiety and very little light.

Into the Kingdom, through the Son

When John says God sent His Son so that the world through Him might be saved, saved means brought out of perishing, out of a condemnation already standing, and out of a darkness men prefer—into the kingdom, into the light, and into life that begins now and does not end.

The Father gives. The Son is lifted up. The Spirit gives birth. The sinner looks, and lives.

Nicodemus came at night, knowing a great deal about Jesus. What he needed was not more information but a birth from above—and so does everyone else who comes to this passage hoping that being right about Christ is the same thing as belonging to Him.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Perishing, a condemnation already in force, chosen darkness, and exclusion from God’s kingdom
Saved into what?New birth, entrance into the kingdom, freedom from condemnation, and eternal life in the Son
Who saves?The Father, through the Son He sent and lifted up, by the life-giving Spirit
Through what means?The Son given and lifted up; the sinner believing in Him
When?Life begins in conversion and answers perishing and judgment forever
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. Few passages show the whole saving encounter so fully
