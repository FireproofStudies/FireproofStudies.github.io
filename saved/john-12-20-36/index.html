“Father, Save Me From This Hour”

Not a mocker’s taunt but Jesus’ own prayer — contemplated, and set aside, for the very purpose the hour was given

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • John 12:20–36
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › John 12:20–36

Every other place the ironic saving verb appears at the cross, it is on the lips of mockers demanding that Jesus rescue Himself. Here it is different in kind. The prayer for escape is Jesus’ own — contemplated aloud, and deliberately not completed.

The Hour Has Come

Greeks have come asking to see Jesus, and their arrival triggers an announcement: “The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified.” But the glory Jesus names is not what anyone expects. He explains it with a seed:

Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.

John 12:24

The paradox of the whole ironic cluster, in an image. A seed kept safe — never buried — abideth alone; it stays a single seed. Only a seed that falls into the ground and dies bears a harvest. Jesus is describing His own death, and immediately He turns it into the law of discipleship: “He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal.” The same paradox as the Synoptic save your life and lose it — here in John’s vocabulary of loving and hating, keeping and losing.

The Prayer Contemplated

Then, uniquely, Jesus voices the prayer for escape Himself:

Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour.

John 12:27

Pater, sōson me ek tēs hōras tautēs — Father, save me from this hour. It is the saving verb, and for once it is Jesus asking. His soul is troubled — John does not hide the recoil; this is the closest thing in his Gospel to the agony the others record in Gethsemane. The prayer for deliverance from the hour rises to His lips.

And then He sets it down in the same breath: but for this cause came I unto this hour. The prayer is contemplated and relinquished. He will not ask to be saved from the hour, because the hour is the very thing He came for. To be delivered from it would be to abandon the purpose of His coming.

This is the crucial difference from the mockery scenes. There, others demand that He save Himself, and He silently refuses. Here, He frames the request Himself, holds it up, and declines to complete it — not because He could not pray it, but because to pray it would be to undo His mission. The escape the mockers will later jeer at Him for not taking, He here considers and rejects on His own, before anyone taunts Him.

What He Prays Instead

Having refused save me from this hour, He prays the opposite:

Father, glorify thy name. Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again.

John 12:28

He asks not for rescue but for the Father’s name to be glorified — and the glorifying, He has already said, comes precisely through the seed falling into the ground to die. The Father answers audibly, the only such voice in John’s Gospel outside the crowd’s hearing, confirming that the hour is not a defeat to be escaped but a glory to be accomplished.

Jesus reads the moment plainly: “Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out. And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me.” John adds the interpretation — this he said, signifying what death he should die. The lifting up is the cross. The drawing of all men is accomplished not by escaping the hour but by being lifted up in it.

The Refused Deliverance and the Real One

Set this beside the study on Hebrews 5, where the Son offered up prayers … unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard. The two passages fit together precisely. In Hebrews the Son prays to the One able to save Him from death and is heard — not by being spared death, but by being brought through it. Here in John He declines to pray save me from this hour at all, because the hour is the point.

Between them they define what the saving of the Son looks like. It is never escape from the cross. It is either the prayer refused (John) or the prayer answered by resurrection rather than exemption (Hebrews). Either way, the deliverance runs through the hour, not around it — and it is exactly the deliverance the mockers at the cross will insist He should have taken instead.

What the Word Means Here

Save here means deliverance from the hour of the cross — physical escape from death — contemplated by Jesus Himself and set aside. Unlike the mockery scenes, the request is His own, which makes the refusal more revealing: He wanted, in His troubled soul, what the escape would give, and He would not have it, because the hour was His purpose.

Its contribution to the gateway is to show the refused deliverance from the inside. The mockers demand an escape and are ignored; here Jesus frames the escape as a prayer and declines to finish it. The grain of wheat must fall and die, or it abides alone. The salvation of the world was accomplished by a Saviour who could have prayed His way out of the hour, and chose instead to be lifted up in it — and to draw all men to Himself by the very death He would not escape.
