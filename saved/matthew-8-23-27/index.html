What Does “Lord, Save Us” Mean in Matthew 8:25?

The disciples are not asking to be forgiven. They are asking not to drown — and the answer reveals who was in the boat

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Matthew 8:23–27
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Matthew 8:23–27

“Lord, save us: we perish.”

It is one of the shortest prayers in the New Testament, and one of the most misquoted. It gets preached as though the disciples were asking about their souls. They were not. They were asking not to drown.

The verb is sōson — the aorist imperative of the ordinary Greek word for saving, the same verb Matthew used seven chapters earlier when the angel said Jesus would save His people from their sins. Here it means: get us out of this water.

That is worth sitting with rather than hurrying past, because this passage is one of the clearest demonstrations in the Gospels that the word has to be read in its own setting. Matthew is not being loose. He is using an ordinary word in an ordinary way, about men in a boat that is filling up.

What Actually Happened

Matthew’s placement is deliberate. Jesus has just been dealing with would-be followers — one who promised to follow anywhere, another who wanted to bury his father first — and the paragraph ends with a summons to leave and come. Then: “And when he was entered into a ship, his disciples followed him.”

They followed Him into a storm.

The word Matthew uses for the tempest is seismos, the word for an earthquake. A shaking of the sea. These were fishermen who had worked this lake for years; several of them had grown up on it. Whatever came down on them that night was outside their experience, and the boat was already being covered by the waves.

And Jesus was asleep.

The Prayer of Men Who Have Run Out of Options

There is no theology in what they say. There is no confession, no vow, no bargaining. Three words in Greek and a reason: Lord, save — we are perishing.

It is worth noticing what kind of prayer this is, because it is the kind most people actually pray. It is not composed. It comes out of someone at the end of his own competence, and it is addressed to the only person present who might be able to do something.

Matthew does not criticise them for praying it. Jesus answers it.

What He questions is something else.

“Why Are Ye Fearful, O Ye of Little Faith?”

He asks the question before He calms the storm, which is the detail most readings miss. The rebuke comes to men still in a sinking boat.

And notice the diagnosis. Not no faith — little faith. They had enough faith to wake Him and ask. What they lacked was the confidence that the One in the boat with them could not be lost at sea.

That distinction matters pastorally, and it is easy to get wrong in both directions. Jesus does not treat fear as proof of unbelief; He treats it as evidence of faith that has not yet grown into what it knows. These men are not condemned. They are asked a question they will spend the rest of the Gospel answering.

The Rescue

“Then he arose, and rebuked the winds and the sea; and there was a great calm.”

He rebukes them. It is the word used for silencing a demon — direct address to something that has no business behaving this way. And the result is not merely that the wind drops but that the water goes flat, which is not what happens after a squall. Waves outlive the wind that raised them. This calm is immediate and total.

The men are saved in the plainest sense available. They do not drown. Their bodies are on the shore at Gadara in the next paragraph.

The Question They Ask Afterwards

“But the men marvelled, saying, What manner of man is this, that even the winds and the sea obey him!”

This is why Matthew tells the story, and it is the reason a narrow rescue belongs in a project about salvation.

The disciples asked to be saved from drowning, and they were. But the way it happened put a question in front of them that their request had not anticipated. They had appealed to Jesus as a man who might do something. They got an answer that only the Creator could give — because in the Psalms it is the LORD who stills the noise of the seas, who commands and raises the stormy wind, who makes the storm a calm so that the waves are still.

A carpenter had just done that.

So the rescue is not a lesser thing that happens to be recorded next to greater ones. It is a smaller act by the same hands, and the smallness is what makes it legible. They could see this one.

What This Passage Does Not Say

It does not say the disciples were converted in the boat, and it does not use saved to mean anything about their sins. Reading it that way would flatten the scene and lose its actual force.

It also does not promise that everyone who prays this prayer in a storm gets a calm sea. The New Testament is honest about that elsewhere: Paul prayed in a ship that broke apart under him, and the promise he received was that the lives would be spared while the vessel was lost. James was killed with a sword while Peter walked out of prison. This story is not a technique.

What it does establish is that when the New Testament says saved, the first question is always: from what? Here the answer is water. Two verses later in the next scene, the answer will be a legion of demons. Seven chapters earlier, it was sins. The word did not change. The peril did.

A Small Rescue With a Large Signature

There is a pattern in the Gospels that this passage begins. Someone is in trouble, cries out, and is helped — and the help is of a kind that raises a question about the helper.

That is the shape of the whole thing. The healings, the feedings, the calming of the water: none of them is the salvation Matthew announced in chapter one. All of them are performed by the person who came to accomplish it, and each one leaks a little evidence about who He is.

The disciples got what they asked for that night. What they went away with was a question: what manner of man is this? The rest of Matthew is the answer.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Drowning. A boat filling with water in a sudden squall on the Sea of Galilee
Saved into what?The preservation of their lives, and a question they had not yet answered about Jesus
Who saves?Jesus, by a word to the wind and the sea
Through what means?A cry for help — “Lord, save us: we perish”
When?Immediately, within the scene
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?No. But the rescue exposes who the rescuer is
