“The Lord Knoweth How to Deliver”

Noah and Lot preserved while the world around them was judged — and the conclusion Peter draws for his own readers

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • 2 Peter 2:4–10
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › 2 Peter 2:4–10

Peter is warning his readers about false teachers, and he argues from history: God has judged before, and in judging He has always known how to keep His own. The saving language is bound up in that pattern.

Two Rescues Inside a Chain of Judgments

Peter lists God’s past judgments — the angels that sinned, the ancient world, the cities of the plain — and threads two preservations through them.

And spared not the old world, but saved Noah the eighth person, a preacher of righteousness, bringing in the flood upon the world of the ungodly … and delivered just Lot, vexed with the filthy conversation of the wicked.

2 Peter 2:5, 7

Ephylaxen — preserved, guarded — is used of Noah; errysato — delivered, drew out of danger — of Lot. The vocabulary is rescue vocabulary, and in both cases it means the same thing Hebrews meant of Noah: a righteous person brought through a judgment that fell on everyone around him.

Noah the Eighth

Peter calls Noah the eighth person — one of eight souls, the tiny remnant against a whole world. And he adds a detail Genesis implies and Peter makes explicit: Noah was a preacher of righteousness. The years of building were also years of warning. The world that drowned had been told.

That turns the flood from a bare catastrophe into a judgment against people who had been given notice and refused it. Noah’s preservation is not favouritism; it is the sparing of the one household that heeded the warning it was preaching to everyone else.

Just Lot, Vexed

Lot is the harder case, and Peter’s handling of him is instructive. Genesis does not present Lot flatteringly — he chose the plain, lingered in the city, and left it reluctantly. Yet Peter calls him just Lot, and says his righteous soul was vexed day after day by the wickedness around him.

Peter is not whitewashing Lot’s failures. He is making a point his readers need: that a genuinely righteous person can be found in a thoroughly corrupt place, distressed by it rather than at home in it, and that God knows how to tell the difference. The mark Peter fixes on is not Lot’s record but his vexation — that the surrounding evil grieved him rather than pleased him.

The Conclusion Peter Draws

The two rescues are not told for their own sake. Verses 4–8 are one long conditional, and verse 9 is the then:

The Lord knoweth how to deliver the godly out of temptations, and to reserve the unjust unto the day of judgment to be punished.

2 Peter 2:9

That is the point of the whole recital. God has a demonstrated competence — He knoweth how — at two things done in the same act: drawing His own safely out, and holding the guilty for judgment. The flood and Sodom prove it. The false teachers threatening Peter’s readers will not escape, and the godly troubled by them will not be lost in the reckoning.

Deliver here — rhyesthai, to rescue, draw out of — is the same word Jesus put in the Lord’s Prayer, deliver us from evil. Its sense in verse 9 stretches a little wider than Noah’s flood: not only preservation through a historical judgment, but God’s settled way of extracting the godly from testing generally. Peter moves from two ancient rescues to a standing assurance.

What the Word Means Here

Historically, physical preservation of the righteous through judgment — Noah guarded through the flood, Lot drawn out of Sodom. Peter does not spiritualise these; they happened, and people lived who would otherwise have died.

But he presses them into a pattern for his readers, and a study should follow him there. The God who preserved Noah and Lot knows how to deliver the godly and reserve the unjust — and He is still doing both. The rescues are not only past events; they are evidence about the character and competence of the God his readers are trusting under pressure from teachers who deny Him.

At a Glance
