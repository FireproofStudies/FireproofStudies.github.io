“Christ Jesus Came into the World to Save Sinners”

Paul states the gospel in nine words, then puts himself at the bottom of the list — and explains why that was the point

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • 1 Timothy 1:12–17
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › 1 Timothy 1:12–17

“This is a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief.”

It is the clearest one-sentence statement of purpose in the New Testament. Nine words in English before the qualifier: Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners.

Not to instruct sinners. Not to improve them, or to demonstrate an ethic, or to establish a movement. To save them. The infinitive is sōsai — the ordinary saving verb, in its full sense.

A Faithful Saying

Paul flags it. Pistos ho logos — a trustworthy word — is a formula he uses five times across the pastoral letters, apparently to mark a saying already in circulation among the churches.

So this may not be Paul’s own composition. It may be something Christians were already saying to each other, which he quotes because it is exact.

And he adds four words that are certainly his own: of whom I am chief.

Not Modesty

It is tempting to read chief of sinners as a pious exaggeration — the sort of thing a humble man says. Paul’s surrounding argument makes clear it is not.

He lists the specifics: “who was before a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious.” That last word means violent, insolent, someone who inflicts harm arrogantly. Luke’s account fills it in — Paul consenting to Stephen’s death, making havoc of the church, entering houses, dragging out men and women, committing them to prison, breathing out threatenings and slaughter.

He is not being self-deprecating. He is naming a record.

And notice the tense in verse 15. Not of whom I was chief. Am. Years into an apostolic ministry, writing to a younger man he is instructing, Paul keeps the present tense.

“I Obtained Mercy, Because I Did It Ignorantly in Unbelief”

This clause makes some readers uneasy, because it sounds like an excuse. It is not.

In the Old Testament law, sins of ignorance had a sacrifice; the high-handed, deliberate sin against light did not. Paul is not claiming innocence — he has just called himself a blasphemer and a violent man. He is locating his case within the category where mercy was possible, and he says so as an explanation of why mercy came, not as a mitigation of what he did.

The next verse settles the direction anyway: “And the grace of our Lord was exceeding abundant with faith and love which is in Christ Jesus.” The word is a compound Paul seems to have coined — super-abounded. Grace did not merely cover the case; it overflowed it.

Why He Uses Himself as the Example

Verse 16 gives the reason, and it is the theological heart of the paragraph:

Howbeit for this cause I obtained mercy, that in me first Jesus Christ might shew forth all longsuffering, for a pattern to them which should hereafter believe on him to life everlasting.

A pattern. The word is hypotypōsis — a sketch, an outline, a prototype.

Paul is saying: my case was not an exception to how this works. It was a demonstration of how this works. If the man who was actively destroying the church can be met with mercy, then the door is not narrow in the way people fear.

That is why he keeps the present tense on chief. His usefulness as the pattern depends on the case not being softened. The moment he becomes a reformed man with a colourful past, the argument weakens. He needs to stay the worst example, because the point of the example is the size of the grace it required.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense, without qualification.

The peril: sin, unbelief, active opposition to Christ. The result: mercy, faith and love in Christ Jesus, life everlasting. The agent: Christ Jesus, who came into the world — which is incarnation language, the same movement John describes.

There is no narrower reading available. This is not rescue from danger or recovery from illness. It is the reason the Son came.

The Doxology

Paul cannot finish the paragraph without breaking into praise:

Now unto the King eternal, immortal, invisible, the only wise God, be honour and glory for ever and ever. Amen.

1 Timothy 1:17

That is worth noticing structurally. He begins with thanks — I thank Christ Jesus our Lord, who hath enabled me — recounts what he was, states the gospel, explains why his case matters, and ends in doxology.

The whole passage is a movement from personal gratitude to public praise, with the faithful saying sitting in the middle as the thing that explains both.

What This Passage Gives the Rest of the Project

A definition, stated by Paul and evidently agreed across the early churches, of what the saving verb means when it is used at full stretch.

Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners. Every narrower use in this gateway — the storm, the illness, the shipwreck, the demoniac — is performed by the same person whose stated purpose is this one. That is why the smaller rescues are not a distraction from the subject. They are done by the hands that came to do the larger thing.

And a second gift, which is pastoral rather than lexical. The pattern Paul offers is deliberately the extreme case. Anyone reading this gateway who suspects they are beyond the range of the word has been anticipated by the man who wrote the sentence — and answered before they asked.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sin — and in Paul’s own case, blasphemy, persecution, and violence against the church
Saved into what?Mercy, faith and love in Christ Jesus, life everlasting, and a commission to ministry
Who saves?Christ Jesus, who came into the world for that purpose
Through what means?Grace exceedingly abundant, received in faith
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. This is the mission statement of the incarnation in one clause
