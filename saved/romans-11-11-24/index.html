“If By Any Means I Might … Save Some of Them”

Paul makes his Gentile ministry a roundabout instrument for the salvation of his own people, and warns the grafted branches not to boast

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Romans 11:11–24
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Romans 11:11–24

Paul has been agonising over his own people’s unbelief, and here he finds a strange purpose in his mission to outsiders: it might, by a long route, bring some of his own kin home. The saving verb appears in that hope.

The Roundabout Strategy

For I speak to you Gentiles, inasmuch as I am the apostle of the Gentiles, I magnify mine office: if by any means I may provoke to emulation them which are my flesh, and might save some of them.

Romans 11:13–14

Sōsō tinas ex autōn — save some of them. Paul is not claiming to be the source of anyone’s salvation; God is that. But he calls his ministry a means to it, and states the mechanism openly: he magnifies his office among the Gentiles, hoping the sight of Gentiles receiving Israel’s blessings will provoke Israel to a holy jealousy, and so bring some of them to faith.

It is an indirect strategy, and Paul is candid about it. He works one field partly in hope of a harvest in another. His labour among the Gentiles is aimed, in part, past them, at the people he calls my flesh.

Human Agency as a Real Means

This is the note the passage adds to the gateway, and it matches what Paul says elsewhere. God is the sole saving agent, and yet human ministry is a genuine instrument He uses — not a decoration on a process that would run identically without it. Paul expects his work to make a difference to who is saved: if by any means … I might save some.

The corpus has already met this in the studies on human instrumentality — the minister who saves himself and his hearers, the one who turns a wanderer back. Romans 11 is the same principle at the scale of a whole mission. God saves; Paul’s ministry is a real means He employs; and Paul orders his labours accordingly, working the Gentile field with an eye to the jealousy it might stir in Israel.

The Olive Tree

Paul then turns to the Gentile believers with an image and a warning. Israel’s unbelieving branches have been broken off the cultivated olive tree, and wild branches — Gentiles — grafted in. But grafting is no ground for pride.

Boast not against the branches. But if thou boast, thou bearest not the root, but the root thee.

Romans 11:18

The warning is pointed. A grafted-in Gentile stands by faith, not by any superiority, and stands in a tree whose root is not his own. “Because of unbelief they were broken off, and thou standest by faith. Be not highminded, but fear.”

Paul will not let the salvation of the Gentiles become a platform for contempt toward the Jews. The same principle that broke off natural branches for unbelief can break off grafted ones — and the same God who broke off the natural branches is able to graff them in again. The whole arrangement runs on faith and God’s kindness, never on the branch’s own standing.

“God Is Able to Graff Them In Again”

The passage ends on hope rather than warning. The natural branches are not beyond recovery: “for God is able to graff them in again.” If Gentiles, contrary to nature, could be grafted into a tree not their own, how much more can the natural branches be restored to their own olive tree, when their unbelief gives way to faith.

That is the horizon Paul’s ministry serves. His hope of saving some of them now is a foretaste of a larger restoration he believes God is able to accomplish. The strategy of provoking jealousy is one instrument inside a purpose far bigger than Paul’s own reach.

What the Word Means Here

The full sense — salvation from unbelief, restoration to the tree of God’s people — with the particular accent on human agency. Paul names his own ministry as a means by which some might be saved, and he is not exaggerating for effect; he plans his work around it.

Its contribution to the gateway is twofold. It shows salvation genuinely served by human labour, without making the labourer the saviour. And it fences that truth against pride: the branches that stand, stand by faith and by a root not their own, and the God who saves some through Paul’s ministry now is able to graft the rest in again. Human means, divine power, and no room for boasting — all in one olive tree.
