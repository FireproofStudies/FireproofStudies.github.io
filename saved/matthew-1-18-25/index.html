What Does “He Shall Save His People from Their Sins” Mean?

Why the angel names sin itself as the ruin Jesus came to overcome—and not merely the punishment sin deserves

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 9 min read • Matthew 1:18–25
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Matthew 1:18–25

Most readers pass over Matthew 1:21 quickly, because it sounds like something they already know. An angel tells Joseph what to name the child, and gives the reason: “thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins.” It reads like a summary of the Christian faith, and in a sense it is. But familiarity has a way of flattening a sentence, and this one repays a second look.

Notice what the angel does not say. He does not say that Jesus will save His people from hell, or from judgment, or from the wrath to come—though Matthew will speak of all three before his Gospel closes. He says that Jesus will save His people from their sins. The ruin named here is not the sentence. It is the thing that earned the sentence.

That distinction is easy to lose in either of two directions. Some readers hear the verse as a promise of acquittal only: Jesus absorbs a penalty, the record is cleared, and the saved person goes on much as before, forgiven but unchanged. Others hear it so broadly that it dissolves—salvation becomes a warm word meaning everything good God does, and the particular ruin Matthew names disappears into the haze. The verse resists both. It is precise. And because it is the first thing Matthew tells us about Jesus’ work, it governs everything he says afterward.

The Two Names Interpret Each Other

The announcement arrives at an awkward moment. Joseph knows the child is not his, and he has already decided what to do—quietly, without disgracing Mary. The angel does not simply reassure him that Mary has done no wrong. He tells Joseph who the child is, and what the child has come to do.

Joseph is addressed as “thou son of David.” That address places this birth inside Israel’s royal hope. The child will be legally received into David’s line through Joseph and identified as the promised King. Yet the lineage does not produce Him. He is conceived by the Holy Ghost. The birth is entirely real and entirely human, and the coming is an act of God.

Then two names are given, and they interpret one another. Jesus names the mission: the Lord saves. Emmanuel names the manner: God with us. Matthew has arranged them deliberately. God did not send salvation as a benefit dispatched from a distance, or a decree issued from a throne no one may approach. He came among His people in the Son who saves. And Matthew will close his Gospel with the risen Jesus making the matching promise—“lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world.” The book opens and ends with God present among those He saves.

It is worth saying plainly that the name is not a formula. Joseph obeys by naming the child, but the naming does not accomplish anything; it publicly receives what the angel revealed. Salvation is in the Person the name identifies, never in the correct pronunciation of religious words.

The Greek keeps the emphasis exactly where Matthew wants it. The angel says autos gar sōsei—“for He Himself will save.” The pronoun is unnecessary for the grammar and included for the weight. Joseph gives the name. Jesus does the saving.

Saved from Their Sins, Not Merely from Their Sentence

The verse answers the question of ruin with unusual directness: their sins.

Sin is not a stain on an otherwise intact life, and it is not merely a debt entered in a ledger. It is enacted rebellion against God—the embrace of self-rule, the preference for falsehood, the disordered desire, the conduct that runs against the grain of the Creator. And it does not stay still. Sin produces guilt, corrupts action and relationship, hardens into estrangement, and finally belongs to the dominion of death.

So when the angel says Jesus will save His people from their sins, he is naming a ruin with several dimensions at once: the guilt and debt of what they have done; the estrangement from God those acts express and deepen; the mastery of a settled allegiance to self; the corruption that spreads through a life; and the death toward which the whole thing carries them.

Consider what rescue from a tyrant means. If a people are delivered from the tyrant who rules them, that rescue includes deliverance from what he has been doing to them—it is not merely the cancellation of one sentence he had scheduled. Sin is both the guilty act and the ruling power. To save a people from their sins must therefore reach their record, their relationship with God, their allegiance, their life, and their destiny.

Matthew fills in each of these as his Gospel proceeds. Jesus tells a paralyzed man, “Son, be of good cheer; thy sins be forgiven thee,” and the scribes understand exactly how large a claim that is. Jesus gives His life “a ransom for many,” language of release from a claim that held them. At the table He speaks of “my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins.” And at the end the risen King sends His disciples to teach the nations to observe everything He commanded—because a people saved from sin are not left to go on as they were.

The plural is worth noticing too. Not sin in the abstract, but their sins: personal, particular, committed. Matthew’s people are born into a humanity already governed by rebellion, and they also make that rebellion their own. Jesus saves them from the guilt and the grip of what they themselves have done.

The People Who Belong to the King

“His people” first awakens Israel’s covenant hope. Joseph is a son of David; the child is the promised King; Matthew will shortly quote a prophecy about a ruler who shall “rule my people Israel.” Jesus comes within Israel, in fulfilment of promises made to Israel.

But Matthew has already complicated any reading that stops there. Four women appear in his genealogy, and they are not the women a Jewish reader would expect. Foreign magi arrive to worship the young King while Jerusalem is troubled. Jesus marvels at the faith of a Roman centurion and says that many will come from east and west to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. And the Gospel ends with a command to make disciples of all nations.

“His people,” then, are the people who belong to Jesus the Messiah. The promise begins inside Israel’s story and reaches the nations without ever ceasing to be the fulfilment of Israel’s hope.

This possessive language does not mean that everyone is already saved, or that belonging to a nation regenerates anyone. It identifies whose they are and what He intends for them. Matthew’s own narrative shows people receiving Him, following Him, trusting Him, deserting Him, and opposing Him. The saving mission calls for personal repentance and faith, and it creates a people whose allegiance is to the King.

The Gospel Answers Its Own Announcement

Matthew 1:21 states the mission before it explains the method. The verse should not be pressed to say everything at once; the rest of the book is the answer.

Jesus saves His people by coming among them as Emmanuel, so that salvation begins in God’s gracious approach and not in any human ascent toward Him. He lives in the faithful obedience that humanity refused. He exercises an authority to forgive sins that His healings reveal but do not exhaust. He gives His life as a ransom rather than preserving it by power. He sheds covenant blood for the remission of sins, answering the guilt directly.

And then, at the cross, the mockers frame the matter better than they know: “He saved others; himself he cannot save.” They meant it as proof of failure. Matthew records it as the shape of the mission. He does not come down, because coming down would abandon the very people He came to save.

Then He rises and reigns with all authority—which means His people are not merely left with a cleared past. They are brought under the King’s authority and into the life He now holds. This is why salvation cannot finally be described as receiving things from Jesus. It reaches its intended end when the sinner is joined to the risen Christ, so that Christ’s own life becomes his.

A Mission That Cannot Be Confined to One Moment

The verb is future—He will save—and it looks forward from the manger to the whole mission. But we should not read an order of salvation out of a single tense.

Jesus accomplished this salvation historically, in a life, a death, and a resurrection that happened. A person enters it in conversion, turning to Christ in repentant faith as the Spirit joins him to the risen Son. That life then continues to free and reshape the believer as the union bears its fruit. And resurrection completes the deliverance, when sin and death can no longer touch him at all.

Matthew 1:21 is comprehensive because it announces the whole mission—not because one future verb encodes a sequence.

This matters for reading the rest of Matthew. The word save appears later in much narrower settings: sailors keeping afloat, a woman healed of a long illness, disciples preserved through persecution, Jesus taunted to rescue Himself. Those are real deliverances and they are not conversions. Matthew 1:21 sets the horizon within which the narrower rescues reveal the Savior’s authority without every healing being turned into a regeneration.

God With Us, and God For Us

When the angel tells Joseph that Jesus will save His people from their sins, he announces the mission that governs everything Matthew will write. Jesus does not rescue people from punishment and leave them under sin’s rule. He forgives their guilt, ransoms them from an old allegiance, restores them to God, gathers them under His kingship, and brings them into the life His death and resurrection secured.

The name Jesus tells us that He saves. The name Emmanuel tells us how near He came to do it. He saves His people from their sins by bringing them to Himself—the crucified, risen, and ever-present King.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Their sins—the guilt, the estrangement, the mastery, and the death sin carries with it
Saved into what?Forgiveness, restored fellowship with God, allegiance to Jesus, and finally a share in His risen life
Who saves?Jesus Christ Himself—conceived by the Spirit, heir of David, and Emmanuel
Through what means?Verse 21 does not yet say. Matthew unfolds it: the ransom, the covenant blood, the cross, the resurrection—received in repentant faith
When?Accomplished in Christ’s mission, entered in conversion, continued under His reign, completed in resurrection
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. This is Matthew’s governing statement of what Jesus came to do
