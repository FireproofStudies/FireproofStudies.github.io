“We Are Saved by Hope” — What Paul Means

A past-tense verb, a future body, and a groaning creation — Paul puts salvation in a tense that unsettles people, on purpose

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Romans 8:18–30
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Romans 8:18–30

“For we are saved by hope: but hope that is seen is not hope: for what a man seeth, why doth he yet hope for?”

Two things in that sentence trip readers, and both are worth getting right, because between them they explain why Paul talks about salvation in three different tenses without contradicting himself.

The Verb Is Past

Esōthēmen — aorist passive, first person plural. We were saved.

Not we are being saved, and not we shall be saved. Paul reaches for the tense that describes a completed action. Something happened, and it is done.

The KJV’s we are saved renders it reasonably in English, but it can leave the impression of a present ongoing state. Paul’s Greek is more definite than that. He is pointing back to something settled.

“By Hope” Does Not Mean Hope Saved Us

This is the phrase that causes the trouble. Read carelessly, saved by hope sounds as though hope were the instrument — as if optimism accomplished something.

Paul has spent five chapters saying the opposite. We are justified by faith, saved by Christ’s blood, reconciled by His death, saved by His life. Hope does not join that list as a rival cause.

The dative here describes the sphere or manner in which the salvation is held, not the means by which it was obtained. We were saved — and the shape of our present possession of it is hope. We have it in the mode of expectation.

Paul then explains exactly that: hope that is seen is not hope. If you can see it, you are not hoping for it; you are looking at it. So to say we are saved in hope is to say the thing is real and not yet visible.

Why a Body Is the Missing Piece

The context makes the unseen part specific, and it is not a vague heavenly future.

Even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

Romans 8:23

That is what is outstanding. Not forgiveness — that is settled. Not adoption as a status — Paul has just said we have received the Spirit of adoption and cry Abba, Father. What is waiting is the body.

This is why Paul can say we were saved and we are waiting in the same breath without incoherence. The saving act is finished. Its effect on the believer’s body has not yet arrived. A Christian is a person whose standing has changed completely and whose knees still hurt.

Three Groans

Paul builds the paragraph on a striking triple.

Creation groans. It was subjected to vanity, not willingly, and waits for the manifestation of the sons of God. Paul says it travails in pain together until now — a birth image, not a death rattle. The world is not merely decaying; it is in labour.

We groan. Having the firstfruits of the Spirit, we groan within ourselves. Notice that possessing the Spirit does not stop the groaning; Paul makes the Spirit’s presence the reason we feel it. A deposit makes you aware of what is still owed.

The Spirit groans. “The Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered.” God’s own Spirit, inside the believer, praying beyond words in the same key as the creation outside.

That is an extraordinary thing to say, and it changes the emotional register of the whole passage. The groaning is not evidence that something has gone wrong with your faith. It is the sound the whole system makes while it waits.

“We Know Not What We Should Pray For”

Sitting in the middle of this is one of the most quietly merciful sentences Paul wrote: we know not what we should pray for as we ought.

He does not treat that as a failure to be corrected by better technique. He treats it as the normal condition of people who cannot see the end, and he says the Spirit covers it.

That is worth noticing in a passage about salvation held in hope. Not knowing what to ask for is exactly what you would expect of someone waiting for something he has never seen.

The Golden Chain

Then Paul does something that has occupied theologians ever since:

Whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son... Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified.

Romans 8:29–30

Five links, and every verb is past tense — including glorified, which has not happened yet to anyone reading it.

That is deliberate. Paul puts the last link in the same tense as the first because from God’s side the chain is complete. The believer’s glorification is as settled as his calling.

This project does not need to resolve every question that these two verses raise, and it should not pretend to. What the passage does establish, unambiguously, is the ground of the confidence: the reason salvation can be held in hope rather than in anxiety is that its completion rests on the same God who began it.

The Purpose Named

One more thing deserves saying, because it is often skipped in the rush to argue about predestination.

The stated goal is not that we should get to heaven. It is conformity to the image of His Son — that He might be the firstborn among many brethren.

That is the shape of this whole gateway’s argument arriving in Paul’s language. Salvation is not primarily an escape or a transaction. Its end is a family resemblance, and a Son who is not diminished by having brothers.

What “Saved” Means Here

The comprehensive sense, viewed from its unfinished edge. The peril in view is bodily corruption and the whole groaning condition of a subjected creation. The result awaited is the redemption of the body and sonship made visible.

And the tense is the lesson. We were saved — done. In hope — not yet seen. Both true at once, which is why the Christian life feels the way it does: settled at the root and unfinished in the body, groaning with a creation that is in labour rather than in decline.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Bodily corruption, groaning, and the unfinished condition of a creation in bondage
Saved into what?The redemption of the body, and sonship made visible
Who saves?God, who predestined, called, justified, and glorified
What does “by hope” mean?The sphere and orientation of the salvation, not its cause
When?Accomplished already — the verb is past — and awaiting its completion in the body
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes, seen from its unfinished end
