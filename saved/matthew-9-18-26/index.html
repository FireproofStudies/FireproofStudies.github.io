What Does “Made Whole” Mean in Matthew 9:22?

Matthew uses the word usually translated “saved” three times in two verses—and every one of them means healed

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 7 min read • Matthew 9:18–26
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Matthew 9:18–26

Anyone reading Matthew 9 in English will notice that Jesus heals a woman. What no English reader can see is that Matthew describes it three times using the word ordinarily translated save.

The woman says to herself, “If I may but touch his garment, I shall be whole”—I shall be saved. Jesus tells her, “thy faith hath made thee whole”—your faith has saved you. And Matthew reports, “the woman was made whole from that hour”—she was saved from that hour.

Three forms of sōzō in two verses, and every one of them means her body stopped bleeding.

That is worth sitting with, because it is one of the clearest demonstrations in the New Testament that this word does not carry a fixed theological cargo wherever it appears. Matthew has already told us that Jesus will “save his people from their sins.” He now uses the same verb, three times over, for a woman’s chronic illness. The word did not change. The situation did.

Two Impossible Cases

Matthew has built the scene carefully by folding one story into another.

A synagogue ruler arrives to say his daughter has just died and asks Jesus to come and lay a hand on her. On the way, a woman who has bled for twelve years comes up behind Him and touches the fringe of His cloak.

The two accounts interpret one another. In both, ordinary human power has run out. In both, the afflicted person cannot produce wholeness or life. In both, someone comes to Jesus in trust, physical contact occurs, and embodied life is restored.

So the woman’s healing is not a spiritual metaphor dropped into a narrative, and it is not an isolated marvel. It sits inside Matthew’s concentrated demonstration of Jesus’ authority over sickness and death—the two things that end every human story.

Twelve Years

Matthew is spare about her condition; Mark and Luke fill it in. She had spent everything she had on physicians and grown worse rather than better.

Under Israel’s purity law, a continual discharge of blood also rendered her ceremonially unclean, and anything she touched with her. Twelve years of that means twelve years of a particular kind of isolation—not merely illness, but a life arranged around not touching anyone.

Which explains why she comes from behind, silently, hoping to take something without being noticed. She has good reason to expect that being noticed will go badly for her.

Did the Garment Heal Her?

The obvious question the story raises, and the one it answers most carefully.

She does not trust cloth in the abstract. She reaches for His garment, and her reasoning—“If I may but touch his garment”—places the power in the person wearing it. The fringe is the point of contact, not the source.

Jesus makes this explicit by refusing to let the transaction stay anonymous. He turns around. He looks at her. He speaks to her, and He calls her daughter—the only person in the Gospels He addresses that way. What she had hoped to take invisibly, He insists on giving her face to face.

And then He points not at His clothing but at her faith. The gesture mattered because it expressed her reliance on Him. It did not operate on Him.

Can Faith Save Anyone?

“Thy faith hath made thee whole” raises the same question Luke 7 raises, and the answer is the same: Scripture will name the receiving instrument without turning it into the healer.

Faith has no power in itself; it has an object. Her faith was her chosen reliance on Jesus, carried out by getting close enough to touch Him because she believed His power was sufficient and her situation was not.

The structure of the story keeps this straight. Jesus possesses the power. The woman knows she has none. Faith moves her toward Him. He sees her, addresses her, and confirms what has happened.

The verb form in His declaration is a perfect—an accomplished act with a standing result. She is not going to be healed; she stands healed. Matthew then adds his own timestamp: from that hour.

Does This Mean She Was Converted?

Not as far as Matthew tells us, and we should be careful here.

Matthew says she was healed. He does not say she was justified, regenerated, forgiven, or brought into the people of God. Those things cannot be imported into the verb simply because sōzō carries them elsewhere in the New Testament. That is exactly the mistake this whole project exists to prevent—loading the full weight of a word’s range onto every occurrence of it.

And yet the healing is not unrelated to the salvation Jesus came to bring. A body released from twelve years of ruin is a small, local, visible instance of what the Savior does with ruin generally. It reveals His authority over the disorder that runs through human life, and it points toward the wholeness His kingdom will finally bring.

Two things are true at once, and both need saying. Jesus really healed this woman’s body. And that healing displays, in concrete and touchable form, the restorative character of His saving authority.

Anticipation is not identity. A signpost is not the city.

Why “Made Whole” Is a Good Translation

Given all that, the KJV’s rendering is not a weakening of the Greek. It is an accurate reading of what Matthew describes. The woman was made whole; that is precisely what happened.

Its only drawback is invisibility. An English reader will never suspect that the verb behind “made whole” is the same verb behind “he shall save his people from their sins.” The translators made the right decision about this passage and, in doing so, concealed something useful about the word.

Which is the reason a project like this exists. The English word saved has been asked to mean one thing—conversion—and every passage where it means something else has quietly been either mistranslated in the reader’s head or ignored. Matthew 9 is the plainest available correction. Here it means a woman’s bleeding stopped after twelve years, and Matthew says it three times so that no one can miss it.

At a Glance

Saved from what?A chronic bodily affliction of twelve years, with its religious and social isolation
Saved into what?Immediate and complete bodily wholeness, from that hour
Who saves?Jesus. Faith is her reliance on Him, not an independent power
Through what means?Faith, expressed by approaching Him and touching the hem of His garment
When?Immediately, within the narrated encounter
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Not directly. But the healing reveals the Savior’s authority over ruin
