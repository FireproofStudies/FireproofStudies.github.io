“One Lawgiver, Who Is Able to Save and to Destroy”

James stops a quarrel by naming who actually holds the power of life and death — and it is not the one passing judgment on his brother

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 6 min read • James 4:11–12
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › James 4:11–12

James is dealing with a community that speaks against itself — believers judging and disparaging one another — and he stops it with a single sentence about who actually holds the power they are playing at. The saving word is at its centre.

Speaking Against a Brother

Speak not evil one of another, brethren. He that speaketh evil of his brother, and judgeth his brother, speaketh evil of the law, and judgeth the law: but if thou judge the law, thou art not a doer of the law, but a judge.

James 4:11

James makes a surprising move. To sit in judgment on a brother, he says, is to set yourself above the law itself — to treat the law as something you stand over and evaluate, rather than something you stand under and obey. The self-appointed judge has quietly promoted himself out of the ranks of the doers and into the seat of the assessor. He has assumed an authority that is not his.

And then James names whose authority it actually is.

One Lawgiver

There is one lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy: who art thou that judgest another?

James 4:12

Ho dynamenos sōsai kai apolesai — the one who is able to save and to destroy. There is a single Lawgiver, and He is defined by a power no one else possesses: He can save, and He can destroy. Life and ruin are in His hand alone. That is what makes Him the Judge, and it is precisely what the quarrelling believers do not have.

The saving word here is a statement of divine prerogative. To save stands opposite to destroy as the two things only God can do with a soul, and the pairing is the point. The one who can do both is the only one entitled to judge, because judgment is the exercise of exactly that power. Anyone else pronouncing condemnation on a brother is claiming a chair he cannot fill.

The Question That Ends It

James closes with a question rather than a command: who art thou that judgest another? It is deflating on purpose. Set beside the one Lawgiver who holds life and death, the pretensions of the community’s mutual judging collapse. Who are you? — not God, not the Lawgiver, not able to save or destroy anyone. The question shames the impulse to condemn by measuring it against the only One with the right.

This connects to Jesus’ own warning that we should fear the One able to destroy both soul and body in hell rather than those who can only kill the body — the same recognition that the power over the soul’s final state belongs to God alone. James applies it not to fear of persecutors but to restraint toward brothers: the God who alone can save or destroy has not delegated His bench to us.

Humility Before the Judge

The deepest note is the humility James is pressing. To refuse to judge a brother is not moral indifference; it is submission to the one Lawgiver — an acknowledgment that the seat of final judgment is occupied, and not by us. The believer who grasps that God alone is able to save and to destroy will hold his tongue about his brother, because he knows the case is not his to try.

That fits the letter’s whole burden. James has been calling for a wisdom that is peaceable, gentle, easy to be intreated, and against a bitter, self-exalting spirit. Naming God as the sole Lawgiver who saves and destroys is the theological ground of that gentleness: leave the judgment to the One who can actually carry it out.

What the Word Means Here

The saving word appears as divine prerogative — the authority to save or to destroy, held by the one Lawgiver alone. It is not, here, a description of someone being converted; it is a statement of who has the power that salvation and destruction represent, and the answer is God, and God only.

Its contribution to the gateway is that framing. Salvation belongs to a Judge who alone can grant or withhold it, and that fact carries a practical demand: humility toward those we are tempted to condemn. The one who is able to save and to destroy has not shared His seat. Who art thou that judgest another?
