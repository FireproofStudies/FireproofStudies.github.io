“Thy Faith Hath Made Thee Whole”

All ten lepers are cleansed — but only one comes back, and to him alone Jesus speaks the saving word, after the healing is already done

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • Luke 17:11–19
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Luke 17:11–19

This is one of the most carefully constructed healing scenes in the Gospels, and its careful construction is the whole point. Ten are cleansed; one comes back; and the saving word is spoken only to the one, only after the cleansing is already complete.

Ten Cleansed

And as he entered into a certain village, there met him ten men that were lepers, which stood afar off: and they lifted up their voices, and said, Jesus, Master, have mercy on us.

Luke 17:12–13

Jesus tells them, “Go shew yourselves unto the priests” — the procedure for a leper declared clean. And Luke records: “And it came to pass, that, as they went, they were cleansed.” All ten. Their cleansing is narrated plainly and completely; every one of them is healed of leprosy on the way. The healing is not in doubt, and it is not partial. Ten lepers went, and ten lepers were made clean.

That is the fact the rest of the passage is built on. The cleansing is finished before the saving word is ever spoken.

One Returns

And one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, and with a loud voice glorified God, and fell down on his face at his feet, giving him thanks: and he was a Samaritan.

Luke 17:15–16

One of the ten, seeing himself healed, does what the other nine do not. He turns back. He glorifies God with a loud voice. He falls on his face at Jesus’ feet. He gives thanks. And Luke adds the detail that lands last and hardest: he was a Samaritan — the outsider, the one from whom least was expected.

Jesus marks the absence of the others: “Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine? There are not found that returned to give glory to God, save this stranger.” The nine are cleansed and gone. The one is cleansed and back, at Jesus’ feet, worshipping.

The Saving Word

Then, to this one alone:

And he said unto him, Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole.

Luke 17:19

Hē pistis sou sesōken se — thy faith hath made thee whole, thy faith hath saved thee. And here is the interpretive question the passage is designed to raise: the Samaritan was already cleansed. His leprosy was gone before he turned back. So when Jesus tells him thy faith hath saved thee, He is saying it to a man whose bodily healing is already an accomplished fact. What, then, does the saving word add?

The structure invites the conclusion that it adds something. Luke has gone out of his way to separate the cleansing (which all ten received) from this final declaration (which only the returning one received). If thy faith hath saved thee meant nothing more than you have been healed of leprosy, it would be redundant — the man knew that already, and so did the nine. The deliberate distinction between the ten cleansed and the one saved suggests that the Samaritan received, in that final word, something the other nine did not.

What the Something More Might Be

A study must be careful exactly here, and the caution is itself the ruling. The broader meaning should not be asserted merely from the verb — sōzō can mean simple bodily healing, and it does elsewhere in this very gateway. If the deeper sense is present, it does not come from the lexeme alone; it comes from Luke’s narrative construction.

And that construction points a particular way. The one who received the further word is the one who returned in faith — who saw the healing as occasion for worship, who came back to the Healer rather than merely enjoying the healing, who recognized in Jesus something worth falling on his face before. The nine received cleansing and went on their way, presumably grateful, certainly healed. The one received cleansing and came back to Christ, and to him Jesus speaks of a wholeness that his returning faith had laid hold of.

So the layered reading is this: all ten were healed; the one who returned in faith and worship received, in Jesus’ final word, a wholeness that reached past his skin — a salvation grasped by the faith that brought him back. The study holds this as what the passage most naturally teaches, while noting that it rests on Luke’s deliberate design, not on the bare meaning of the word.

Why the Samaritan

Luke’s identification of the returning leper as a Samaritan is not incidental. Throughout his Gospel, Luke keeps showing outsiders — a centurion, a Samaritan, a tax collector — grasping what insiders miss. The nine, presumably Jews who knew the law of the leper and went dutifully to the priests, did exactly what was required and no more. The Samaritan, from whom the least was expected, did the one thing that mattered most: he came back to Jesus.

There is a quiet rebuke in it, and a quiet hope. Right procedure — going to the priests — is not the same as returning to Christ. And the person least expected to see it may be the one who sees it most clearly.

What the Word Means Here

For all ten, the bodily sense: cleansing from leprosy, complete and real. For the one who returned, the saving word spoken over an already-healed man, which Luke’s careful structure suggests reaches further — a wholeness laid hold of by worshipping faith.

Its contribution to the gateway is this deliberate layering. Luke separates cleansing from saving on purpose, so that the reader must ask what the one received that the nine did not. The answer is not forced from the verb; it arises from the shape of the story — that the man who turned back in faith and fell at Jesus’ feet was told his faith had made him whole. Ten were healed. One came back. And to the one who came back, Jesus spoke of a wholeness the other nine walked away without.
