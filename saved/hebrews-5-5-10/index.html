How Was Jesus Saved from Death in Hebrews 5:7?

He prayed to the One able to save Him from death, and “was heard”—and then He died. What kind of answer was that?

By James Burke • July 20, 2026 • 10 min read • Hebrews 5:5–10
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Hebrews 5:5–10

Hebrews 5:7 contains one of the strangest sentences in the New Testament, and most readers pass over it without noticing.

Jesus “offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard.”

He was heard.

And then He died.

That is not an oversight, and Hebrews is not being loose. This is the one place in the New Testament where Christ Himself is the object of the verb save, and understanding what the Father actually did with that prayer opens up a great deal of the rest of the New Testament’s language about salvation.

Why Hebrews Is Telling Us This

The passage sits inside an argument about priesthood. An Aaronic high priest had to meet two conditions: he had to be taken from among human beings, so that he could deal gently with human weakness; and he had to be appointed by God rather than seizing the honour for himself.

Hebrews shows Christ satisfying both—without sharing the priests’ sin.

He has genuinely entered human weakness. A real body. Real temptation, without sin. Suffering. Prayer with tears. Submission. Death. And He is genuinely appointed: Hebrews sets Psalm 2 (“Thou art my Son”) beside Psalm 110 (“Thou art a priest for ever”), so that sonship does not exempt Him from suffering and suffering does not undercut His appointment.

Verses 7–10 then trace how the appointed Son arrives at the completed exercise of that priesthood: prayer, suffering, obedience, perfection, and eternal salvation. That is not an order of salvation applied to Jesus. It is the road His incarnate mission actually travelled.

Out of Death, Not Away from It

The Greek phrase is sōzein auton ek thanatou—to save Him out of death.

The preposition can describe rescue from a danger before it arrives, or emergence out of a condition already entered. On its own it settles nothing.

The context settles it. Jesus died, and Hebrews says He was heard. The same book later speaks of God bringing “again from the dead our Lord Jesus, that great shepherd of the sheep.” So in this case, being saved from death means being brought out of it.

The Father did not answer by preventing the crucifixion. He answered by refusing to leave His obedient Son under death’s dominion.

Was He Asking Not to Die?

Gethsemane is the obvious setting—“Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done”—though Hebrews speaks more broadly of “the days of his flesh,” plural prayers, strong crying, and tears. The whole passion may be in view.

And His recoil was real. It should not be softened into a performance. For the sinless Son, death was not a neutral biological event. The cross meant bearing human sin, enduring its shame, and entering the full horror of death itself.

But the prayer was never an autonomous refusal. The decisive clause is nevertheless not my will, but thine. He did not demand rescue on His own terms; He entrusted Himself to the Father who was able to save Him and left the form of the answer with Him.

So it is too narrow to say either that Jesus felt no horror at death, or that He simply asked to avoid it. He faced it truthfully and submitted to a purpose that required Him to go through it.

How the Prayer Was Answered

In two distinguishable ways.

First, He was sustained in obedient surrender. He did not turn from the mission, and death did not break His trust in the Father.

And then, finally, the Father raised Him. That is the completed answer the phrase “was heard” requires.

The resurrection is not God making up for a prayer that failed. Passing through death was the shape the answer took inside the Father’s saving purpose.

There is a pattern here for Christian prayer that is worth naming carefully, because it is often either ignored or abused. Being heard does not always mean being carried around the suffering. God may answer by keeping a person faithful through it and bringing them out the other side. In Christ’s unique case, that other side was literal resurrection and the defeat of death itself.

“In That He Feared”

The KJV’s phrase translates apo tēs eulabeias—reverence, godly fear, careful submission before God. It does not portray Jesus as cowering.

The most natural sense is that He was heard because of His reverent submission. His prayer expressed perfect trust: He did not set His will against the Father’s, did not use His sonship as an exemption from obedience, and entrusted both His life and His death to God.

The hearing is not payment earned by an unusually intense emotional performance. It belongs to a perfectly aligned relationship between a Father and an obedient Son.

How Could the Son “Learn Obedience”?

Not by moving from disobedience to obedience. Hebrews has already said He was “without sin.”

The eternal Son wills in perfect unity with the Father and the Spirit. In the incarnation, that same Person takes a complete human nature and lives a genuinely human life—dependent, tempted, suffering, praying, submitting.

So He learns obedience experientially: by enacting perfect submission under conditions He had not previously known in human life, and finally by obeying when obedience required dying.

What the Son eternally willed with the Father, He historically performed through a suffering human will.

How Could the Sinless Son Be “Made Perfect”?

The word does not mean morally repaired, and Christ was never morally defective.

Perfected here means brought to the appointed completion of a vocation. Through obedient suffering, death, resurrection, and exaltation, He becomes fully fitted and consecrated for the everlasting exercise of His priesthood.

Hebrews had used the same idea earlier: it was fitting for God, “in bringing many sons unto glory, to make the captain of their salvation perfect through sufferings.” The pioneer reaches the destination by travelling the whole road. Completed vocation, not corrected character.

What Kind of Life Did He Enter?

This is where the passage becomes theologically load-bearing.

The eternal Son never ceased to possess divine life; Father, Son, and Spirit have life in themselves, dependent on nothing. But the human life the Son assumed was creaturely, dependent, and capable of suffering and death. He truly entered death according to that humanity.

In resurrection He did not discard His humanity, and He did not simply restart His former mortal condition. He re-entered creation in human life over which death has no power. Hebrews says He was crowned with glory and honour after tasting death; that through death He destroyed the one who had the power of death; that He holds His priesthood “after the power of an endless life”; that He continues forever with an unchangeable priesthood.

“The days of his flesh,” then, does not mean the risen Christ stopped being embodied or human. It names the mortal phase of His incarnate life, before He passed through death into incorruptible life.

The Author of Eternal Salvation

The Greek word behind “author” means source or cause—not a writer drafting a plan.

He becomes the source of eternal salvation because He has personally completed the passage. He entered mortal human life. He obeyed without sin. He suffered. He died. He was brought out of death. He entered eternal priestly life in the presence of God.

He can bring others where He has already been.

Which is why salvation in Hebrews is never merely a benefit dispensed from a distance. The destination is the living Christ Himself. Those joined to Him share the resurrection life He pioneered and now holds.

“Unto All Them That Obey Him”

The verse places Christ’s obedience and the believer’s obedience side by side, and it is worth being precise about the relationship, because the sentence can be misread as merit.

Christ’s obedience completes the saving work. The believer’s obedience describes the response of someone surrendering to the saving Son.

In Hebrews, faith is never bare agreement with facts. It hears God’s word, trusts His promise, enters His provision, and keeps going toward Him. To obey Christ, in this book’s vocabulary, is to yield to Him as Lord—which is what trusting surrender means.

So the verse does not say that Christ saves those who first accumulate enough obedience to deserve Him. It says the obedient Son is the source of eternal salvation for all who yield themselves to Him.

What This Passage Gives the Rest of the Bible

One sentence, which reorganises a great deal:

Salvation is not primarily protection from entering death. It is participation in the Son who entered death and came out beyond its dominion.

That changes what Christian hope is for. The gospel does not promise that mortal life will continue indefinitely in its present form; it plainly will not. It promises that death cannot separate the believer from Christ or stop His life from reaching its intended end.

The Father saved the Son out of death. The risen Son now saves His people eternally. Union with Christ is what joins those two sentences together.

At a Glance

Who is saved here?The incarnate Son—the only place in the New Testament where Christ Himself is the object of the saving verb
Saved from what?Not sin or guilt. From remaining under the power of death
How was the prayer answered?Not by cancelling the cross. By resurrection—He was brought out of death, not spared it
What did it make Him?The source of eternal salvation, having completed the passage He now brings others through
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Indirectly in verse 7; explicitly in verse 9
