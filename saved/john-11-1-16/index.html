“If He Sleep, He Shall Do Well”

The disciples use the great saving verb to mean that a sick friend will get over it — and they could not be more wrong about what is happening

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 8 min read • John 11:1–16
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › John 11:1–16

This is the plainest use of the saving verb in John’s Gospel, and it is spoken by men who have misunderstood the situation entirely.

Then said his disciples, Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well.

John 11:12

Sōthēsetai — he shall be saved. The KJV translates it he shall do well, and that is precisely the sense: if he is sleeping, he will get over it. It is the sentence you say about a friend with a fever whose temperature has broken.

It is also, at that moment, completely beside the point. Lazarus is already dead.

Why the Word Appears Here At All

John does not use sōzō often. When he does, it is usually carrying weight: that the world through him might be saved, by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved.

Here it means recovery from illness — the most ordinary thing the word can mean. And the reason it appears in that sense is a misunderstanding, which John records without embarrassment because misunderstanding is how his Gospel teaches.

It happens repeatedly. Nicodemus hears born again and pictures a womb. The woman at the well hears living water and thinks about the walk to the well. The crowd hears destroy this temple and counts building years. Every time, the flat literal reading is voiced, and Jesus’ meaning opens out behind it.

This is that pattern applied to death.

The Delay

The sisters send word: “Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick.” It is a masterpiece of restraint — no request, just a fact, addressed to someone they trust to know what to do with it.

Jesus’ response has two parts, and both are strange.

First: “This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby.” Which is either wrong — Lazarus dies — or means something other than what it appears to mean. The end of this sickness is not death; death is a station on the way, not the terminus.

Second, and harder: “When he had heard therefore that he was sick, he abode two days still in the same place where he was.”

John puts the word therefore in the sentence. Because He loved them, He stayed. That is not a comfortable clause, and John does not soften it.

“Our Friend Lazarus Sleepeth”

After two days He announces they are going back to Judea. The disciples object on practical grounds — the Jews there had recently tried to stone Him.

Then: “Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep.”

And the disciples, relieved to find a reason not to go: Lord, if he sleep, he shall be saved.

You can hear the argument underneath it. If he is merely resting, the crisis has passed, and there is no need to walk into a town that wants you dead.

So John adds the correction: “Howbeit Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in sleep. Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead.”

Sleep

The metaphor is not a euphemism, and it is worth being careful here. Jesus is not softening death or denying its reality — three verses later He will stand at the grave and weep, and John will use a word for His reaction that carries anger in it.

Sleep says something specific: this condition is temporary, and I can end it. That is why the disciples’ misunderstanding is so useful. They heard sleep and thought he will recover naturally. Jesus meant he will be woken deliberately.

The word stayed in the church’s vocabulary for exactly this reason. Paul writes about those who sleep in Jesus. Luke says Stephen fell asleep while being stoned to death. It is not denial. It is a claim about who has authority over the condition.

What the Word Means Here, and What It Does Not

In verse 12, saved means recover from an illness. Nothing more. It is a datum for anyone who wants to know how wide the range of this verb runs — from he shall save his people from their sins to he’ll be fine by morning, inside the same New Testament, on the same lips.

What the passage as a whole is doing is something else. It is setting up the seventh and largest sign in John’s Gospel, and the claim Jesus makes to Martha a few verses later: I am the resurrection, and the life.

Lazarus does not get the salvation the disciples predicted. He does not recover from his illness. He dies, is buried, and stays four days in a tomb — long enough, in the thinking of the time, that no one could suggest he had merely appeared to die.

And then he is called out by name.

Two Kinds of Deliverance, and the Difference

Here is the thing this passage is quietly good at teaching.

The disciples wanted the small salvation, and it was a reasonable thing to want. Recovery from sickness is a genuine mercy; the Gospels are full of it, and several studies in this gateway are about exactly that.

What they got instead was a demonstration that the greater deliverance does not depend on avoiding the lesser loss.

Lazarus was raised, and Lazarus died again later — Jesus was not exempting him from mortality. The sign points past itself. The One who called him out of a tomb would shortly be laid in one, and would come out of His own without being called.

Thomas Gets the Last Word

The passage ends with a line that deserves to be better known:

Then said Thomas, which is called Didymus, unto his fellowdisciples, Let us also go, that we may die with him.

John 11:16

He has misjudged the situation as badly as anyone. He thinks they are walking to a funeral and possibly their own.

But he goes. And there is something in that worth more than accurate prediction: a man who has understood nothing about what is coming, and follows anyway.

The disciples said he shall be saved and meant a fever breaking. What John is about to show them is a good deal larger, and it starts with a dead man hearing his own name.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sickness. The disciples expect Lazarus to recover in the ordinary way
Saved into what?Nothing, as it turns out. He dies, and receives something else entirely
Who is speaking?The disciples, misunderstanding Jesus’ word for death
What does the word mean here?To get better — the most everyday sense the verb carries anywhere in the New Testament
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?No. But the misunderstanding is the door into the largest claim in the Gospel
