“Able to Save to the Uttermost”

The completeness of this salvation rests on a priest who does not die — and the reason given is not His power but His permanence

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Hebrews 7:20–28
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Hebrews 7:20–28

“Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them.”

The phrase everyone remembers is to the uttermost. The word that carries the argument is wherefore — because the sentence is a conclusion, and the reasoning that produced it is what makes the promise trustworthy.

What “To the Uttermost” Means

The Greek is eis to panteles. It can mean completely, or it can mean forever, and the two senses are not really rivals here.

Completely: nothing left unsaved, no part of the case unaddressed. Forever: no expiry, no point at which it lapses.

The writer’s argument supports both, and the second is what he has been building toward for the whole chapter.

The Argument: Priests Kept Dying

Hebrews 7 is a comparison, and the decisive difference is mortality.

And they truly were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death: but this man, because he continueth ever, hath an unchangeable priesthood.

Hebrews 7:23–24

That is the crux. The Levitical priesthood worked by succession. A priest served, died, and was replaced. Aaron to Eleazar to Phinehas, on and on for centuries — a chain of men each of whom stopped.

Every one of those deaths was an interruption. Whatever a priest had been doing for you ended when he did.

Christ’s priesthood, the writer says, is aparabatos — untransferable, unchangeable, not passed on. Not because no one is worthy to succeed Him, but because the post never becomes vacant.

So able to save to the uttermost is not a claim about the size of His power in the abstract. It is a claim about the continuity of His office. He can finish what He starts because He is still there at the end of it.

“Seeing He Ever Liveth”

The reason clause is the resurrection, stated in its priestly consequence.

This is worth putting alongside the earlier chapter of Hebrews already covered in this gateway. In 5:7 the Son prayed to the One able to save Him out of death, and was heard — not by escaping death but by being brought through it. Here that same emergence is doing continuous work: he ever liveth.

The two passages belong together. The Father saved the Son out of death; because the Son is out of death, He is able to save others completely.

Intercession Is Not Pleading With a Reluctant Father

The picture of intercession can go wrong in a particular way — as though the Son were perpetually persuading an unwilling Father to relent.

Hebrews will not allow that. It is God who swore the oath establishing this priesthood. It is God who sent the Son. The intercession is not the overcoming of divine reluctance; it is the permanent presence of the One who has made the offering, in the presence of God, on behalf of His people.

And the writer has already said the sacrifice does not repeat: “this he did once, when he offered up himself.” The intercession continues; the offering does not. What is ongoing is the priest, not the sacrifice.

Who Is Saved to the Uttermost

The promise has an address: them that come unto God by him.

Two things in that phrase. The direction is unto God — not unto safety, not unto benefits. The destination of this salvation is God’s presence, which is what the whole book is about: a torn veil, a new and living way, boldness to enter.

And the route is by him. Not alongside, not in addition to. The priesthood is the access.

The participle for come is present and continuous — those who are coming, who keep drawing near. That is not a condition to be anxious about; it is a description of what people who trust a priest do. They approach.

The Oath

The paragraph opens with something easy to skim: this priesthood was established with an oath, and the Levitical one was not.

For those priests were made without an oath; but this with an oath by him that said unto him, The Lord sware and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec.

Hebrews 7:21

God does not swear oaths often. The writer’s point is that an oath adds a solemnity — a divine self-commitment — that the arrangement it establishes will not be revised.

Hence the next clause: “By so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament.” A surety is someone who guarantees an obligation with himself. Christ does not merely mediate the covenant; He stands behind it personally.

Why This Matters for Assurance

Follow the chain and see where confidence is located.

The oath is God’s. The priesthood is permanent because the priest does not die. The intercession is continuous because He ever lives. The offering was made once and does not need remaking. And the salvation is therefore to the uttermost.

Not one link in that chain runs through the believer’s performance. Assurance in Hebrews does not rest on the steadiness of the one drawing near; it rests on the permanence of the One being drawn near to.

That is worth saying plainly, because Hebrews is also the book with the sharpest warnings in the New Testament, and readers often hold those warnings and this promise in an anxious tension. The writer evidently did not. He wrote both, in the same letter, to the same people — and the warnings are addressed to those tempted to stop coming, which is precisely the group this promise does not describe.

What the Word Means Here

The full comprehensive sense, with the accent on completeness. The peril: sin, accusation, death, exclusion from God’s presence. The result: permanent access to God.

And the distinctive contribution of this passage to the gateway is the reason attached. Most of the New Testament grounds salvation in what Christ did. Hebrews 7:25 grounds its completeness in what He continues to be.

A priest who dies leaves your case unfinished. This one does not die, and so the case does not remain unfinished. That is the whole argument, and it is why the writer can use a phrase as absolute as to the uttermost without overstating.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sin, accusation, death, and exclusion from God’s presence
Saved into what?Complete and permanent access to God, kept by a priest who never leaves office
Who saves?Jesus, the surety of a better covenant, who ever lives
Through what means?Coming to God by Him — and His unceasing intercession
Why “to the uttermost”?Because the priesthood is unchangeable. Nothing interrupts it, including death
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes, with the emphasis on its completeness and permanence
