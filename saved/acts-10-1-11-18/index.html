“Words, Whereby Thou and All Thy House Shall Be Saved”

A Gentile household saved by hearing the gospel — and the moment the early church discovered salvation was never about ancestry

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 9 min read • Acts 10:1–11:18
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Acts 10:1–11:18

This is one of the longest single narratives in Acts, and Luke tells it at length because the church nearly missed what it means. The saving word arrives in a promise made to a Gentile centurion.

The Promise Made to Cornelius

An angel tells Cornelius to send for Peter, who shall tell thee words, whereby thou and all thy house shall be saved. Luke gives us the line twice — once as Peter recounts it to the believers in Jerusalem: “Who shall tell thee words, whereby thou and all thy house shall be saved.” (Acts 11:14)

Sōthēsē — thou shalt be saved. And the means is precisely specified: words. Not the vision, not the angel, not Cornelius’s evident devotion. He was already a devout man who feared God, gave alms, and prayed continually — and none of that had yet saved him. He needed words spoken, a message delivered, the gospel of Jesus Christ. The angel could announce the promise but not preach the sermon; for that, a man had to come and speak.

Peter Has to Be Persuaded First

The drama is not really about whether Cornelius will believe. It is about whether Peter will go.

Luke spends a whole vision on it — the sheet let down from heaven, the unclean animals, the command rise, Peter; kill, and eat, and Peter’s refusal: Not so, Lord; for I have never eaten any thing that is common or unclean. Three times, and then the interpretation Peter finally reaches: “God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.”

The vision was about food; the meaning was about people. Peter had to be pried loose from the assumption that salvation ran along the lines of ancestry and ceremonial cleanness before he could carry the saving words to a man outside them.

“God Is No Respecter of Persons”

When Peter finally preaches, he opens with what the whole episode taught him: “Of a truth I perceive that God is no respecter of persons: but in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him.”

This is not a claim that every religion saves, or that sincerity is enough — Cornelius still had to hear the words and believe them. It is a claim that the door is not guarded by nationality. The gospel Peter then preaches is the same one preached at Pentecost: Jesus of Nazareth, crucified, raised, ordained judge, and whosoever believeth in him shall receive remission of sins.

The Spirit Interrupts the Sermon

Then the moment that settles the argument for the whole church: “While Peter yet spake these words, the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word.”

The Jewish believers who came with Peter were astonished that the gift of the Holy Ghost was poured out on the Gentiles too. God did not wait for circumcision, or a probationary period, or a ruling from Jerusalem. He gave the Spirit while Peter was still talking, on the strength of the word believed — and Peter’s only possible response was to ask who could forbid water for baptism to people God had so plainly received.

The Verdict in Jerusalem

Chapter 11 is the aftermath: Peter called to account for eating with Gentiles, and defending himself simply by narrating what God did. The church’s conclusion is the saving word’s widest opening yet: “Then hath God also to the Gentiles granted repentance unto life.”

Repentance unto life — granted, given, to the Gentiles. The sentence closes the episode and opens the mission. What began with one centurion’s household becomes the settled recognition that salvation was never a matter of descent.

What the Word Means Here

The full comprehensive sense: forgiveness of sins, the gift of the Holy Ghost, repentance unto life, and inclusion among the people of God — the whole of conversion, gathered in the angel’s promise that Peter’s words would save a household.

Its particular contribution to the gateway is the means and the reach. The means is the spoken gospel, which even a devout, praying, alms-giving man still needed. And the reach is universal: the same saving word that fell on Jerusalem at Pentecost falls, in this chapter, on a Roman soldier’s household, and the church discovers that the God who saves is no respecter of persons.

At a Glance

Saved from what?Sin, exclusion, and alienation from the people of God
Saved into what?Forgiveness, the Holy Ghost, repentance unto life, and inclusion among God’s people
Who saves?God, through Jesus Christ, who is no respecter of persons
Through what means?Words — the gospel heard and believed
Comprehensive salvation in Christ?Yes. Full conversion, extended for the first time openly to Gentiles
