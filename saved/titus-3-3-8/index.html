“According to His Mercy He Saved Us”

Paul gathers regeneration, renewal, justification, and heirship into one saving act — and does not arrange them into a sequence

By James Burke • July 21, 2026 • 10 min read • Titus 3:3–8
Home › What Does It Mean to Be Saved? › Titus 3:3–8

Paul has just told Titus to remind the believers to be obedient, gentle, and meek toward everyone. Then he gives the reason, and the reason is the whole gospel in one sentence.

Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost.

Titus 3:5

Esōsen — He saved us. The aorist, the completed act. And Paul crowds into the space around that verb nearly everything the New Testament says about how salvation comes.

First, Who We Were

The mercy only registers against what preceded it, so Paul begins there, and includes himself.

For we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, and hating one another.

Titus 3:3

We ourselves. Not the pagans out there; the writer and his reader. The list is not exotic wickedness — it is folly, disobedience, being led about by appetite, and the ordinary poison of resentment. Hateful, and hating one another is the summary, and it is the line that stings, because it describes a condition most people recognise from the inside.

That is the soil the mercy fell on. Paul wants it clearly in view before he says a word about what God did, because the point of verse 3 is to remove any possibility that verse 5 was deserved.

“Not By Works of Righteousness”

Then the turn: “But after that the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward man appeared.”

And immediately Paul rules something out before he says what God did. Not by works of righteousness which we have done.

Note the precision. He does not say not by our sins — that would be obvious. He says not by our works of righteousness, our best efforts, the things we might have offered as a claim. Even those are excluded as the ground. The salvation did not answer to anything we produced, righteous deeds included.

What it answered to was His mercy. Mercy is not reward for the good; it is kindness to the undeserving, which is why Paul spent verse 3 establishing that undeservingness. The whole architecture of the sentence puts the cause on God’s side and leaves nothing on ours but the need.

One Saving Act, Not a Sequence

Here is where this passage is easy to mishandle, and worth handling carefully.

Paul names a great deal in a few words: a washing, regeneration, renewing of the Holy Ghost, the Spirit poured out, justification by grace, heirship, hope of eternal life. It is tempting to lay these out as stations on a line — first this happens, then that, then the next — and to argue about their order.

Paul does not do that, and the sentence resists it. He is not narrating a sequence in which one item causes the next. He is describing one saving act viewed through several relations. The same event is a washing when you look at what it removed, regeneration when you look at the life it gave, renewal when you look at the Spirit’s work, justification when you look at your standing, heirship when you look at your future. These are not detachable steps on a conveyor belt. They are facets of a single mercy.

Reading it that way keeps the passage from becoming a battleground over the order of salvation — a battle Paul shows no interest in fighting here. His interest is that all of it is God’s doing and none of it is ours.

“The Washing of Regeneration”

This is the phrase people stop on, because it sounds like it might be about baptism, and Titus 3:5 has long been a front line in that argument.

The language does evoke baptism — washing is water language, and the early readers would have heard the echo. But the saving power Paul locates is not in the water. He says God saved us by the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Ghost, and then, so there is no mistake about the agent, he adds: which he shed on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Saviour. The active party throughout is the Spirit, poured out. The washing is real, but what does the cleansing is regeneration — the Spirit giving new life — not the rite that pictures it.

This is the same pattern found where Paul speaks of being buried with Christ in baptism and raised with Him “through the faith of the operation of God.” The imagery is baptismal; the power is God’s. Baptism signifies the washing; it does not perform it. To read Titus 3:5 as though the water regenerated is to make the sign do the work of the thing signified — and to contradict the verse’s own insistence that salvation came not by works of righteousness, which a rite dutifully performed would be.

So: the washing is the cleansing of the new birth, pictured in baptism, accomplished by the Spirit.

Regeneration and Faith

One more thing needs saying plainly, because the passage is often used to settle it in a direction the passage does not require.

Regeneration is the Spirit’s gift of life. It is not something the sinner does, and it is not moral self-improvement — it establishes a new origin in the risen Christ, not a better version of the old man. On that, Titus and John 3 agree entirely.

But Paul does not here make the new birth a stage that precedes and produces faith, with the sinner passive and then, later, believing. Faith is the sinner’s own trusting reliance on Christ; regeneration is the Spirit’s gift of life; neither performs the other’s action, and neither exists as a saving state cut off from the other. The Spirit gives life as the sinner rests on Christ. This study does not narrate a microscopic order between them, because Paul does not, and the passage that most tempts readers to insist on one is precisely the passage that refuses to supply it.

Justified, and Made Heirs

The sentence runs on to its purpose: “That being justified by his grace, we should be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life.”

Two more facets. Justified by his grace — the standing, given not earned, the same grace that did the saving now doing the acquitting. And heirs according to the hope of eternal life — the future, not as a wage but as an inheritance, which is a family word. An heir does not earn the estate; he receives it by belonging to the family. Regeneration made him a son; heirship is what sonship comes to.

Hope here is not wishing. It is the settled expectation of what belongs to an heir who has not yet entered the estate.

Then the Good Works

And now the works reappear — the ones excluded as a cause in verse 5 return as a consequence in verse 8.

This is a faithful saying, and these things I will that thou affirm constantly, that they which have believed in God might be careful to maintain good works.

Titus 3:8

This is the shape the whole New Testament keeps: works are ruled out as the ground of salvation and required as its fruit, and the two are not in tension because they answer different questions. How were you saved? By mercy, not works. What does that salvation now produce? A life careful to maintain good works. Paul saw no contradiction in putting both in the same paragraph, addressed to the same people.

And it returns the passage to where it began. Paul started this by telling Titus to remind the believers to be gentle and obedient. The gospel sentence was the reason. People who were once hateful and hating one another, saved by sheer mercy, now have both the motive and the new life to live otherwise.

What the Word Means Here

The full comprehensive sense, gathered more densely than almost anywhere else in Paul. The peril is the whole old life — folly, bondage, malice. The result is the whole new one — regeneration, renewal, a justified standing, an inheritance.

The distinctive contribution of Titus 3 to this gateway is its refusal to itemise. Other passages take up one facet at a time; here Paul holds the entire saving act in a single breath, credits all of it to mercy, and lets the good works follow rather than lead. He saved us — not because of what we had done, but according to what He is.

At a Glance

Saved from what?The old life of folly, disobedience, bondage, malice, and mutual hatred
Saved into what?Regeneration, renewal, a justified standing, and heirship in hope of eternal life
Who saves?God our Saviour, according to His mercy, through Jesus Christ
Through what means?The washing of regeneration and the renewing of the Holy Ghost — not works of righteousness
What is the named response?Faith receiving mercy, not merit presented for reward
