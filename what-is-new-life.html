What Happens When You Trust Christ

There is a moment the whole Bible moves toward and the whole gospel calls for: the moment a person stops running his own life and hands it to Jesus Christ. The New Testament reaches for picture after picture to say what happens then. It is a birth — a life that was not there before. It is a resurrection — the dead raised. It is a rescue — a drowning man pulled from the water. It is a homecoming — a runaway received back into the family. It is a wedding — two joined into one. It is a branch cut from a wild tree and grafted into a living vine.

Six pictures, and underneath them a single reality: a person is joined to Jesus Christ, and Christ's life becomes his. That joining — being in Christ — is what this whole article is about. Every image circles the same center.

Not a better record. A new life.

The wider biblical story explains why this new life is needed: we are born into Adam's world under self-rule and death, and Jesus Christ has done everything needed to save us. But the question here is personal and direct, whether you are a seeker, a new believer, or a Christian who has never seen these truths held together: what actually happens when a sinner trusts Christ?

A New Life, Not a Better Record

Most people, if they think about being right with God at all, think of it as a matter of the record. The good deeds must somehow outweigh the bad; the debt must be cleared; the slate wiped clean. And it is true that guilt is real and forgiveness is needed. But the gospel offers something far deeper than a corrected record. It offers a new life.

The reason is that our problem was never only what we have done. It is what we are. In Adam we are not merely people with bad marks against us; we are people whose very life is bent away from God — "dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1), breathing and walking about, yet cut off from the God who is the source of life. A dead man does not need his record improved. He needs to be made alive.

This is why reformation is not the answer. You can improve the old man — teach him manners, curb his worst habits, dress up his religion — and he is still the old man, only tidier. A corpse in a clean suit is still a corpse. Jesus did not tell Nicodemus to try harder. He told him, "Ye must be born again" (John 3:7). Not repaired. Not reformed. Born — given a life he did not have before. That is what happens when a sinner trusts Christ, and everything depends on seeing it rightly.

The Gospel Comes and Calls

New life does not begin with something we do. It begins with something we hear.

Before a person ever turns to Christ, the gospel comes to him — the good news that Jesus, the Son of God, lived, died for sinners, and rose again, and that He calls all who will to come to Him. Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the Word of God (Romans 10:17). No one manufactures new life out of himself; it is awakened from outside, by a message he did not invent and could not have guessed.

And the message does not come alone. As the gospel is heard, the Spirit of God works in related but distinct ways. He draws — He makes Christ appear desirable to a heart that a moment before had no desire for Him, so that the sinner finds himself pulled toward the very One he had ignored. He convicts — He presses home the truth about our sin until it can no longer be shrugged off, so that a man feels the weight of what he had always excused. And He enlightens — He gives real understanding of the gospel, so that what once sounded like a tired religious story is recognized as true and weighty. Yet enlightenment is not itself surrender or new life; a person may understand the gospel and still refuse Christ. "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44).

The sinner cannot manufacture the life he needs, and he cannot create the light by which he sees Christ. The drawing, the conviction, and the light are all God's gift, given before the sinner has done a thing. Grace is first. It is always first.

Yet this drawing is not a force that overrides the will and drags a man to Christ against himself. It can be refused. Scripture is full of people who felt its pull and turned away — some for a season before they finally came, some to the very end. To say the drawing "can be resisted," then, is to say this: it is real grace, genuinely offered, that a person may truly reject; many resist it for a time, and some resist it altogether. But no one who comes to Christ comes without it. The one who comes can never boast that he found Christ by his own wisdom; he came because God first drew near.

So the Spirit's work brings a person to the edge of a decision he cannot avoid: what will he do with Christ? That answer is the turn.

The One Turn: Repentance and Faith

When the Bible describes how a sinner comes to Christ, it uses two words that sound like two things and are really one: repentance and faith. "Repent ye, and believe the gospel" (Mark 1:15).

Repentance is a turning from — from self-rule, from the quiet sovereignty each of us tries to keep over his own life, from the sins that flow out of it. Faith is a turning to — to Christ, entrusting oneself to Him. They are not two separate steps, one finished before the other starts. They are the two sides of a single motion, like a man turning his back on one thing in the very act of turning his face to another. You cannot come to Christ without leaving the throne you were sitting on; you cannot leave that throne except by coming to Him.

It is worth being clear about what faith is, because the word is easily hollowed out. Saving faith is not merely believing that certain things are true. The devils believe, and tremble (James 2:19). It is not a feeling worked up in a quiet moment, nor a decision recited from a card. Saving faith is trust — the entrusting of yourself to a Person. It is what a drowning man does when he stops thrashing and lets the lifeguard carry him. It is giving yourself to Christ and giving up the right to run your own life.

This means that receiving Christ as Saviour is receiving Him as Lord. The two cannot be split apart, as though a person could take His rescue while declining His rule. There is only one Christ, and He is Lord; to come to Him at all is to come to Him as He is. "If thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved" (Romans 10:9). The confession and the trust are one surrender.

And this surrender is a genuine choice — as real a choice as love is. But it earns nothing. Faith is not a payment; it is the empty hand that receives what Christ has already done, and an empty hand adds nothing to the gift it takes. The drowning man does not save himself by ceasing to struggle; he is saved by the One who carries him — yet it is truly he who lets go and is carried. So faith is real, and faith is the sinner's own, and faith contributes not one thing to the price of his salvation, which was paid in full at the cross. He believes. He turns. The whole gospel calls him to it, holds him responsible for it, and welcomes him when he comes.

What God Does: The Spirit Gives Life

Here we arrive at the heart of it — and here the greatest care is needed, because it is easy to get the pieces in the wrong order and lose the gospel in the process.

When a sinner turns to Christ in that repentant faith, the Holy Spirit joins him to the risen Son and gives him life. This is the new birth. This is being "quickened" — made alive — "together with Christ" (Ephesians 2:5). The dead man lives, because he has been united to the One who is alive forevermore, and Christ's life has become his.

Notice who does what.

The sinner believes. The Spirit gives life.

These are not the same act, and they must never be confused. Faith does not create the new life — a man cannot regenerate himself by believing hard enough, as though trust were an engine that manufactures life. And the Spirit does not do the believing for us — He does not repent and trust in our place. The sinner trusts; God makes alive. The two meet in a single moment, and in that moment a person passes out of death into life.

This is why we must not say that God secretly makes a person new before he can believe, as if a hidden regeneration had to come first to switch on a faith the sinner was too dead to exercise. Scripture does not lay it out that way. It calls dead sinners to hear, to turn, to trust — and it is in that turning that the Spirit gives life. Think again of the blind man: the Spirit gives the light, but it is the man himself who opens his eyes and looks. God's grace runs through the whole of it, drawing and convicting and enabling; but the life is given as the sinner comes, not handed to him while he still stands with his back turned. He believes, and he lives. The one movement of grace and the one movement of trust are not rivals splitting the credit between them — it is not part God and part man. It is grace from first to last, and inside that grace a real person really turns.

For none of this is the sinner's achievement. The drawing was God's. The gospel was God's. The Christ he trusts is God's gift. The sinner's faith is his genuine response, awakened and called forth by God's Word and Spirit. From beginning to end salvation is God's work — and yet it comes through faith, to a person who really turns. That is why the gospel can look any sinner in the eye and say: come, and live.

What Kind of Life Is This?

When we say that Christ's life becomes the believer's life, we need to say carefully what we mean. We do not mean that a human being becomes God, or receives the self-existent life that belongs to God alone. The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit have life in themselves. They do not receive life from another. No creature can share that independence.

Nor is new life merely a better state of mind, a religious feeling, or a promise that we will live somewhere after we die. Christ gives something far more solid. The eternal Son truly took mortal human life in the incarnation. He hungered, grew weary, suffered, and died. But when He rose, His human life entered a condition that had never existed before in creation: incorruptible life over which death has no dominion.

That is the life the Spirit shares with the believer. He joins us to the risen Christ, not to an idea about Christ. We remain creatures, and we remain dependent upon God. Our mortal bodies still grow weak and die. But death can no longer sever us from the One whose human life has passed through death and cannot be held by it. The life we receive is resurrection life already begun: real life with God now, and the sure promise that our bodies also will be raised.

This means salvation does not merely take us back to Adam's first condition. Adam's life was good, created, and received from God, but it was mortal and dependent. In Christ we are brought forward into the life of the risen last Adam — a life still received from God, yet now secure beyond death's dominion. The new birth is the beginning of that life in us; bodily resurrection will be its full and visible completion.

One Life Seen from Every Side

Because this is the decisive center of salvation, the New Testament keeps circling it, naming it from every side. And the many names can make it sound like a row of separate transactions handed across a counter one after another — first this is done to you, then that, then the next thing. It is not like that at all. It is one event, and the names are windows looking in on it from different sides of the room.

Stand at the moment a sinner comes to Christ and watch what happens. From one side you see a man turning — his back to the throne he sat on, his face to the Lord he fled: that is conversion. From another side you see a corpse drawing breath, a dead man opening his eyes: that is regeneration, the new birth. From another side you see a wedding — two joined so that what is His becomes hers: that is union with Christ. Three windows, one room. Not three steps down a conveyor belt, but three ways of watching a single thing: a person joined to Christ, and in that joining turned, made alive, and made to belong.

And through those same windows you can see everything else that comes with Him. His sins are carried away, and he is forgiven. He is declared right with God — justified — not by scrubbing his record but by being clothed in Another's. He is taken into the family and given a Father — adopted. The Spirit moves in to stay. These are not later prizes to be earned, or separate purchases collected over the years. They are all one gift, because they are all Christ. The moment a sinner is joined to the Son, he has, in Him, every blessing salvation holds. Not because he climbed a ladder rung by rung, but because he was given a Person, and in that Person every treasure was already hidden.

The Life That Takes Root

If new life is real, it will show. Not because our works keep us saved, but because of a simpler thing.

Life, by its nature, lives.

Jesus told a parable about this. A sower scattered seed, and it fell on different ground. Some sprang up quickly and withered under the sun for lack of root. Some was choked by thorns — the cares and riches of this life. And some fell on good ground and brought forth fruit with patience (Luke 8:5–15).

Notice what Jesus says about the seed that withered. He does not say it never sprouted, or that its owner only pretended to plant it. He says these hearers "receive the word with joy; and these have no root, which for a while believe, and in time of temptation fall away" (Luke 8:13). For a while they believe — really, and gladly. This is not, in the first place, a picture of the hypocrite who never meant it; it is a picture of a response that was genuine as an experience and yet never sank down into rooted, lasting life in Christ. The New Testament is honest enough to name such a thing, and to warn about it, so that no one rests easy in a springtime flourish that never went down deep.

But the warning is not meant to send a trusting believer inward on himself, forever taking his own spiritual pulse to see whether the roots are deep enough. That is exactly the wrong response, and it kills assurance. New life is not held by the tightness of our grip; it is held by the One we are joined to. "I am the vine, ye are the branches," Jesus said; "he that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). The branch does not strain and sweat to squeeze out grapes. It abides — stays joined — and the life of the vine bears the fruit through it.

So the mark of real new life is not a flawless record or an unbroken emotional high. It is a life that keeps coming back to Christ — that abides, that lasts, that little by little bears His fruit. How that assurance rests and grows is the subject of another study. Here it is enough to say: the life God gives is a rooted life, and rooted life endures, because the root is not in us but in Him.

Now You Are in Christ

Step back and see the whole. A sinner, dead in Adam, hears the gospel. The Spirit draws and convicts and opens his eyes. He turns — from his own rule, to Christ — trusting, surrendering, receiving the Lord who gave Himself for him. And in that turning the Spirit joins him to the risen Son and gives him life. Conversion, regeneration, union — one saving reality, and in it every blessing of salvation becomes his, because Christ Himself has become his.

Go back now to where we began. All six pictures come true at once. It is a birth: a life is there that was not there before. It is a resurrection: the dead is raised. It is a rescue: the drowning man is lifted out and set on dry ground. It is a homecoming: the runaway is folded back into the Father's house. It is a wedding: he is joined to Christ, and all that is Christ's is now his. It is a branch grafted into the vine: cut from the wild and wasting tree of Adam, and made to live on the life of Another.

The gospel does not offer a set of benefits. It offers a Person — and everything in Him.

And this is only the beginning. To be in Christ is to have, in Him, forgiveness and righteousness and adoption and peace and an inheritance that cannot fade — every blessing of salvation, held in one union. What those blessings are, and how the rooted life grows and is kept safe to the end, are the next things to explore. But they all flow from here, from the moment a dead man was made alive by being joined to the risen Christ.

If you have never made that turn, the gospel's call is still open, and it is personal: leave the throne you were never made to sit on, and give yourself to the Christ who gave Himself for you. As many as received Him, to them gave He power to become the sons of God (John 1:12). That power is offered still.
