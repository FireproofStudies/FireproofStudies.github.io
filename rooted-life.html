How the New Life Grows, and How You Can Know It Is Yours

The Christian life is learning to live in the house you have already been given — not adding rooms, but settling into the ones already yours. Every saving blessing is already the believer's, held in Christ. Nothing needs to be earned; nothing remains to be purchased.

But a house that is given must still be lived in. And a life that is given still has to be lived out. So two questions rise in the heart of anyone who has come to Christ and begun to grasp what he has been given. The first is practical: what should this new life actually look like? The second is deeper, and often quieter, and sometimes keeps a believer awake at night: how can I be sure this life is really mine — that I am truly His, and will be to the end?

This study is about both. And the two answers turn out to be closely joined, because the same life that grows is the life that assures.

What God plants, He grows.

The Life That Shows

Start with something simple: living things grow.

When God gives a sinner new life, He does not hand him an inert possession to be filed away. He plants a living thing, and living things, by their nature, grow and bear. This is why the new life will become visible. Not all at once, not evenly, not without setbacks — but really. The life that God plants, God tends, and in time it shows.

Jesus said it with an image no one forgets: "I am the vine, ye are the branches: he that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit" (John 15:5). A branch joined to a living vine does not have to manufacture grapes by effort. The life of the vine flows into it, and fruit is the natural result. The believer joined to Christ is a branch in the true Vine. The fruit will come — not because he is straining to produce it, but because the life is really there.

So the first thing to say about the Christian life is not a command but a promise: what God has begun in you, He means to grow. The question is not whether new life produces something. It is what that something is, and how it comes, and how you can tell it is the real thing. To those we now turn.

Root and Fruit

Everything in this study depends on getting one distinction right: the difference between the root and the fruit.

The root is the new life itself — the believer's union with Christ, the life of the risen Son given and joined to him. The fruit is what that life produces: a changed character, love for God and neighbor, obedience, holiness, the slow reshaping of a person into the likeness of Christ. The New Testament cares enormously about the fruit. But it never, ever lets us confuse the two, and the whole health of the Christian life hangs on keeping them straight.

Here is the order that Scripture insists on. The fruit grows from the root. It does not create the root, and it does not buy it.

Fruit grows from the life. It never buys it.

Paul is emphatic: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast." And then, in the very next breath: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works" (Ephesians 2:8–10). Notice the sequence. We are not saved by good works; we are saved unto them. The works are real and intended and good — but they are the fruit of a life already given, not the price of a life still to be earned. To reverse that order is to lose the gospel entirely, turning the Christian life into an anxious campaign to deserve what Christ has already freely given.

But the distinction cuts the other way too. If fruit does not buy the root, neither is the root indifferent to fruit. A branch truly joined to the Vine will bear; that is what living branches do. A profession of faith that produces no fruit at all, across time, gives no evidence that the life is actually there. So we hold both: the fruit never earns the life, and the living root really does bear fruit. Grace is free, and grace is not barren.

Sanctification: The New Life Overtaking the Old

How does the fruit actually grow? The Bible's word for the process is sanctification — the ongoing work of God's Spirit by which the new life He has given increasingly overtakes the old patterns of sin.

It helps to be honest about what this is and is not. It is not sinless perfection; no believer reaches that in this life, and the New Testament never pretends otherwise. It is not self-improvement, the old man polishing himself. And it is not automatic or effortless, as though the believer were merely a spectator. Sanctification is the Spirit of God, living in the believer, gradually working Christ's life out through him — and the believer really participates, really strives, really puts sin to death and puts on the new man.

Paul holds both sides together without embarrassment. On the one side: "Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh" (Galatians 5:16). The power is the Spirit's; the fruit — "love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance" (Galatians 5:22–23) — is the fruit of the Spirit, not the achievement of the flesh. On the other side he can say, "reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God" and "let not sin therefore reign" (Romans 6:11–12) — real commands, calling for real effort. The believer's striving is genuine. But it is always response to grace, never the ground of it. He works because the life is there, not in order to obtain it.

This is why growth is usually gradual, and often two steps forward and one back. The old patterns are deep, and they do not surrender all at once. But the direction is real, and the outcome is sure, because the One doing the work does not fail. Which brings us to the deeper comfort underneath the whole struggle.

Kept by God

Here is where the anxious question — will I make it to the end? — must be answered, and answered rightly, because getting it wrong has tormented many sincere believers.

The believer does not keep himself. He is kept.

You are not held by the strength of your grip. You are held by His.

This is the consistent witness of the New Testament. Paul is "confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6). Peter says believers "are kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation" (1 Peter 1:5). Jesus says of His sheep, "I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand" (John 10:28). The perseverance of a Christian to the end is real — those who truly belong to Christ do endure — but it is the fruit of God's keeping, not the achievement of the believer's own tenacity.

This matters enormously for how a believer lives. If your standing depended on the strength of your grip on God, every weak day would be a crisis and every doubt a threat to your soul. But your safety was never in your grip on Him. It is in His grip on you. The hand that holds you is not your trembling hand closed around His; it is His almighty hand closed around you. Faith does not hold on by its own strength; it rests in the One who holds.

To say this is not to make the believer passive or careless — a truly living branch is not indifferent to the Vine. It is to locate his security where the Bible locates it: in God's faithfulness, not his own. And it is precisely this that turns the Christian's endurance from anxious striving into settled rest. He perseveres, yes — but as one carried, not as one clinging by his own fingernails over an abyss.

The Warnings Are Real

But Scripture also warns. It warns sharply, and often, about falling away — and any honest treatment of assurance has to face those warnings squarely rather than explain them away.

The warnings are not empty threats or mere hypotheticals. When Hebrews says, "Take heed, brethren, lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, in departing from the living God" (Hebrews 3:12), it means it. When Jesus tells of seed that springs up with joy and then withers in the heat, having "no root," and of those "which for a while believe, and in time of temptation fall away" (Luke 8:13), He is describing something that genuinely happens. People do make a real, glad response to the gospel and then fall away. The warnings are addressed to the visible, professing community, and they do the work God appoints them to do: they wake the drowsy, sober the presumptuous, and are one of the means by which God actually keeps His own — for the warned who heed the warning are being kept through it.

The question is what such falling away reveals. And here Scripture's own language is careful. It does not say that a rooted life was torn up and lost. It says the plant "had no root." Falling away, when it is final, discloses a faith that never became rooted life — a real response, a temporary belief, that sprang up in the shallow soil and never sank down into genuine union with Christ. John says it plainly of those who left: "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would no doubt have continued with us" (1 John 2:19).

So the two truths stand together without contradiction. God keeps all who are truly His, and none of them will finally be lost. And the warnings are real, and final apostasy is real, and it reveals that the Word never became rooted life in that person — not that rooted life was genuinely given and then destroyed. The warnings do not threaten the security of the truly rooted; they are one of the ways that security is worked out. The believer takes them seriously not because his salvation hangs by a thread, but because heeding God's warnings is part of how God holds him fast.

The warnings are real. So is the keeping. They are not enemies.

How You Can Know

Which brings us at last to the question underneath all the others: how can I know that I am truly His?

Begin by looking in the right direction. Assurance is not found by staring endlessly at yourself, taking your own spiritual pulse hour by hour to see whether your faith is strong enough or your fruit abundant enough. That road leads only to despair, because on any given day a sincere believer can find plenty of reasons to doubt himself. Assurance rests first and mainly on Christ and on the witness of His Spirit — on what He has done, not on what you have achieved. "The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God" (Romans 8:16). The primary ground of your confidence is outside you: the finished work of Christ, the promise of God, the Spirit He has given.

Look mostly at Christ. Only then at yourself.

And yet Scripture does invite a genuine, secondary confirmation. John wrote his first letter for exactly this reason: "These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Throughout that letter he offers tests of life — do you believe that Jesus is the Christ, do you love the brethren, do you walk in the light rather than making a settled practice of sin? These are not a scorecard by which you earn assurance. They are the marks that genuine life leaves, and seeing them can rightly confirm what the Spirit witnesses: that the life is truly there.

So assurance grows in two movements, and in this order. You look mainly to Christ — His cross, His promise, His keeping — and rest there. And then, secondarily, you may look for the fruit of His life in you, not as the price of your acceptance but as the confirming evidence that you have indeed been made alive. The confidence is grounded in Him and confirmed in you. That keeps the presumptuous honest — a claimed faith with no fruit at all is a claim worth questioning — and it keeps the anxious resting, because their assurance was never meant to stand on the strength of their own performance.

Held to the End

Step back and see where the whole path has led. You were dead in Adam, and you were made alive. You were joined to Christ, and in Him given every blessing. That life, once given, grows — bearing the fruit of the Spirit as He works Christ's likeness out in you. And that life is kept, not by your grip but by God's, so that the warnings sober you without terrifying you and your endurance is finally a gift as much as the new birth was.

And it is all going somewhere. The kept life is kept for something: the inheritance reserved in heaven, resurrection, the sight of God, a renewed creation. What the believer holds now is real but partial; the full possession is still ahead, awaiting the day Christ returns and raises His own and delivers the kingdom up in its completed order. Until then the Christian lives as one already secured and not yet home — kept for an inheritance he does not yet fully hold, by a God who does not fail.

Kept — because the keeping is His.

That is the rest the gospel offers, and it is a deep one. Not the rest of a man who has finished the race by his own strength, but the rest of a child carried by a Father who will not let go. Your assurance was never asked to stand on how tightly you can hold on. It stands on Him. Abide in the Vine; heed His warnings; look to Christ; and be at peace — for He which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ.

Where this life finally arrives — resurrection, the renewed creation, and salvation complete — is the subject of the study still to come. But you can walk toward it in confidence, because the One who gave the life is the One who keeps it, and He has never yet lost one who was truly His.
