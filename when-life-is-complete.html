Where Salvation Is Taking You

God keeps the life He gives, but He keeps it for something. Salvation is not a possession to be clutched in place. It is a present reality with a promised completion.

So we come to one of the sweetest questions in the Christian faith: where is all of this going? A sinner was dead in Adam and was made alive in Christ. He was given every blessing, and the life planted in him was tended and kept. To what end? What does salvation finally become when it is complete?

Scripture is not vague about the answer, and it is grander than most people imagine.

Salvation is going somewhere.

Where Is Salvation Taking Us?

Ask most people what the Christian hope is, and they will describe something like this: when you die, if you have been good enough, your soul goes to heaven — a far-off, cloudy, disembodied place, where spirits drift about forever. It is a picture drawn more from folklore than from Scripture, and it is thin, wispy, and finally unsatisfying. It is not the hope the Bible holds out.

The biblical hope is not less physical than this life but more. It is not the escape of the soul from the world but the redemption of the whole person — body and all — in a world made new. Paul does not long to be rid of his body; he longs for its redemption. The creation itself, he says, "groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now," waiting "for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:22–23). The destination of salvation is not a vague forever somewhere else. It is resurrection, in a renewed creation, under God.

God has not promised His people a shadowy existence apart from the world He made. He has promised resurrection from death and a creation made new, where His people will live before Him forever. This is the hope Scripture sets before us.

Not Escape, but Resurrection

Start with the center of the whole hope: resurrection.

The Christian's future is not merely survival — some part of him carrying on after death. It is resurrection: the dead raised bodily, as Christ was raised bodily. This is not a footnote to the gospel; Paul makes it the hinge on which everything turns. "If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain... ye are yet in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:14, 17). And because Christ is risen, He is "the firstfruits of them that slept" (1 Corinthians 15:20) — the first sheaf of a harvest that guarantees the rest. His resurrection is the promise and pattern of ours.

Remember what The Two Adams established: what Adam lost was embodied life under God. He was not made to be a disembodied spirit; he was made a living creature, body and breath together, in God's good world. The rebellion did not make physical death possible, but it made death inevitable for Adam's race. So salvation, if it is really to answer the ruin, cannot stop at rescuing souls out of bodies. It must raise the body itself. And it does. "For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality" (1 Corinthians 15:53). The resurrection body is the same person, transformed — no longer subject to weakness, decay, or death.

The hope is not out of the body, but out of death.

This is why the Christian can look at the grave with defiance rather than despair. Death is real, and it is an enemy, and the Bible never pretends otherwise. But it is a defeated enemy, and its defeat will one day be total: "The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26). What awaits the believer is not endless disembodiment but the undoing of death itself.

When a Believer Dies

But that resurrection is future, and in the meantime believers die. What happens then?

Here Scripture speaks tenderly and plainly, and its comfort is real. For the Christian, to die is to be with Christ. Paul is torn between staying to serve and departing, "having a desire to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better" (Philippians 1:23). He can say that to be "absent from the body" is to be "present with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). The believer who dies does not sleep in unconsciousness nor drift into nothing; he is, at once, with the Lord he loved. There is deep comfort in this, and it may be spoken without hesitation at any Christian's grave: they are with Christ, and that is far better.

And yet — this is important — even this is not the final hope. Being "present with the Lord" is precious, but Paul describes it as being "unclothed," and it is not what he ultimately longs for. What he longs for is to be clothed upon, to receive the resurrection body, "that mortality might be swallowed up of life" (2 Corinthians 5:4). The state between death and resurrection is a real and blessed waiting, but it is a waiting. The Christian's hope does not terminate in a disembodied soul with the Lord; it terminates in a raised and glorified person in a renewed creation. Death ushers the believer into Christ's presence, but the destination lies further on.

To die is to be with Christ. To rise is the hope beyond even that.

So the grave is not the end of the story, and it is not the goal. It is a defeated enemy through which the believer passes into Christ's presence, there to wait — in joy, not in fear — for the morning of resurrection.

When Christ Appears

That morning has a name and an hour, though the hour is hidden: the return of Christ. Everything the believer waits for arrives with Him.

Paul comforts grieving Christians not with the immortality of the soul but with the coming of the Lord: "the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout... and the dead in Christ shall rise first: then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them... and so shall we ever be with the Lord" (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17). At His appearing the dead are raised and the living are transformed — "in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump... the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:52). This is glorification: the believer conformed at last, body and soul, to the image of the risen Son, every trace of sin and decay gone forever.

It is worth being careful here, because the return of Christ opens onto vast questions — of order and timing and the shape of the ages to come — that the Bible addresses and that deserve their own patient study. This article does not take them up, and it does not need to. One thing must simply be held straight: Christ is presently seated at the Father's right hand, awaiting the day when His enemies are made His footstool. His promised public reign, and the day His people openly reign with Him, are still future. They arrive with Him. The believer does not now possess the visible kingdom; he waits for it, and it will come when the King does.

So the whole weight of Christian hope leans forward to one point: not chiefly the hour of our death, but the hour of His appearing, when the dead are raised and salvation is complete.

No Condemnation: The Believer and the Judgment

Scripture joins to that appearing a solemn word: judgment. And honesty requires that we not pass over it, for the resurrection is "the resurrection of life" for some and "the resurrection of damnation" for others (John 5:29). There is a real reckoning, and every person will stand in it.

But for those in Christ, the judgment is not a trial whose verdict is in doubt. It is the public confirmation of a verdict already given. Jesus said, "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life" (John 5:24). Paul says it as plainly as it can be said: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). The believer's standing was settled in Christ and sealed in his union with Him; the judgment does not reopen it. There is no judicial condemnation for the one in Christ. Yet his life will be laid open, and what was done in the body will be seen. Christ will also evaluate and reward the faithfulness expressed in the life He gave. This is not a second trial for salvation, but a true and meaningful accounting before the One who is both Judge and Saviour.

For the believer, the judgment is not a trial. It is a vindication.

This is why the Christian can await that day without terror. He does not stand before the bar hoping his good has outweighed his bad; that was never the ground of his acceptance. He stands in Christ, and the judgment will only make public before all creation what grace made true at the cross: that he belongs to the Lord.

A Renewed Creation

And then — the destination itself. Not a cloud, not a disembodied forever, but a new heavens and a new earth.

John sees it: "a new heaven and a new earth... And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them... And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (Revelation 21:1–4). This is where the whole story has been going. The curse pronounced east of Eden is lifted. Death is abolished. The Tree of Life, from which Adam's race was shut out, stands again in the midst of the city, its leaves "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2). And the deepest note of all is not the absence of pain but the presence of God: He dwells with His people, and they see His face.

Look how the end answers the beginning. The Two Adams opened with a vocation lost — embodied human life, under God, in a good creation, entrusted with dominion and communion, forfeited in the rebellion. Here it is all restored, and more than restored. What Adam let fall, Christ lifts up and secures forever. The redeemed are not returned to a fragile Eden that could be lost again; they are brought into a renewed creation that cannot fail, because it rests on the finished work of the last Adam who will never fall.

The story ends in a garden — and surpasses it.

This is no escape from the world. It is the world made new, and the redeemed at home in it, embodied and glorified, under the God who made them and remade them.

Life Complete

Stand back one last time and see the whole road.

A sinner was born into Adam's world, under self-rule and death. He heard the gospel; the Spirit drew him; he turned from his own rule and trusted Christ, and in that turning was joined to the risen Son and made alive. In that union he received every blessing — justified, reconciled, adopted, indwelt, made an heir. The life planted in him grew and was kept, not by his grip but by God's, sobered by real warnings and settled on real grace. And at the last he is raised, glorified, vindicated in the judgment, and brought home to a renewed creation where God dwells with His people and death is no more.

That is salvation, whole and complete. Not a corrected record but a new life; not a bundle of benefits but a Person; not an escape from the world but the redemption of the whole person in a world made new.

From death in Adam to life in a world made new — this is what God has done.

There are questions this study has deliberately left standing — about the order and the ages and the shape of things to come — and they have their own place in the study still ahead. But the hope itself is not uncertain, and it does not depend on settling every question. It rests on the risen Christ, the firstfruits, who has already passed through death and lives beyond its reach, and who has never yet lost one who was truly His.

If you belong to Him, this is where you are going. And if you have not yet come to Him, the door still stands open. As many as received Him, to them gave He power to become the sons of God (John 1:12) — and this, all of this, is their inheritance. Come to Christ, and be carried all the way home.
