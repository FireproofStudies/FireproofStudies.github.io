What Belongs to You Because You Belong to Him

When a sinner is joined to Christ, he is not given a single benefit with more perhaps to follow. He is given everything at once. Paul begins his letter to the Ephesians almost breathless with it: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who hath blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places in Christ" (Ephesians 1:3). All of them. Already. And every one of them in the same place — in Christ.

That last phrase is the key to the whole subject, and it is easy to read past. Paul does not say we are blessed with all spiritual blessings and then, separately, that we are in Christ. He says the blessings are in Him. They are not stored in a warehouse from which God ships them to us one parcel at a time. They are held in a Person, and they become ours the moment He does.

To have Christ is to have everything in Him.

Whether you arrived here from What Is New Life? or began with this question, the truth is the same: to be in Christ is to have, in Him, every blessing of salvation. This article walks through the door and shows you the house. We will move through its great rooms — a new standing, a restored relationship, a new family, a new belonging, the Spirit's own presence, and a secure inheritance. These rooms are not steps a believer enters one after another; they are realities given together in the one union God establishes with His Son. But before we walk from room to room, one thing has to be settled, or the whole tour goes wrong.

One Gift, Not Many

The blessings of salvation are not a shopping list.

It is natural to picture them that way — justification here, adoption there, peace and forgiveness and the rest lined up like items to be collected, some received now and some earned later. But that picture quietly falsifies the gospel. It turns salvation into an inventory and God into a dispenser, and it leaves the believer forever wondering whether he has acquired enough of the items yet.

Scripture will not let us think like that. The blessings are not independent gifts handed out one by one. They are the many dimensions of one gift — of sharing the life of the risen Son. Justification, adoption, reconciliation, the indwelling Spirit, the inheritance: these are not so many parcels but so many facets of a single jewel, and the jewel is Christ Himself. You do not receive them apart from Him or in addition to Him. You receive them in Him, because you have received Him.

Not a longer list. A larger Christ.

This is why the New Testament can pile the blessings up in a single breath, as Paul does in Ephesians 1 — chosen, adopted, accepted, redeemed, forgiven, sealed, all in a few clauses — without pausing to arrange them in order. He is not counting out coins. He is turning one diamond in the light and naming what he sees on each face. So as we walk through the rooms that follow, keep hold of the thread: every blessing has one address, and the address is in Christ.

A New Standing: Justification Brings Us into Line

Begin with the problem beneath the sinner's fear of judgment: he is out of line with the holy God who made him. His guilt is real, but so is his dislocation. He has lived under self-rule, apart from God's righteous order, and cannot set himself right.

The answer is a cluster of blessings that belong together. He is redeemed — released from the old dominion through the costly blood of Christ and claimed as Christ's own. The cross does not appraise a prior worth equal to its price; it shows what Christ chose to make His people by making them His treasured possession. He is forgiven — his sins not merely overlooked but carried away, "having forgiven you all trespasses" (Colossians 2:13). And at the center of this room stands justification: God's righteous act of bringing the sinner into right standing by placing him in the standing of Christ Himself. "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1).

To be justified is to be brought into line. God does not pretend that the sinner was always right, nor does He wait until the sinner has made himself right. He brings the believer onto the level ground of Christ's righteousness — into Christ's accepted standing before the Father. The believer's position does not rise and fall with his performance, because it was never built on his performance. It is established in the Righteous One.

The courtroom language is true. God really declares the believing sinner righteous, and Christ's righteousness is truly credited to him. But the declaration is not a legal fiction floating free of reality, nor is the courtroom the whole picture. God can truthfully declare the believer righteous because He has truly placed him in Christ's righteous standing. Justification is therefore legal, but it is also relational, covenantal, participatory, and architectural: the sinner who stood outside is brought into the right place before God.

The verdict is true because God has brought you into the standing of Christ.

This is not a first room in a sequence but one permanent dimension of life in Christ. The debt is paid, the record is cleared, and the believer is brought into right standing before the Father. From there the room opens immediately onto something warmer than a courtroom.

A Restored Relationship: Reconciliation, Peace, and Access

A verdict of acquittal leaves a man free. It does not, by itself, make him a friend. The gospel does more than clear the sinner; it ends the war between him and God and brings him near.

This is reconciliation. "When we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (Romans 5:10). Atonement is Christ's deeper uniting work; reconciliation is the restored peace and relationship that work establishes. The one who was estranged, hiding, and at odds with his Maker is brought near. He is not merely tolerated under a truce but restored to the fellowship for which he was made.

Out of that reconciliation come two related forms of peace that Scripture is careful to name, and which we should be careful not to confuse. Peace with God is objective: hostility has ended, the sinner has been brought into right relationship with his Creator, and the war is over because Christ has made peace. Whether or not the believer feels it on a given morning, that peace holds, because it was made outside him, by Another.

The peace from God is the settled rest God gives His children as they live within the right relationship and purpose He restores through Christ. It is more than a passing calm. It is the quietness of a life being returned to its proper end — no longer self-directed and dislocated, but held in the Father's peace. It may strengthen or weaken in felt experience with faith and circumstance, but it grows out of the peace with God already made.

Peace with God restores the relationship. Peace from God settles the life within it.

And with reconciliation comes access. The believer has "access by one Spirit unto the Father" (Ephesians 2:18) — the door into God's presence, once barred, now standing open. He may come. Not as a defendant edging back into the courtroom, but as one welcomed in. Which is exactly the next thing the gospel says about him, and the most astonishing.

A New Family: Adoption and Acceptance

Here the blessings pass beyond anything the language of the courtroom can hold. For the God who is the sinner's Judge is also, in Christ, his Father.

This is adoption. "God sent forth his Son... to redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons. And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father" (Galatians 4:4–6). The believer is not merely pardoned and sent on his way; he is taken into the family, given a name and a place and a Father. Pardon says, you may go free. Adoption says, you may come home.

New birth and adoption are not rival descriptions of salvation, nor are they blessings given on different schedules. New birth names the life and new origin the Spirit gives in Christ. Adoption names the recognized family standing, inheritance, and full rights the Father grants to those in His Son. The person who receives Christ's life is also received into the Father's household.

It matters to see that adoption is genuinely more than justification, not simply another word for it. A judge may acquit a man he will never see again. A father takes him in. Justification settles the sinner's standing before the law; adoption settles his place in the household. Both are true together, because the same God who brings the sinner into right standing opens His arms as Father. To flatten adoption into justification is to stop at the courtroom door and miss the Father waiting in it.

And he is accepted — "accepted in the beloved" (Ephesians 1:6). Not accepted on probation, not welcomed on the condition that he keep his footing, but accepted in Christ, the Beloved Son, and therefore as securely as the Son is loved. His acceptance does not fluctuate with his obedience, because it was never grounded in his obedience. It is grounded in the Beloved, in whom he now stands.

The Judge who acquits you is the Father who takes you home.

A New Belonging: Citizenship and the People of God

The gospel never saves a person into isolation. To be brought into the family is to be brought among a people.

Paul reminds the Ephesians what they were — "strangers and foreigners," aliens with no share in the promises — and what they have become: "fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). The believer is granted a citizenship: his commonwealth is in heaven, his belonging no longer defined by the world he came from but by the kingdom to which he now belongs. He is no longer an outsider looking in. He has a country and a people.

This new citizenship does not erase a believer's earthly citizenship, family, history, or ordinary responsibilities. It gives all of them their proper order. The Christian now belongs first to Christ, and that allegiance reshapes his conduct, loyalties, hopes, and willingness to suffer when the world demands what belongs to God. The local church becomes a visible embassy of that citizenship: a gathered people who already belong to the King and bear witness to the kingdom whose public fullness is still to come.

This is not a decorative addition to personal salvation. It is part of what salvation is. The blessings we have been naming are not delivered to solitary individuals who happen to receive the same package; they are the common inheritance of one family, one citizenry, one household. To be in Christ is to be joined to all who are in Christ. The reconciled are reconciled to one another as well as to God, "for through him we both have access by one Spirit unto the Father" (Ephesians 2:18). Shared life in Christ does not erase Israel's future or the redeemed diversity of the nations; it creates one reconciled people with equal access to the Father. A private Christianity, hoarding its blessings alone, has misunderstood the gift.

The Spirit's Presence: Indwelling, Sealing, and the Earnest

We have spoken of standing, relationship, family, and belonging. But God does not give these blessings at a distance. He gives His own Presence.

God does not save the believer and then leave him to manage on his own. He gives him the Holy Spirit, to dwell within him. "Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16). This indwelling is the nearness of God made personal and permanent: the same Spirit who joined the sinner to Christ now makes the believer His dwelling place. The believer's life in Christ is therefore never a life lived alone.

This personal indwelling must not be confused with the baptism of the Holy Spirit. Christ's outpouring of the Spirit publicly constituted His New Covenant church; indwelling is the Spirit's personal presence in those whom He joins to Christ. Both belong to God's saving purpose, but they are not the same blessing or the same question.

Scripture names two things this indwelling Spirit does that reach toward the future. He is a seal: "in whom also after that ye believed, ye were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise" (Ephesians 1:13). A seal marks ownership and secures what it stamps — the believer is marked as God's own and kept for the day of redemption. And He is the earnest: "which is the earnest of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:14). An earnest is a down payment, the first installment that guarantees the rest. The Spirit given now is God's own pledge that everything promised will certainly come. These are not later spiritual prizes for an advanced class of Christians; they belong to those who are in Christ.

The Spirit within you is God's down payment on everything He has promised.

How the seal and the earnest steady the believer's assurance — how a Christian may know that he is truly kept — is the subject of a later study, and it deserves its own careful treatment. Here it is enough to see the blessing itself: the God who justifies, reconciles, and adopts also comes to live within, and His presence is the guarantee of all that is still to come.

A Secure Inheritance

Which brings us to that "all that is still to come." The blessings of the present open onto a future, and the future is already secured.

The believer is an heir. "If children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17). What is laid up for him is nothing less than a share in Christ's own inheritance — resurrection life, the vision of God, a place in the renewed creation, and the future privilege of reigning with Christ under His perfect rule. It is an inheritance "incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven" (1 Peter 1:4). It is kept for him, and — by the sealing Spirit — he is kept for it.

Two things must be held together here without confusion. The inheritance is guaranteed now: the down payment has been made, the heir named, the reservation secured. Yet it is not yet possessed in full. The believer does not now hold all that is his; he waits for it. Christ is presently seated at the Father's right hand, awaiting the final subjection of His enemies. His promised public reign is still ahead, and those united to Him will reign with Him when it comes. So the Christian lives as a genuine heir whose inheritance is certain and whose full possession is future — already secured in the risen Son, not yet enjoyed in full.

This is not anxious hoping; it is settled waiting. The guarantee is God's own Spirit, and the inheritance is held in the One who has already passed through death and lives beyond its reach. The redeemed will not receive an independent throne or autonomous life. They will share the Son's life, creation, and reign as dependent and beloved creatures forever.

Kept for the inheritance, and the inheritance kept for you.

Every Blessing, One Christ

Step back out to the front of the house now and look at the whole of it. A new standing before the law. A restored relationship with God. A place in His family. A belonging among His people. His own Spirit dwelling within. An inheritance that cannot fade. Room opening onto room — and yet, seen from the outside, plainly one house, and not a street of separate buildings.

For these are not six gifts, or twenty. They are one gift, seen from six sides. Every one of them is a dimension of the single reality named in the phrase we started with: in Christ. Justified, because he is in Christ. Reconciled, adopted, accepted, made a citizen, indwelt, made an heir — all of it because he is in Christ, and Christ is his. And the house has one foundation, which is Christ Himself: "for other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 3:11). Every room rests on Him. Try to pry any blessing loose from the Person who holds it and it comes apart in your hands, because it never existed apart from Him. There is no justification outside the Righteous One, no adoption outside the Son, no inheritance outside the Heir.

Every blessing has one address: in Christ.

This is why the Christian life is not, at bottom, the management of a portfolio of benefits. It is communion with a Person who is Himself all the benefits. To grow as a Christian is not to acquire more items from the list; it is to know more deeply the Christ in whom the whole list is already yours. The poorest believer, newly come to Christ this morning, already possesses every spiritual blessing, because he already possesses Christ. And the most mature believer, after fifty years, is still drawing on the same inexhaustible Person.

What remains is to see how this life, so richly endowed, actually grows — how Christ's life becomes visible in those who belong to Him, and how they may be sure they are His to the end. In a sense, the rest of the Christian life is simply learning to live in the house you have already been given: not adding rooms, but settling into the ones already yours. That is where the next study goes. But it goes from here, from a settled and astonished grasp of what is already true: you belong to Christ, and in Him, everything is yours.

If you have not yet come to Him, understand what is being offered. Not a set of benefits held out at arm's length, but the Son of God Himself, and in Him every blessing named here. As many as received Him, to them gave He power to become the sons of God (John 1:12). The whole inheritance waits in one Person. Come to Him, and it is all yours.
